122: 1. The nonlinear term, and the number that decides
124: Module 2 derived the Navier–Stokes equation and then spent the rest of the book throwing its viscous term away. The licence to do that came from one number, and Module 2 §9 printed it for four gases. This module asks what the equation does when the viscous term is small but not zero — which is the ordinary condition of almost every fluid in the sky, and which is not the same thing as the viscous term being absent.
126: The distinction is the whole subject. Set $\nu = 0$ exactly and the Euler equations conserve energy; there is nowhere for energy to go. Set $\nu$ small and the flow develops structure on smaller and smaller scales until the scales are small enough for viscosity to act, and energy is destroyed at a rate that does not go to zero as $\nu$ does. That is the anomaly at the centre of turbulence, and everything in this module is a consequence of taking it seriously.
128: Definition 1 (Reynolds number, and the turbulent regime). For a flow of characteristic speed $U$ over a characteristic scale $L$ in a fluid of kinematic viscosity $\nu = \mu/\rho$,
$$ \mathrm{Re} \;\equiv\; \frac{UL}{\nu}. \tag{1.1}$$
It is the ratio of the inertial term $(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u} \sim U^2/L$ of Module 2 's equation (7.5) to the viscous term $\nu\nabla^2\mathbf{u} \sim \nu U/L^2$. The flow is called turbulent when $\mathrm{Re}$ is large enough for the nonlinearity to dominate at every scale between $L$ and the scale at which viscosity finally acts.
132: "Large enough" is not a number this module derives. Pipe flow goes turbulent near $\mathrm{Re}\approx 2300$ and a flat-plate boundary layer near $\mathrm{Re}\approx 5\times10^{5}$: two thresholds differing by more than two orders of magnitude for the same fluid, because the threshold depends on the geometry and on how the flow is disturbed, not on $\nu$ alone. What matters below is not the threshold but the enormous margin by which astrophysical flows clear it. Both numbers are quoted here as inputs and are checked nowhere in this module.
134: Proposition 1 ( Module 2 's identity, re-derived). If the viscosity is the kinetic-theory value $\mu \simeq \tfrac13\rho\,\bar v\,\lambda$ of Module 1 , with $\lambda$ the mean free path and $\bar v$ a thermal speed, then
$$ \mathrm{Re} \;=\; \frac{\rho UL}{\tfrac13\rho\,\bar v\,\lambda} \;=\; 3\,\frac{U}{\bar v}\,\frac{L}{\lambda} \;=\; \frac{3\,\mathrm{Ma_{th}}}{\mathrm{Kn}}, \qquad \mathrm{Ma_{th}}\equiv\frac{U}{\bar v},\quad \mathrm{Kn}\equiv\frac{\lambda}{L}. \tag{1.2}$$
The identity holds for any thermal speed, provided the same one is used in the viscosity and in $\mathrm{Ma_{th}}$: $\bar v$ appears once in each, to the same power and on opposite sides of the ratio, so the two occurrences cancel and the $\mathrm{Re}$ they produce is unchanged. What is not unchanged is $\mathrm{Ma_{th}}$ itself , which is why a module must say which speed it means.
138: Substitute $\mu = \tfrac13\rho\bar v\lambda$ into (1.1) written as $\mathrm{Re}=\rho UL/\mu$. The density cancels, leaving $3(U/\bar v)(L/\lambda)$, which is the right-hand side. The clause about the speed is the part worth care. Nothing above fixes what $\bar v$ means; it enters the viscosity and the Mach number to the same power, so replacing it everywhere by any other thermal speed leaves (1.2) true and leaves $\mathrm{Re}$ where it was, while moving $\mathrm{Ma_{th}}$. It is therefore not enough to say which identity is being used; a module must say which speed. §6.4 prints every speed this module uses and how many components each one averages over. ∎
142: This module's choice, stated once. Throughout, $\bar v \equiv \sqrt{8k_{\rm B}T/\pi m}$, the mean thermal speed — the speed that enters the kinetic-theory viscosity $\nu = \lambda\bar v/3$. Module 2 made the other consistent choice, $v_{\rm th}=\sqrt{2k_{\rm B}T/m}$, which is the speed Module 1 uses for a gyroradius. The two differ by $\sqrt{\pi/4} = 0.8862$, so a Reynolds number computed here is smaller than the same gas's Reynolds number in Module 2 by $\sqrt{4/\pi} = 1.1284$ — and the reconciliation is printed in §6 rather than left for a reader to notice.
144: Equation (1.2) is the reason this module can be written at all. It converts a Reynolds number, which needs a viscosity, into a Knudsen number, which needs only a mean free path — and Module 1 tabulated mean free paths for every gas in this book. Two examples, both computed from Module 1 's own inputs:

154: A Reynolds number of $10^{9}$ in the layer of the Sun one can photograph is the fact this module exists to explain. It is not a number any laboratory reaches, and it is at the small end of the astrophysical range. What the rest of this section, and §3 , will show is that because it is so large, almost nothing about the flow can be computed — and that a surprising amount about it can nevertheless be predicted, with no calculation at all, from dimensions alone.
156: 2. Kolmogorov 1941, by dimensional analysis alone
158: The argument in this section is due to A. N. Kolmogorov, in a three-page note of 1941, and it is the most economical piece of physics in this book. It solves no equation. It makes one assumption about what a turbulent flow forgets, and then counts dimensions.
160: Definition 2 (the dissipation rate and the inertial range). Let $\varepsilon$ be the energy dissipated per unit mass per unit time, with dimensions $\mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-3}}$. In a statistically steady flow stirred at the scale $L$, $\varepsilon$ is also the rate at which energy is supplied , since none accumulates. The inertial range is the band of scales
$$ \eta \;\ll\; \ell \;\ll\; L \tag{2.1}$$
on which neither the stirring scale $L$ nor the viscosity $\nu$ appears in the statistics, so that $\varepsilon$ and the scale $\ell$ — equivalently the wavenumber $k = 1/\ell$ — are the only quantities available. Here $\eta$ is the scale at which viscosity acts, constructed in §3 .
164: That is an assumption, and it is the only one. Equation (2.1) asserts that a scale in the middle of the range has forgotten both how the energy was put in and how it will be taken out, and remembers only how fast it is passing through. Nothing proves it. Everything in §5 and §7 is a test of it, and §11 names the respect in which it is known to be false.
166: Proposition 2 (the K41 spectrum). Let $E(k)$ be the energy spectrum per unit mass, defined so that $\int_0^\infty E(k)\,\mathrm{d}k$ is the kinetic energy per unit mass and $E$ therefore has dimensions $\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-2}}$. If in the inertial range $E$ depends only on $\varepsilon$ and $k$, then
$$ E(k) \;=\; C\,\varepsilon^{2/3}k^{-5/3} \tag{2.2}$$
with $C$ a dimensionless constant, and the exponents are unique . The corresponding velocity increment across a separation $\ell$ is
$$ \delta v(\ell) \;=\; (\varepsilon\ell)^{1/3}. \tag{2.3}$$
172: Write the most general such dependence, $E = C\,\varepsilon^{a}k^{b}$, and match dimensions. With $[\varepsilon] = \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-3}}$ and $[k] = \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$,
$$ \mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-2}} \;=\; (\mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-3}})^{a}\,(\mathrm{cm^{-1}})^{b}. $$
The two units give two equations in two unknowns:
$$ \text{seconds:}\quad -2 = -3a \;\Rightarrow\; a = \tfrac23; \qquad \text{centimetres:}\quad 3 = 2a - b \;\Rightarrow\; b = 2a-3 = -\tfrac53. \tag{2.4}$$
The system is $2\times2$ with a non-singular matrix, so the solution is unique: no other pair of exponents is dimensionally possible. That is the force of the result — nothing was fitted , and the exponent $-5/3$ is not a best-fit slope but the only slope two units permit.

For (2.3), the same two quantities give exactly one speed at the scale $\ell$: $[\varepsilon\ell] = \mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-3}}$, whose cube root is a speed. Consistency with (2.2) is a check rather than a second assumption — the energy in a band of wavenumbers of width $\sim k$ around $k$ is $kE(k) \sim C(\varepsilon/k)^{2/3}$, and its square root is $(\varepsilon\ell)^{1/3}$ up to the constant. ∎
182: The units line in Definition 2 fixes the exponent, and changing it changes the subject. If $E(k)$ had been defined as an energy rather than an energy per unit wavenumber — that is, if the $\mathrm{cm}$ count in $\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-2}}$ were $2$ rather than $3$ — the second equation of (2.4) would give $b = -2/3$ and the whole subject would have a different exponent. The $5/3$ is a statement about a spectral density .
184: What dimensional analysis cannot supply is $C$. It is a pure number, the same for every flow if the assumption of Definition 2 is right, and different from flow to flow if it is wrong. §5 measures it in the laboratory, before this module builds anything astrophysical on it. Fig. 1 draws what (2.2) asserts, and draws beside it the exponent $-2$ that §9 will derive for a field of shocks, so that the two can be compared by eye.
232: The inertial range is a band, bounded at both ends. Energy enters at the stirring scale $L$ on the left and leaves at the dissipation scale $\eta$ on the right; between them (2.2) applies and the slope is $-5/3$. The dashed line is the $k^{-2}$ of a field of shocks, Proposition 8, anchored at the same point on the left so that only the slopes are being compared: after one decade the two differ by a factor $10^{1/3} = 2.15$, and across the four decades drawn, by $21.5$. The vertical normalisation is schematic and the band width is a choice: no measured spectrum is plotted anywhere in this module, and four decades of inertial range is what $\mathrm{Re} = (10^{4})^{4/3} = 2.15\times10^{5}$ would give by Proposition 4. The curvature at the right-hand end is an exponential cut-off drawn to show that the range ends; its shape is not predicted by anything in §2 .
235: 3. The dissipation scale, and what a simulation would cost
237: The inertial range of Definition 2 has a lower end, and the same counting that fixed the spectrum fixes where it is. This section builds that scale, and then uses it to price a direct numerical simulation — which is the calculation that decides, once and for all, that astrophysical turbulence is not going to be computed from first principles.
239: Proposition 3 (the Kolmogorov microscales). At the small-scale end the available quantities are $\nu$ and $\varepsilon$, and they form exactly one length, one speed and one time:
$$ \eta \;=\; \left(\frac{\nu^3}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/4},\qquad u_\eta \;=\; (\nu\varepsilon)^{1/4},\qquad \tau_\eta \;=\; \left(\frac{\nu}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/2}. \tag{3.1}$$
These are not three statements but one, because
$$ \frac{u_\eta\,\eta}{\nu} \;=\; 1 \tag{3.2}$$
identically: the Reynolds number of the smallest eddy is exactly unity, which is what it means for viscosity to act there.
245: With $[\nu] = \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $[\varepsilon] = \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-3}}$, seek $\nu^{a}\varepsilon^{b}$ with the dimensions of a length: $2a+2b = 1$ and $-a-3b = 0$, so $b = -a/3$ and $a = 3/4$, giving $\eta = (\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}$. The same two equations with the dimensions of a speed give $a = b = 1/4$, and with those of a time, $a = -b = 1/2$. Each system is $2\times2$ and non-singular, so each answer is the only one.

For (3.2), substitute: $u_\eta\eta/\nu = (\nu\varepsilon)^{1/4}(\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}/\nu = (\nu^4)^{1/4}/\nu = 1$. The prep script evaluates this numerically and prints $1.000000$, which is the intended reading: it is an algebraic identity, and a numerical value differing from one would mean a typing error and nothing else. ∎

255: Precision, stated once here and inherited everywhere below. The $0.15\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ is a two-significant-figure value for which no primary source was obtained : the standard reference, Kadoya, Matsunaga & Nagashima (1985), is listed in Sources and was not served by any archive tried. Every quantity in (3.3) therefore carries two significant figures whatever width it is printed at, and so does every number derived from it, including Problem C1. The extra digits are carried only so that the identity (3.2) closes to $1.000000$. This statement is not repeated at each later use.
257: Proposition 4 (the number of degrees of freedom, and the cost of resolving them). With $U$ the outer-scale speed and $\varepsilon \sim U^3/L$,
$$ \frac{L}{\eta} \;=\; \mathrm{Re}^{3/4}, \qquad N \;=\; \left(\frac{L}{\eta}\right)^{3} \;=\; \mathrm{Re}^{9/4}. \tag{3.4}$$
A time-explicit code is limited by the Courant condition to a step $\delta t \sim \eta/U$ — the largest speed on the smallest cell — so one outer turnover $L/U$ costs $L/\eta = \mathrm{Re}^{3/4}$ steps and
$$ \mathrm{Re}^{9/4}\times\mathrm{Re}^{3/4} \;=\; \mathrm{Re}^{3} \tag{3.5}$$
point-updates in total.
263: Take $\varepsilon = U^3/L$, which is (2.3) at $\ell = L$ rearranged, and substitute into $\eta = (\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}$:
$$ \frac{L}{\eta} \;=\; L\left(\frac{U^3}{L\nu^3}\right)^{1/4} \;=\; \left(\frac{UL}{\nu}\right)^{3/4} \;=\; \mathrm{Re}^{3/4}. $$
A cube of side $L$ resolved to $\eta$ needs $(L/\eta)^3$ cells, which is (3.4). For the step count, $(L/U)/(\eta/U) = L/\eta$, and multiplying by the cell count gives $\mathrm{Re}^{9/4+3/4} = \mathrm{Re}^{3}$. ∎
269: A softer count exists and it is wrong for this purpose, by a factor that can be named. Dividing the turnover time $L/U$ by the Kolmogorov time $\tau_\eta$ rather than by $\eta/U$ gives $\mathrm{Re}^{1/2}$ steps and $\mathrm{Re}^{11/4}$ updates. That is the number of turnovers of the smallest eddy, not the number of steps a code takes, and it understates the cost by exactly
$$ \frac{U}{u_\eta} \;=\; \mathrm{Re}^{1/4}. \tag{3.6}$$
Both columns are printed below so the difference is visible rather than argued about. $\mathrm{Re}^{3}$ is the standard result; Pope, Turbulent Flows , their Sect. 9.1.2 states it — a section number this book has not confirmed against a copy, as Sources records.

297: 4. The one exact result, and its minus sign
299: Everything in §2 and §3 is dimensional analysis: correct up to a constant that dimensions cannot fix. There is exactly one result in the theory of turbulence that is exact, that follows from the Navier–Stokes equation rather than from counting units, and that has no adjustable constant at all.
301: Proposition 5 (Kolmogorov's four-fifths law). For homogeneous, isotropic, statistically stationary turbulence at high Reynolds number, the third-order longitudinal structure function
$$ S_3(\ell) \;\equiv\; \big\langle\,\delta u_\parallel(\ell)^3\,\big\rangle, \qquad \delta u_\parallel(\ell) \;\equiv\; \big[\,\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\ell}) - \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\,\big]\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}, $$
satisfies, for $\eta \ll \ell \ll L$,
$$ S_3(\ell) \;=\; -\tfrac45\,\varepsilon\,\ell. \tag{4.1}$$ Stated, not derived here. The derivation is von Kármán and Howarth's, from the two-point correlation equation; it is a page of tensor algebra that would displace two of this module's four checks, and it is quoted in the same way Module 9 quotes the Eddington luminosity. Its four hypotheses — homogeneous, isotropic, stationary, $\mathrm{Re}\to\infty$ — are named in this sentence because each of them fails somewhere in this module.
307: Not given; see the statement. What can be checked here without the derivation is consistency with (2.3): $S_3 \propto \varepsilon\ell$ has the dimensions of a speed cubed, and $(\varepsilon\ell)^{1/3}$ is that speed, so the exact law and the dimensional estimate agree on the scaling and differ only by the coefficient $4/5$. The ratio of the two is $(4/5)^{1/3} = 0.9283$, which is the number Problem C3 asks a reader to recognise. ∎
311: The minus sign is the physics. A symmetric distribution of velocity increments has zero third moment, so (4.1) says the distribution is skewed: at any separation in the inertial range, large negative longitudinal increments are more common than large positive ones. Fluid elements approach each other along the line joining them more violently than they separate. That asymmetry is the direction of the cascade written as a number — energy flows from large scales to small, and the sign of $S_3$ is what says so. Reverse it and the energy would flow the other way, which is what happens in two-dimensional turbulence and is one of the reasons that subject is different.
313: The second-order structure function $S_2(\ell) = \langle\delta u_\parallel^2\rangle$ has no such exact law; it is $C_2(\varepsilon\ell)^{2/3}$, with $C_2$ related to the spectral constant of (2.2) by an isotropy factor. §5 quotes both.
315: 5. The terrestrial check: is there one Kolmogorov constant?
317: Proposition 2 predicts an exponent and leaves a number free. The exponent is what most accounts of K41 quote; the number is where the assumption of Definition 2 is actually tested, and it is tested in a laboratory. This section comes before any astrophysical number in this module, and the ordering is deliberate : §9 and Problem K2 use $C$ to evaluate a spectrum in a molecular cloud, and a constant used in the sky should be pinned on the ground first. Modules 3 and 4 put their terrestrial measurement early for the same reason.
319: The convention matters, because three different constants are called "the Kolmogorov constant" in the literature and they differ by factors of two and more. Sreenivasan (1995) defines his through the one-dimensional longitudinal spectrum $E_{11}(k_1)$, the thing a single hot wire in a single mean flow actually measures:
323: The three-dimensional constant $C$ of (2.2) is related to it by an isotropy factor, and the second-order structure function by another:
327: Both factors are Sreenivasan's, from his Section I, and both assume local isotropy — the same assumption Definition 2 makes. They are quoted, not derived.
329: A second Reynolds number, and it is not Definition 1's. Experimenters report the Taylor-microscale Reynolds number $R_\lambda \equiv u'\lambda_T/\nu$, where $u'$ is the root-mean-square velocity fluctuation of one component and $\lambda_T$ is the Taylor microscale $u'/\langle(\partial u/\partial x)^2\rangle^{1/2}$. They report it because $\lambda_T$ can be measured from a single probe where $L$ often cannot. For homogeneous turbulence $R_\lambda \propto \mathrm{Re}^{1/2}$, so a range in $R_\lambda$ is half as many decades as the same range in $\mathrm{Re}$ . This relation is standard and is quoted here, not derived. $R_\lambda$ is new to this book, is not numbered as a definition because nothing later depends on it, and appears only in this section.
331: Sreenivasan compiled more than a hundred measurements of $C_K$ — grid turbulence, jets, wakes, boundary layers, a tidal channel — and found:
333: Check 2 — CONFIRMED, and note exactly what is confirmed. $C_K = 0.53 \pm 0.055$ across more than $100$ flows, a fractional scatter of $10.4$ per cent , over $R_\lambda$ from $50$ to about $10^{4}$ — which is $\mathbf{2.30}$ decades in $R_\lambda$ and, since $\mathrm{Re}\sim R_\lambda^{2}$, $\mathbf{4.60}$ decades in $\mathrm{Re}$. K41 does not predict the value $0.53$. It predicts that there is a value: that one number describes flows of entirely different geometry across four and a half decades of Reynolds number. A ten per cent scatter is that prediction, met.
337: In the three-dimensional form (5.2) the compiled value is $C = 1.619 \pm 0.168$, and the second-order structure constant is $C_2 = 2.131 \pm 0.221$. Sreenivasan then revises his own number: allowing that the local-isotropy estimate of $\varepsilon$ undercounts by about ten per cent moves $C_K$ to about $0.50$ and the three-dimensional constant to $1.53$. This module therefore quotes the range $1.53$–$1.62$ wherever a three-dimensional constant is needed , including in Problem K2, rather than a single digit that would hide a correction the author himself applied.
339: One entry of the compilation deserves separate treatment, because it is the measurement the subject is usually said to rest on. Grant, Stewart & Moilliet (1962) measured spectra in a tidal channel at $R_\lambda$ of $3000$–$18000$ and obtained $C_K = 0.47\pm0.02$, three-dimensional form $C = 1.436\pm0.061$: a departure of $-1.09$ population standard deviations from the compiled mean, which is to say, an ordinary member of the population.
341: That number is quoted at second hand and the module says so. The 1962 paper is paywalled and was not read . The value above is read from Sreenivasan's Table IV, whose own footnote records that the tabulated constants are averages over seventeen data sets read off log–log plots with a straight edge, and that Kraichnan judged a different reading of the same plots would raise the value by more than ten per cent. The $R_\lambda$ range is Sreenivasan's estimate, not Grant et al.'s measurement . Nothing in this module depends on the entry; it is included because a reader who knows the subject will look for it.
343: And a gap in this module, stated here rather than in a footnote. This module has no terrestrial fitted slope with a published error bar. Every measured exponent it quotes is a solar-wind exponent, from §7 . What the laboratory data contribute is the constant and its universality — a different claim, and a weaker one, than a measured $-5/3$. A reader who wants the exponent measured must take it from a spacecraft, which is the next-but-one section.

370: Proposition 6 (when there is no viscosity to put in). If $\mathrm{Kn} \gtrsim 1/3$, the Chapman–Enskog expansion of Module 1 is failing, the Navier–Stokes viscosity does not exist as a property of the gas, and $UL/\nu$ is a formula evaluated outside its own domain. By (1.2), such a gas has
$$ \mathrm{Re} \;\lt\; 1 \quad\text{whenever}\quad \mathrm{Kn} \;\gt\; 3\,\mathrm{Ma_{th}}, \tag{6.1}$$
so "the Reynolds number is small" and "the fluid description has failed" are one statement in such a gas, not two. Where a magnetic field is present, the transport step across the field is not $\lambda$ but the gyroradius $r_g$, so the perpendicular viscosity is bounded above and the perpendicular Reynolds number below:
$$ \nu_\perp \;\le\; \frac{r_g\,\bar v}{3}, \qquad \mathrm{Re}_\perp \;\ge\; \frac{UL}{r_g\bar v/3}. \tag{6.2}$$
376: The first part is (1.2) rearranged: $\mathrm{Re} = 3\,\mathrm{Ma_{th}}/\mathrm{Kn} \lt 1$ exactly when $\mathrm{Kn} \gt 3\,\mathrm{Ma_{th}}$. Nothing further is needed, and that is the point — one identity turns a statement about a transport coefficient into a statement about a mean free path, which is why Fig. 2 can draw both on one plane.

For (6.2), Module 1 §8 showed that across $\mathbf{B}$ a charged particle's excursion between scatterings is bounded by $r_g$ however long it goes without colliding, whereas along $\mathbf{B}$ it is free to travel $\lambda$. A diffusivity is a step squared over a time, so replacing the step $\lambda$ by $r_g$ in $\nu = \lambda\bar v/3$ gives the perpendicular value, and it is an upper bound because scattering can only be more effective than the gyro-orbit alone, never less. Dividing $UL$ by an upper bound on $\nu$ gives a lower bound on $\mathrm{Re}_\perp$, which is (6.2). It is printed as a bound in both directions throughout this module and never as a value. ∎

549: 7. The anchor: the solar wind at 1 au
551: This is the module's anchor, and it earns that name for a reason no other check in it can claim: one table, from one instrument, over one fit window, confirms one prediction and refutes another. A check that can only ever confirm is a weak instrument, because it cannot tell a law from a coincidence. This one is sharp enough to separate two fields of the same plasma.
553: Podesta, Roberts & Goldstein (2007) took Wind spacecraft data in the ecliptic at 1 au and fitted power laws to the magnetic, velocity, kinetic-energy and total-energy frequency spectra over four intervals: 1995 May, 1997 December, 2000 November and 2003 June — one near solar minimum, one near maximum, one on the ascending phase and one on the descending phase, each 54 to 81 days long. Every exponent was fitted independently of every other, with no fluid theory imposed.
555: Taylor's hypothesis is doing work here, and it is an assumption. A spacecraft measures a time series at a fixed point, so what is fitted is a frequency spectrum $P(f)$. Converting it to a wavenumber spectrum assumes the structures are swept past the spacecraft faster than they evolve or propagate — that the wind speed greatly exceeds the Alfvén and sound speeds. On Module 1 's census values the Alfvén speed is $v_A = B/\sqrt{4\pi\rho} = 48.8\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ with $\rho = n m_{\rm p}$, so $U/v_A = 8.2$: the wind sweeps a structure past the spacecraft about eight times faster than the structure can propagate. That is a ratio of order ten and not of order a hundred, so the assumption is good rather than exact. It is not repaid anywhere in this book. Frequencies in this section are written $f$, in hertz , and never $\nu$, which is the kinematic viscosity throughout this module.
557: The fit window is $10^{-3} \le f \le 10^{-2}$ Hz. Their Sect. 3 bounds the window's own effect: moving the endpoints over $7\times10^{-4}$ to $1.5\times10^{-3}$ Hz and $7\times10^{-3}$ to $1.5\times10^{-2}$ Hz changes an exponent "typically by 1%", always less than the tabulated error. The tabulated errors are $99$ per cent confidence limits, not standard deviations: divide by $2.576$ for one $\sigma$. 694: The density cancels; the mean mass per particle does not. That is why a line width and a temperature suffice, with no density measurement anywhere — and it is why $\mu = 0.61$ must be named every time (8.3) is used , including in every problem statement that uses it, because the ratio is linear in it.

703: 8.2 The mass a hydrostatic analysis misses
705: Proposition 7 (the hydrostatic mass bias). Suppose the gas is supported against gravity by $P_{\rm tot} = P_{\rm th} + P_{\rm turb}$, and suppose $\alpha = P_{\rm turb}/P_{\rm th}$ is constant with radius. Then an observer who infers a mass from the thermal pressure gradient alone obtains $M_{\rm th}$, whereas the true mass is $M = (1+\alpha)M_{\rm th}$, and the fraction of the mass missed is
$$ \frac{M - M_{\rm th}}{M} \;=\; \frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}. \tag{8.4}$$ The constant-$\alpha$ assumption is stated before the number, not after it.
709: Hydrostatic balance for the total pressure is $\mathrm{d}P_{\rm tot}/\mathrm{d}r = -GM\rho/r^2$, which is Module 3 's equation with the turbulent term added. If $\alpha$ does not depend on $r$ then $P_{\rm tot} = (1+\alpha)P_{\rm th}$ and $\mathrm{d}P_{\rm tot}/\mathrm{d}r = (1+\alpha)\,\mathrm{d}P_{\rm th}/\mathrm{d}r$, so
$$ -\frac{GM\rho}{r^2} \;=\; (1+\alpha)\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\rm th}}{\mathrm{d}r} \;=\; -(1+\alpha)\frac{GM_{\rm th}\rho}{r^2}, $$
giving $M = (1+\alpha)M_{\rm th}$ and hence (8.4). Drop the constant-$\alpha$ assumption and the result changes in kind, not in size: $\mathrm{d}P_{\rm tot}/\mathrm{d}r = (1+\alpha)\mathrm{d}P_{\rm th}/\mathrm{d}r + P_{\rm th}\,\mathrm{d}\alpha/\mathrm{d}r$, so the bias acquires a term in the difference between the two pressures' logarithmic scale lengths, and a single dispersion measured in a single annulus cannot supply it. ∎
715: Check 3 — CONFIRMED: the price of one of Module 3 's idealisations. At $\alpha = 4.15$ per cent, (8.4) gives a hydrostatic mass bias of $\mathbf{3.98}$ per cent — and $\mathbf{7.66}$ per cent if the large-scale shear the paper says could "at most double" the estimate does so. Treating this cluster core as static costs about four per cent of its mass, or about eight in the worst case the observers themselves allow. The idealisation is a good one, and now it is a good one with a number attached.
717: Scope, stated: one pointing, one annulus, one cluster. Hitomi observed $30$–$60$ kpc in one cluster core, and the paper itself restricts the claim to "the central regions". Cluster masses used for cosmology are measured near $r_{500}$, far outside this radius, where no comparable measurement exists. Nothing in this section licenses a four-per-cent correction to a cluster mass function.

The central region — which contains the active galactic nucleus — gives $187 \pm 13\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ at $3.8$ keV, so $\alpha = 5.82$ per cent there: higher, and not cleanly attributable to a cascade. And the systematic budget on the $164$ is energy resolution $\pm6$, temperature $\pm2$ and calibration scatter $-3\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ — all smaller than the $\pm10$ statistical limit, and all quoted by the collaboration.
721: 9. Supersonic turbulence, and the density it makes
723: Everything so far has assumed the flow is incompressible, and this is the first place the assumption is named. Proposition 2's dimensional argument has no sound speed in it, the $4/5$ law is derived for an incompressible fluid, and the intracluster medium of §8 is subsonic at $\mathcal{M}_{\rm 3D} = 0.273$, which is why it was the right place to apply the theory. A molecular cloud is not subsonic. Its turbulent speeds exceed its sound speed by a factor of ten, and this section works out what changes.
725: Definition 3 (sonic Mach number, and the log density). $\mathcal{M} \equiv U/c_s$, with $c_s$ the adiabatic sound speed of Module 4 and $U$ the root-mean-square turbulent speed — not a bulk speed and not a one-component dispersion. And
$$ s \;\equiv\; \ln\!\left(\frac{\rho}{\langle\rho\rangle}\right), \tag{9.1}$$
the logarithm of the density in units of its mean, which is the variable in which the statistics below are simple.
729: For the cloud row of §6 — $10$ K, $\mu = 2.33$, $\gamma = 5/3$ — the sound speed is $c_s = 0.2439\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, against Larson's three-dimensional $\sigma_L = 2.639\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ at $10$ pc. So $\mathcal{M} = 10.82$: ten times the sound speed, where the intracluster medium was a quarter of it. The flow is a field of shocks, and the first thing to ask is what a field of shocks does to a spectrum.
731: Proposition 8 (a field of shocks gives $k^{-2}$, and $h = 1/2$). A velocity field consisting of isolated discontinuities has
$$ E(k) \;\propto\; k^{-2}, \qquad \delta v(\ell) \;\propto\; \ell^{1/2}. \tag{9.2}$$
735: Take a single step of height $\Delta u$ at $x_0$: $u(x) = \Delta u\,H(x-x_0)$. Its Fourier transform is
$$ \hat u(k) \;=\; \Delta u\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kx_0}}{\mathrm{i}k}, \qquad |\hat u(k)|^2 \;=\; \frac{(\Delta u)^2}{k^2}, $$
so the energy per unit wavenumber falls as $k^{-2}$. That $|\hat u(k)|^2$ is a one-dimensional spectral density, and the $E(k)$ of (2.2) is the three-dimensional one; for a power law the two carry the same exponent and differ only by a constant factor — the isotropy factor $55/18$ of (5.2) is that constant for the Kolmogorov case — so the exponent $-2$ transfers and the prefactor does not. A superposition of randomly placed steps adds these spectra incoherently and leaves the exponent unchanged; only the prefactor counts the shocks.

For the increment, consider two points a distance $\ell$ apart in a field with $n_s$ shocks per unit length. The probability that a shock lies between them is $n_s\ell$ for $n_s\ell \ll 1$, and when one does, the increment is $\Delta u$; otherwise it is the small smooth variation, which may be neglected. Then
$$ \big\langle\delta v(\ell)^2\big\rangle \;\simeq\; n_s\ell\,(\Delta u)^2 \;\propto\; \ell, $$
so the root-mean-square increment scales as $\ell^{1/2}$ and $h = 1/2$, against Kolmogorov's $h = 1/3$ from (2.3). The two statements in (9.2) are consistent with each other by Proposition 10 below, which is the general bridge between an increment exponent and a spectral one. ∎
745: Two exponents are now in play — $5/3$ from a smooth cascade and $2$ from a field of shocks — and Fig. 1 drew them together at the start of this module so that the difference would be visible: a factor $2.15$ per decade. §10 measures which one a molecular cloud obeys.
747: 9.1 What supersonic turbulence does to the density
749: Incompressible turbulence has no density statistics to speak of. Supersonic turbulence has nothing else: shocks compress gas, and the distribution of densities they produce is what makes stars.
751: Definition 4 (the forcing parameter). $b$ is the ratio of the fractional density dispersion to the Mach number, $\sigma_\rho/\langle\rho\rangle = b\mathcal{M}$. It runs from about $1/3$ for purely solenoidal (divergence-free) forcing in three dimensions to about $1$ for purely compressive (curl-free) forcing. Both endpoints are stated, from Federrath et al. (2010), their Sect. 5 and Fig. 8 ; this module quotes them and computes nothing about the forcing itself. The value $1/3$ is a three-dimensional statement: solenoidal forcing drives one of the three components compressively by a cascade, and the fraction is $1/3$ for that reason.
753: Proposition 9 (the log-normal density distribution). In isothermal supersonic turbulence $s$ of (9.1) is normally distributed with variance
$$ \sigma_s^2 \;=\; \ln\!\left(1 + b^2\mathcal{M}^2\right) \tag{9.3}$$
— stated , Federrath et al. (2010) equations (18) and (19) — and with mean
$$ s_0 \;=\; -\tfrac12\sigma_s^2, \tag{9.4}$$
which is forced, not fitted . The fraction of the volume with $\rho/\langle\rho\rangle$ above a threshold $x$ is then
$$ f(\gt x) \;=\; \tfrac12\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left[\frac{\ln x - s_0}{\sigma_s\sqrt2}\right]. \tag{9.5}$$
761: Equation (9.3) is quoted; it is a fit to simulations, not a theorem, and the module marks it as such. Equations (9.4) and (9.5) follow from it.

For (9.4): if $s$ is normal with mean $s_0$ and variance $\sigma_s^2$, then $\langle\mathrm{e}^{s}\rangle = \exp(s_0 + \tfrac12\sigma_s^2)$, the standard Gaussian moment-generating result. But $\mathrm{e}^{s} = \rho/\langle\rho\rangle$ by (9.1), whose volume average is $1$ by construction . Setting $\exp(s_0+\tfrac12\sigma_s^2) = 1$ gives $s_0 = -\tfrac12\sigma_s^2$. The mean of $s$ is therefore not an independent parameter: the most probable density is below the mean density, and by an amount fixed entirely by the width. For (9.5): $f(\gt x) = \int_{\ln x}^{\infty} (2\pi\sigma_s^2)^{-1/2}\exp[-(s-s_0)^2/2\sigma_s^2]\,\mathrm{d}s$. Substituting $t = (s-s_0)/(\sigma_s\sqrt2)$ turns this into $\pi^{-1/2}\int_{t_0}^\infty \mathrm{e}^{-t^2}\mathrm{d}t = \tfrac12\mathrm{erfc}(t_0)$ with $t_0 = (\ln x - s_0)/(\sigma_s\sqrt2)$. ∎

846: 10. The interstellar check: the size–linewidth exponent
848: The last check asks whether the smooth cascade of §2 describes a molecular cloud — specifically, whether its size–linewidth exponent is Kolmogorov's $h = 1/3$ — and the answer is no. It is the only refutation in this module that goes against K41 itself rather than against one of its hypotheses, and the way it is obtained needs a bridge between two different kinds of statistic, which is the proposition that follows.
850: Proposition 10 (from an increment exponent to a spectral exponent). Suppose $E(k)$ is a power law $Ak^{-p}$ with $p \gt 1$ over a range of wavenumbers, and let $\delta v(\ell)$ be the root-mean-square increment across the corresponding separations. Then $\delta v(\ell) \propto \ell^{h}$ with $h = (p-1)/2$, equivalently
$$ E(k) \;\propto\; k^{-(1+2h)}. \tag{10.1}$$
In particular $h = 1/3$ gives $5/3$ and $h = 1/2$ gives $2$. The power-law hypothesis is the one that cannot be dropped: a measured increment exponent fixes a spectral exponent only if there is a spectral power law to fix, and §10 measures the increment exponent, not the spectrum.
854: The velocity increment across a separation $\ell$ samples all wavenumbers above $k = 1/\ell$, so
$$ \delta v(\ell)^2 \;\simeq\; \int_{1/\ell}^{\infty} E(k)\,\mathrm{d}k. $$
Put $E(k) = Ak^{-p}$ with $p \gt 1$, which is required for the integral to converge at the upper limit. Then
$$ \int_{1/\ell}^{\infty}Ak^{-p}\,\mathrm{d}k \;=\; \frac{A}{p-1}\,\ell^{\,p-1}, $$
so $\delta v \propto \ell^{(p-1)/2}$. Matching to $\ell^{h}$ gives $h = (p-1)/2$, that is $p = 1+2h$, which is (10.1). Substituting $h = 1/3$ gives $p = 5/3$, recovering Proposition 2, and $h = 1/2$ gives $p = 2$, recovering Proposition 8. ∎
862: This module measures an exponent, and infers a spectrum. What §10 compares against the sky is $h$, a size–linewidth exponent. The corresponding $E(k) \propto k^{-2}$ follows through (10.1) and through the assumption stated below about two-point versus ensemble statistics. No interstellar power spectrum is measured anywhere in this module , and the only measured spectral exponents in it are Podesta's eight, in §7 .

880: 10.2 The comparison, and what it does and does not decide
882: The assumption, stated before the verdict and not after it. $\delta v(\ell) \sim \ell^{1/3}$ is a two-point statistic inside one flow : the typical velocity difference between two points a distance $\ell$ apart. Solomon's exponent comes from a regression across 273 different clouds , each contributing one linewidth and one size. Reading the second as the first assumes that a cloud's internal dispersion samples the same cascade a structure function would, and that the cloud-to-cloud ensemble behaves statistically as one object. Larson made that identification explicitly and it is standard practice. It is still an assumption , and the refutation below depends on it.
884: Check 4 — REFUTED. The measured exponent is $0.50$. Kolmogorov's $1/3$ lies $0.1667$ below it, which is $3.33$ times the whole systematic envelope of $\pm0.05$; Burgers' $1/2$ lies on it, a gap of $0.0000$ envelopes. Incompressible Kolmogorov scaling does not describe a molecular cloud. A gap of three envelopes is a refutation rather than a demolition, and the module prints the gap rather than a confidence level it cannot compute.
886: The statistical case is stronger than the number printed, not weaker. The formal fit error is smaller than the systematic envelope — the authors say so — so quoting the envelope is the conservative choice. And the authors reach the same verdict in their own words, on the same page: "Larson (1981), from a literature search, suggested that the observed exponent was close to 1/3 and reflected a Kolmogorov turbulent spectrum. This hypothesis is ruled out by the data presented here."
888: Why Solomon rather than Larson, when the two exponents differ. Larson's $0.38$ is much closer to $1/3$, and if it were the number in hand the verdict would be different. It is shown here beside Solomon's $0.50$ so that a reader sees both, and the reason this module weights Solomon's is stated rather than assumed: Larson's line is eye-fitted from a literature compilation with no error bar of any kind , its only stated scatter being a factor of $1.38$ in $\sigma$; Solomon's is a least-squares fit to $273$ clouds from one survey with a stated error envelope. Larson's exponent is the reason the question was asked. Solomon's is the reason it can be answered.
890: The exponent refutes Kolmogorov. It does not select Burgers. $\sigma_v \propto S^{1/2}$ is also exactly what virial equilibrium at constant surface density gives: $\sigma^2 \sim GM/S$ with $M \sim \Sigma S^2$ yields $\sigma \sim (G\Sigma)^{1/2}S^{1/2}$, independent of any cascade. Solomon et al. read their own result that way, their constant mean surface density being $170\ M_\odot\,\mathrm{pc^{-2}}$. Two quite different physical pictures predict the same exponent, and this module does not claim the measurement distinguishes them.
892: An order-of-magnitude cross-check of that reading, and its limit. Putting their normalisation $\sigma_v(1\ \mathrm{pc}) = 1.0\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ into $\sigma_{\rm 1D}^2 = \pi G\Sigma S/3$ gives $\Sigma = 222\ M_\odot\,\mathrm{pc^{-2}}$ against the $170$ they quote, a ratio of $1.31$. The factor $\pi/3$ is this book's choice of a uniform sphere, not Solomon et al.'s virial coefficient , so a ratio of order one is all the comparison can show, and it is quoted for no more than that.

941: The three-dimensionality of $\sigma_L$ matters here. Larson adds his dispersion into a three-dimensional $\sigma^2$, so $2.64\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ is the full speed and not one component. Using a one-dimensional linewidth in its place would give a turnover time $\sqrt3$ too long.
945: Solution
946: $\varepsilon = (2.64\times10^{5})^3/3.0857\times10^{19} = 5.963\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$. At $\ell = 1\ \mathrm{pc} = 3.0857\times10^{18}$ cm,
947: $S_3 = -\tfrac45\varepsilon\ell = -0.8\times5.963\times10^{-4}\times3.0857\times10^{18} = -1.472\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-3}}$, so $|S_3|^{1/3} = 1.138\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, against $(\varepsilon\ell)^{1/3} = 1.225\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$.
948: The ratio is $(4/5)^{1/3} = 0.9283$, and it could have been written down before any arithmetic , because both quantities are the cube root of the same product and differ only by the factor $4/5$. The dimensional estimate (2.3) and the exact law (4.1) agree on the scaling and differ by eight per cent in the speed — which is the sense in which "$\delta v \sim (\varepsilon\ell)^{1/3}$" is a good estimate rather than a loose one.
949: The minus sign says the distribution of longitudinal increments is skewed toward approach, which is the direction of the cascade. Note also what has been assumed to get here: $\varepsilon = U^3/L$ is (2.3) at the outer scale, so this problem has used K41 to evaluate the one result in the subject that does not depend on K41.
953: Solution
954: $\nu = \lambda\bar v_i/3 = (6.943\times10^{22})(1.450\times10^{8})/3 = 3.3557\times10^{30}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $\varepsilon = U^3/L = (1.64\times10^{7})^3/(1.8514\times10^{23}) = 2.382\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$. Then $\eta = (\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4} = 1.995\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{cm} = 64.67$ kpc.
955: That answer must be rejected, and rejecting it is the point of the problem. $\eta/L = 64.67/60 = 1.078 \gt 1$: the scale at which viscosity is supposed to take over is larger than the system. There is no inertial range, so (2.1) is empty and $\eta$ is not a dissipation scale but a demonstration that the premise is wrong. The same conclusion arrives from the other direction as $\mathrm{Kn} = 22.5/60 = 0.375$, and the two are one statement by (1.2). Note also that $\varepsilon = U^3/L$ is itself a K41 relation, so the calculation used a cascade to locate the end of a cascade it has just shown cannot exist.
956: With the field: $r_g = 1.341\times10^{10}$ cm at $1\ \mu$G, so $\nu_\perp \le r_g\bar v_i/3 = 6.4824\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$, and $\eta \ge (\nu_\perp^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4} = 5.815\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm} = 3.887$ au. That is $3.1\times10^{-10}$ of the system size, so the inertial range now spans nine and a half orders of magnitude. Both signs must be carried: $\nu_\perp$ is an upper bound, so $\eta$ is a lower bound and $\mathrm{Re}_\perp \ge 4.684\times10^{12}$ is a lower bound too. Neither is a value.
960: Solution
961: $\sigma_s = \sqrt{\ln(1+b^2\mathcal{M}^2)}$. At $b = 1/3$: $b\mathcal{M} = 3.333$, $1+b^2\mathcal{M}^2 = 12.11$, $\sigma_s = 1.5793$. At $b = 1$: $b\mathcal{M} = 10$, $1+100 = 101$, $\sigma_s = 2.1483$. The widths differ by a factor $1.36$.
962: $s_0 = -\sigma_s^2/2$ is $-1.247$ and $-2.308$. With $\ln 100 = 4.605$, equation (9.5) gives $t_0 = (4.605+1.247)/(1.5793\sqrt2) = 2.620$ and $(4.605+2.308)/(2.1483\sqrt2) = 2.275$, so
$f(\gt100) = \tfrac12\mathrm{erfc}(2.620) = 1.054\times10^{-4}$ and $\tfrac12\mathrm{erfc}(2.275) = 6.459\times10^{-4}$.
964: A factor $1.36$ in the width becomes a factor $6.1$ in the tail — and at $\rho \gt 1000\langle\rho\rangle$, a factor $73.9$. The reason is that the tail of a Gaussian is controlled by the exponent $-t_0^2$, and $t_0$ is inversely proportional to $\sigma_s$; a small change in the denominator is a large change after exponentiation. Since star formation is a threshold process in the far tail, the forcing that a cloud experiences matters far more for its star-formation rate than for any bulk property of its density field.
965: Note that $\sigma_s$ is dimensionless and is not a speed; it is the width of $\ln(\rho/\langle\rho\rangle)$, and comparing it with any of the speeds of §6.4 would be meaningless.
969: Solution
970: $\lambda = 1/(n\sigma_{\rm H}) = 10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm} = 0.67$ au and $\bar v = \sqrt{8k_{\rm B}T/\pi\mu m_{\rm u}} = 0.3014\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so $\nu = \lambda\bar v/3 = 1.0048\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ and
$$\mathrm{Re} = \frac{(2.64\times10^{5})(3.0857\times10^{19})}{1.0048\times10^{17}} = 8.107\times10^{7}.$$
972: By (3.4), $L/\eta = \mathrm{Re}^{3/4} = (8.107\times10^{7})^{3/4} = 8.54\times10^{5}$. Directly: $\varepsilon = 5.963\times10^{-4}$ from C3 gives $\eta = (\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4} = 3.6\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm} = 2.4$ au, and $3.0857\times10^{19}/3.6\times10^{13} = 8.5\times10^{5}$. The two agree, as they must: (3.4) was derived by putting $\varepsilon = U^3/L$ into the definition of $\eta$.

The cross-section is good to about a factor of two, so read $\mathrm{Re}$ to one significant figure; nothing in this module depends on more.
977: Solution
978: Side: $\mathrm{Re}^{3/4} = (10^{6})^{3/4} = 31623$ points. Total: $\mathrm{Re}^{9/4} = 3.162\times10^{13}$ points. Snapshot: $24\times3.162\times10^{13} = 7.589\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{B} = 759$ TB.
979: Work on the CFL count: $\mathrm{Re}^{3} = 10^{18}$ point-updates per outer turnover. On the Kolmogorov-time count: $\mathrm{Re}^{11/4} = 3.16\times10^{16}$, smaller by $\mathrm{Re}^{1/4} = 31.6$ — which is (3.6), $U/u_\eta$. The softer count is the number of turnovers of the smallest eddy; it is not the number of steps a time-explicit code takes, because the code is limited by the largest speed on the smallest cell and not by the local eddy time.
980: And $759$ TB is one snapshot of one field at a Reynolds number three orders of magnitude below the solar photosphere of §1 . A time integrator needs at least two copies, and a magnetohydrodynamic code needs three more fields.
984: Solution
985: (a) Invert (8.4): a bias of $0.20 = \alpha/(1+\alpha)$ needs $\alpha = 0.25$. Then (8.3) rearranges to $\sigma_v = \sqrt{\alpha\,k_{\rm B}T/(\mu m_{\rm u})}$. With $k_{\rm B}T = 4.1\ \mathrm{keV} = 6.569\times10^{-9}$ erg and $\mu m_{\rm u} = 0.61\times1.6605\times10^{-24} = 1.013\times10^{-24}$ g, $k_{\rm B}T/(\mu m_{\rm u}) = 6.485\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-2}}$, so $\sigma_v = \sqrt{1.621\times10^{15}} = 4.03\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}} = 403\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$.
986: That is $2.46$ times the measured $164\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ — and, since $\alpha \propto \sigma_v^2$, a factor $2.46$ in the dispersion is a factor $6.0$ in the pressure ratio. A twenty-per-cent hydrostatic mass bias in this core is not marginally excluded; it is excluded by a factor of six in the quantity that matters. The $\mu = 0.61$ is carried explicitly because the answer scales as $\mu^{-1/2}$ and the density never enters.
987: (b) $E(k) = C\varepsilon^{2/3}k^{-5/3}$ with $\varepsilon = 5.963\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-3}}$ and $k = 1/(3.0857\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{cm})$: $\varepsilon^{2/3} = 7.08\times10^{-3}$, $k^{-5/3} = 6.55\times10^{30}$, so $E = 1.62\times7.08\times10^{-3}\times6.55\times10^{30} = 7.503\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-2}}$.
988: Read it as a range. §5 gives the three-dimensional constant as $1.53$–$1.62$: $1.62$ from Sreenivasan's compiled $C_K = 0.53$, and $1.53$ from his own revision to $0.50$ after allowing that the local-isotropy estimate of $\varepsilon$ undercounts by about ten per cent. So $E$ at this wavenumber is $7.1$ to $7.5\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-2}}$, and quoting a single value to four figures would claim a precision the constant does not have. The units are worth checking: $\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-2}}$, an energy per unit mass per unit wavenumber, as Definition 2 requires.
992: Solution
993: (a) Unmagnetised: $\mathrm{Re} = UL/\nu = 0.90$ at $\mathrm{Kn} = 0.375$ . The two must be quoted together, because $\mathrm{Kn} = 0.375$ is outside the Chapman–Enskog domain and the $0.90$ is therefore a formula evaluated where it does not apply — not a statement that the gas is nearly laminar. By (1.2) the two facts are one fact: $\mathrm{Re} \lt 1$ because $\mathrm{Kn} \gt 3\,\mathrm{Ma_{th}} = 0.34$.
994: Magnetised: $\nu_\perp \le r_g\bar v_i/3 = 6.4824\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$, so $\mathrm{Re}_\perp \ge 4.68\times10^{12}$. It is a lower bound and must never be written as a value , because (6.2) bounds $\nu_\perp$ from above: scattering can only be more effective than the bare gyro-orbit. The true effective viscosity is set by microinstabilities, and Zhuravleva et al. (2019) bound its suppression below the Coulomb value at a factor of $10$ to $1000$.
995: (b) $N = \mathrm{Re}^{9/4} = 10^{9}$ gives $\mathrm{Re} = (10^{9})^{4/9} = 1.000\times10^{4}$. A $1000^3$ grid — a large calculation — resolves a Reynolds number of ten thousand. The photosphere of §1 is at $1.4\times10^{9}$, five orders of magnitude higher, and by (3.4) it would need $\mathrm{Re}^{9/4} = 3.8\times10^{20}$ points. No increase in computer power closes a gap of eleven and a half orders of magnitude in $N$ , which is why astrophysical codes model the small scales instead of resolving them.
