120: 1. A fluid with no length
122: Write out the equations of Module 2 and look for a length in them. There is none. Continuity, Euler and the energy equation contain densities, velocities, pressures and derivatives, and not one intrinsic scale — the viscosity and conductivity that would supply one were dropped in Module 2 on the strength of Module 1 's Knudsen numbers. Every module since has treated that as a licence to work with smooth fields. This one treats it as a licence to do the opposite.
124: If the equations contain no length, nothing in them forbids a solution in which $\rho$, $v$ and $p$ change by a finite amount across zero distance. Such a surface is a shock . The physical layer is of course not of zero thickness: Problem D2 works out that in sea-level air it is a few mean free paths, about $2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{cm}$, which is $2\times10^{8}$ times thinner than the Trinity fireball was one millisecond after detonation. Against every other length in the problem that is zero, and the idealisation is safe by eight orders of magnitude.
126: The gain from the idealisation is larger than it first appears. Because the layer is unresolved, no equation of motion inside it is available — and none is needed. The three conservation laws, integrated across the layer, relate the state on one side to the state on the other with nothing said about the constitutive physics of the interior at all . That is §2 , and it is the whole of shock theory in three lines. What comes out is not a small correction: the downstream gas can be a hundred times hotter than the upstream gas, and the ratio is fixed by the upstream Mach number alone.
128: A second absence of length drives the rest of the module. Deposit an energy $E$ at a point, instantaneously, in a uniform gas of density $\rho_0$, and ask for the radius $R(t)$ of the shock that runs out from it. The problem contains $E$, $\rho_0$ and $t$ and nothing else — no length, again. There is exactly one length that can be built from those three, so $R$ must be proportional to $(Et^2/\rho_0)^{1/5}$ and the exponent $2/5$ is settled before any dynamics is done. That argument is §4 , and G. I. Taylor made it in 1941.
130: Four years later the argument met a test that no theoretician arranges for himself. On 16 July 1945 the first nuclear device was fired in New Mexico, and the fireball was photographed by a high-speed camera with a length scale in the frame. In 1950 Taylor published twenty-five radii against twenty-five times, read off that film, and compared them with the prediction he had made nine years before seeing it. §5 repeats the comparison from his own table, and finds two things: the exponent is confirmed to better than two and a half per cent over a factor of 620 in time, and the model that produced the exponent is refuted by the residuals from the very same fit. Both statements come from one table, and the second is the more instructive.
132: The module then leaves the laboratory twice. §6 uses a shock that a spacecraft flew through and measured from inside — Voyager 2 at the solar-wind termination shock in 2007, with Neptune's bow shock from the same instrument eighteen years earlier as a control. The control confirms the compression ceiling exactly; the termination shock refutes the single-fluid closure this book has used since Module 2, and the way it fails is precisely the way Module 1 said a collisionless plasma would fail. §7 then follows a supernova remnant from the moment the Sedov solution becomes valid to the moment it stops being valid, and §8 pays a debt: Module 7 closed by recording that its Rayleigh–Taylor half had no astronomical check anywhere in Modules 1–10. It has one now, and the check refutes the transfer.
134: 2. The jump conditions
136: Everything in this module is a statement about the configuration defined next, and every hypothesis it carries is listed once here so that the propositions can name it rather than restate it.
138: Definition 1 (the shock). A surface across which $\rho$, $v$ and $p$ are discontinuous, of thickness negligible against every other length in the problem, moving through the gas at speed $D$. All quantities below are measured in the rest frame of the surface, in which the flow is steady and, locally, one-dimensional along the normal $\hat n$. Subscript $1$ denotes the state ahead of the surface (upstream) and subscript $2$ the state behind it (downstream), with $v_1, v_2 \gt 0$ the normal flow speeds in that frame, so the gas passes from side $1$ to side $2$. Far enough on each side of the surface the gas is uniform : $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}n = 0$ and $\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}n = 0$ there. That, and not the thickness itself, is what Proposition 1 uses. The gas is an ideal polytropic gas of ratio of specific heats $\gamma$ on both sides, with specific enthalpy $w = \gamma p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$.
140: Three hypotheses there will be paid for later, and it is worth naming which section pays. Steady in the surface frame fails when the shock accelerates on a timescale comparable to the crossing time; it does not fail for any case in this module. Polytropic with the same $\gamma$ on both sides is the assumption §5 finds broken at Trinity, where dissociation, vibration and radiation all act behind the front and Taylor argues — with evidence, and not quite successfully — that they cancel. One fluid, one temperature is the assumption §6 finds broken at the termination shock, where the energy goes to a population of pickup ions that the single-fluid description has no room for.
142: Definition 2 (the upstream Mach number). $$ M_1 \;\equiv\; \frac{v_1}{c_1}, \qquad c_1 \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\gamma p_1}{\rho_1}}, \tag{2.1}$$
with $v_1$ the normal flow speed in the frame of the surface , which is the speed at which the shock advances into gas that is at rest in the laboratory. $c_1$ is the adiabatic sound speed of Module 4 Proposition 1, evaluated in the upstream gas. A shock is called strong when $M_1 \gg 1$; Problem D1 puts a number on how large that has to be.
168: The control volume of Proposition 1. The grey band is the shock layer, drawn with no stated thickness because none is used. Three fluxes enter on the left and three leave on the right, and the content of the proposition is that each pair is equal. The question mark is the point of the figure: the interior of the band contains viscous stress, heat conduction and, at Trinity, dissociation and radiation, and none of it appears anywhere in the result.
171: Fig. 1 is the whole proof in a picture, and the question mark in the middle of it is the content of the proposition.
173: Proposition 1 (the Rankine–Hugoniot conditions). Across the surface of Definition 1,
$$ \rho_1 v_1 \;=\; \rho_2 v_2, \tag{2.2}$$
$$ \rho_1 v_1^2 + p_1 \;=\; \rho_2 v_2^2 + p_2, \tag{2.3}$$
$$ w_1 + \tfrac12 v_1^2 \;=\; w_2 + \tfrac12 v_2^2, \qquad w = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{p}{\rho}. \tag{2.4}$$
No constitutive property of the interior enters: no value of the viscosity, the conductivity or the layer thickness appears in (2.2)–(2.4), and no equation of motion inside the layer is used.
179: The equations to integrate are the conservation forms of Module 2 — Module 2 Proposition 1 for mass, Proposition 2 for momentum and Proposition 5 for total energy — with the dissipative fluxes put back. They must be put back, because the layer is exactly the region where Module 1 's Knudsen argument for dropping them fails; that failure is what makes the layer a shock. In the frame of Definition 1, where $\partial_t = 0$ and only the normal derivative survives, each equation reads $\mathrm{d}F/\mathrm{d}n = S$ with
$$ F_{\rm mass} = \rho v, \qquad F_{\rm mom} = \rho v^2 + p - \sigma_{nn}, \qquad F_{\rm en} = \rho v\left(w + \tfrac12 v^2\right) - \sigma_{nn}v - q_n, $$
where $\sigma_{nn}$ is the normal viscous stress, $q_n$ the conductive heat flux along the normal, and $S$ collects the bounded volume sources — gravity in the momentum equation, radiative gain and loss per unit volume in the energy equation.
Integrate each from $n = -\epsilon$ to $n = +\epsilon$, with $\epsilon$ chosen outside the layer on both sides, so that both endpoints lie in the uniform gas of Definition 1. Two things then happen, and they happen only because of where the endpoints were put. First, $\sigma_{nn}$ and $q_n$ are built from $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}n$ and $\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}n$, and both gradients vanish at the endpoints, so the dissipative terms drop out of $F(+\epsilon)$ and $F(-\epsilon)$ however large they are in between. Second, $\int_{-\epsilon}^{+\epsilon}S\,\mathrm{d}n \to 0$ as the layer thickness goes to zero, because $S$ is bounded. What survives is $F(+\epsilon) = F(-\epsilon)$ for each of the three, with every $F$ evaluated where its dissipative part is zero, and that is (2.2)–(2.4). Dividing the third by the first, which is legitimate because $\rho v \neq 0$ for a shock, turns the energy flux equality into the enthalpy form (2.4). ∎
185: Three remarks on what the proof used, and one on what it did not. It required each equation to be a divergence, which is why the conservation forms were taken and not the convective ones: $\partial_t(\rho v)$ written as $\rho[\partial_t v + (v\cdot\nabla)v]$ is not a divergence and cannot be integrated across a discontinuity. It required the endpoints to lie in gas with no gradients, which is the whole working content of "the thickness is negligible": the layer must be thin enough that uniform gas is reached on both sides before anything else in the problem has changed. And it required every source to be a bounded volume source, so a layer that radiates away a finite amount of energy per unit area — a surface loss, not a bounded volume loss — breaks (2.4). That is the isothermal shock of Proposition 4, where (2.4) is deliberately replaced. What the proof did not use is any value of $\sigma_{nn}$, of $q_n$ or of the thickness, any equation of state inside the layer, or any assumption that the gas in there is in local thermodynamic equilibrium. That, and only that, is the sense in which the interior is not modelled.
189: It is worth pausing on how little was assumed. Nothing was said about viscosity, about heat conduction, about how thick the layer is or about whether the gas inside it is even in local thermodynamic equilibrium. All of that is real, and all of it is invisible to (2.2)–(2.4). What the conservation laws give up in exchange is any information about the structure of the transition: they say where the gas ends up, never how it got there. Problem D2 shows that the trade is a good one in air and a very bad one at the termination shock, where the layer is $10^5$ to $10^6$ times thinner than a collisional mean free path and the phrase "local thermodynamic equilibrium" has no referent at all.
191: 3. What the jumps say: a ceiling, a direction, and an escape
193: Proposition 1 is three equations in the three downstream unknowns $\rho_2$, $v_2$, $p_2$, given the upstream state. Solving them is algebra, and the answer is worth more than the algebra.
195: Proposition 2 (the jump ratios, and the ceiling on compression). For the gas of Definition 1, the non-trivial solution of (2.2)–(2.4) is
$$ \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} \;=\; \frac{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}{(\gamma-1)M_1^2+2}, \qquad \frac{p_2}{p_1} \;=\; \frac{2\gamma M_1^2-(\gamma-1)}{\gamma+1}, \tag{3.1}$$
$$ \frac{T_2}{T_1} \;=\; \frac{p_2/p_1}{\rho_2/\rho_1}, \qquad M_2^2 \;=\; \frac{(\gamma-1)M_1^2+2}{2\gamma M_1^2-(\gamma-1)}. \tag{3.2}$$
As $M_1\to\infty$ the density ratio saturates at
$$ \left.\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}\right|_{\max} \;=\; \frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}, \tag{3.3}$$
which is $4$ for $\gamma = 5/3$ and $6$ for $\gamma = 7/5$, while $p_2/p_1$ and $T_2/T_1$ grow without bound, both as $M_1^2$.
202: Write $r = \rho_2/\rho_1$. Mass conservation (2.2) gives $v_2 = v_1/r$. Substituting into the momentum condition (2.3),
$$ p_2 - p_1 \;=\; \rho_1v_1^2\left(1-\frac1r\right), $$
and dividing by $p_1 = \rho_1c_1^2/\gamma$ turns it into $p_2/p_1 = 1 + \gamma M_1^2(1-1/r)$. Put that and $v_2 = v_1/r$ into the enthalpy condition (2.4), multiply through by $(\gamma-1)/c_1^2$, and the result is a quadratic in $r$ whose two roots are $r = 1$ — the trivial solution, no shock — and the first of (3.1). The pressure ratio follows by back-substitution, the temperature ratio from the ideal gas law $T \propto p/\rho$, and $M_2$ from $M_2^2 = v_2^2\rho_2/(\gamma p_2) = (v_1^2/r^2)(r\rho_1)/(\gamma p_2)$. Taking $M_1\to\infty$ in the first of (3.1) leaves $(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$, which is (3.3). ∎
206: The two behaviours in Proposition 2 are physically different and the contrast is the point of Fig. 2 . Compression is bounded because the gas that piles up behind the front pushes back, and past a certain density no increase in the ram pressure can make it denser; heating is unbounded because the kinetic energy that has to go somewhere goes into internal energy, and internal energy has no ceiling. A strong shock is a device for making a hot gas, not a dense one. That single sentence explains why supernova remnants shine in X-rays rather than looking like dense shells, and why §7 can speak of $10^8\ \mathrm{K}$ gas at a density of a few particles per cubic centimetre.
208: The ceiling is approached quickly. At $\gamma = 5/3$, a Mach 3 shock has reached 75.00 per cent of it, Mach 5 gives 89.29 per cent, Mach 10 gives 97.09 per cent and Mach 30 gives 99.67 per cent. Problem D1 asks for the Mach number at which 99 per cent is reached and gets $M_1 = 17.2$ for $\gamma = 5/3$ and $22.2$ for $\gamma = 7/5$; the Trinity shock at one millisecond was at $M_1 = 43$ and was therefore strong by any measure.

308: 3.1 Why there is no such thing as a rarefaction shock
310: Nothing in Proposition 1 prefers one direction. Put $M_1 \lt 1$ into (3.1) and the formulae return perfectly finite ratios: at $M_1 = 0.7$ and $\gamma = 5/3$ they give $\rho_2/\rho_1 = 0.5616$ and $p_2/p_1 = 0.3625$, a jump from dense to rarefied that satisfies mass, momentum and energy conservation exactly. Such a thing is never seen. The reason is not in the conservation laws, and this is the only place in Modules 1–8 where the selection is made by thermodynamics rather than by dynamics.
312: Proposition 3 (the entropy jump fixes the direction). Across the surface of Definition 1, the entropy change per particle is
$$ \frac{\Delta s}{k_B} \;=\; \frac{1}{\gamma-1}\,\ln\!\left[\frac{p_2}{p_1}\left(\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\right)^{\!\gamma}\right], \tag{3.4}$$
with the ratios of (3.1). It is zero at $M_1 = 1$, positive for $M_1 \gt 1$ and negative for $M_1 \lt 1$ . The second law therefore admits only $M_1 \gt 1$: a shock is always compressive, and always advances into its gas supersonically.
316: For an ideal gas the entropy per particle is $s = c_v\ln(p\rho^{-\gamma}) + \text{const}$ with $c_v = k_B/(\gamma-1)$, which gives (3.4) directly. Set $M_1 = 1+\epsilon$ and expand both ratios of (3.1) to third order in $\epsilon$. The first-order and second-order terms cancel identically in the combination (3.4), and what survives is
$$ \frac{\Delta s}{k_B} \;=\; \frac{16\gamma}{3(\gamma+1)^2}\,\epsilon^3 + O(\epsilon^4), $$
an odd leading power. An odd leading power is the whole argument: $\Delta s$ changes sign with $\epsilon$, so the two branches are not symmetric and exactly one of them is admissible. The sign is confirmed away from $M_1 = 1$ by evaluation: at $\gamma = 5/3$, $\Delta s/k_B = +0.00101$ at $M_1 = 1.1$, $+0.85031$ at $M_1 = 3$, and $-0.00159$ at $M_1 = 0.9$, $-1.21225$ at $M_1 = 0.5$. ∎
320: A second obstruction sits below the first, and it is worth separating because it is not thermodynamic at all. The pressure ratio in (3.1) vanishes at $M_1 = \sqrt{(\gamma-1)/2\gamma}$, which is $0.4472$ for $\gamma = 5/3$, and goes negative below it — at $M_1 = 0.4$ it returns $p_2/p_1 = -0.0500$. Below that Mach number the "rarefaction shock" is not a solution that the second law forbids; it is not a solution at all, because it would require a negative pressure. So the two objections are of different kinds and cover different ranges, and Problem C3 asks the reader to state both.
322: What happens instead is a rarefaction fan : an expansion spreads out into a continuous region whose width grows with time, because each characteristic in it travels at its own speed and the faster ones run ahead. This module does not derive it. What matters here is why one is needed — the discontinuous alternative is forbidden — and that is a statement Proposition 3 makes without any further machinery.
324: 3.2 The escape: a shock that radiates
326: The ceiling (3.3) is a consequence of (2.4), and (2.4) assumed that no energy leaves the layer. Molecular gas radiates efficiently, and a dense shocked molecular cloud can lose its thermal energy far faster than the gas crosses the layer. Then the correct closure is not "no losses" but "the temperature does not change", and the ceiling disappears.
328: Proposition 4 (the isothermal shock). Replace (2.4) by $T_2 = T_1$, keeping (2.2) and (2.3). Then
$$ \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} \;=\; \mathcal{M}^2, \qquad \mathcal{M} \;\equiv\; \frac{v_1}{c_T}, \quad c_T \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{\mu m_u}}, \tag{3.5}$$
and there is no upper bound on the compression.
332: With $T$ fixed, $p = \rho c_T^2$ on both sides. Mass conservation gives $v_2 = v_1\rho_1/\rho_2$, and momentum conservation becomes
$$ \rho_1v_1^2 + \rho_1c_T^2 \;=\; \rho_2 v_2^2 + \rho_2c_T^2 \;=\; \frac{\rho_1^2v_1^2}{\rho_2} + \rho_2c_T^2. $$
Writing $r = \rho_2/\rho_1$ and dividing by $\rho_1c_T^2$ gives $\mathcal{M}^2 + 1 = \mathcal{M}^2/r + r$, whose roots are $r = 1$ and $r = \mathcal{M}^2$. ∎
336: The numbers are startling and they matter for the rest of the book. Molecular hydrogen at $10\ \mathrm{K}$ with $\mu = 2.33$ has $c_T = 0.189\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. A $10\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ flow — a very ordinary speed in the interstellar medium, far below anything a supernova produces — is then at $\mathcal{M} = 52.9$ and compresses the gas by a factor $2802$. Put the same speed through an adiabatic shock and it is at $M_1 = \mathcal{M}/\sqrt\gamma = 41.0$, because the adiabatic sound speed in the same gas is $\sqrt\gamma\,c_T = 0.244\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, and it compresses by $3.993$. That is three orders of magnitude, and it is why the dense filaments in which Module 5 's Jeans criterion is actually met are made by shocks and not by gravity alone. The whole difference is one closure: whether the layer keeps its heat or radiates it away. ($\gamma = 5/3$ and not $7/5$ is right for H₂ at $10\ \mathrm{K}$: the rotational levels of the molecule have a characteristic temperature near $85\ \mathrm{K}$ and are frozen out at $10\ \mathrm{K}$, leaving only the three translational degrees of freedom.)
340: 4. The Sedov–Taylor blast wave
342: Deposit energy $E$ at a point at $t = 0$, in a uniform gas of density $\rho_0$ and negligible pressure, and follow the shock that runs outward. The problem is the cleanest use in this book of an argument that needs no equations at all.
344: Proposition 5 (the exponent, from dimensions alone). If the flow depends on no quantity other than $E$, $\rho_0$ and $t$, then the shock radius is
$$ R(t) \;=\; \xi_0\left(\frac{E\,t^2}{\rho_0}\right)^{1/5}, \tag{4.1}$$
with $\xi_0$ a dimensionless number. The exponent $2/5$ is forced , and no property of the gas enters it.
348: In the dimensions $[\text{mass}]$, $[\text{length}]$, $[\text{time}]$, $E$ has dimensions $\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}$, $\rho_0$ has $\mathrm{M\,L^{-3}}$ and $t$ has $\mathrm{T}$. Seek $R = E^a\rho_0^b t^c$ with $\mathrm{L}$ on the left: matching mass gives $a + b = 0$; matching time gives $-2a + c = 0$; matching length gives $2a - 3b = 1$. The first and third give $a = 1/5$, $b = -1/5$, and the second then gives $c = 2/5$. The solution is unique, so (4.1) is the only possible form. ∎
350: Three consequences follow immediately and none needs the interior solution. The shock speed is $v_s = \mathrm{d}R/\mathrm{d}t = \tfrac25 R/t \propto t^{-3/5}$, so the blast decelerates; by Proposition 7 below, which uses nothing from this section, the post-shock temperature goes as $v_s^2 \propto t^{-6/5}$; and, because $R^5t^{-2} = \xi_0^5E/\rho_0$ is a constant of the motion, a single measurement of $R$ at a single $t$ determines $E$ once $\xi_0$ and $\rho_0$ are known. Taylor used exactly that, and Problem K3 shows what it hides.
352: What dimensional analysis cannot supply is $\xi_0$. That number contains all the dynamics, and getting it requires solving the interior flow.
354: Proposition 6 (the similarity solution and the constant $\xi_0$). Write $\lambda = r/R(t)$ and
$$ v(r,t) = \frac{r}{t}\,u(\lambda), \qquad \rho(r,t) = \rho_0\,g(\lambda), \qquad p(r,t) = \rho_0\frac{r^2}{t^2}\,\Pi(\lambda). \tag{4.2}$$
Then the spherical Euler equations of Module 2 reduce to three ordinary differential equations in $\ln\lambda$, with boundary values at $\lambda = 1$ given by the strong-shock limit of Proposition 2,
$$ u(1) = \frac{4}{5(\gamma+1)}, \qquad g(1) = \frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}, \qquad \Pi(1) = \frac{8}{25(\gamma+1)}, \tag{4.3}$$
and the constant of (4.1) is fixed by the energy integral,
$$ 1 \;=\; 4\pi\,\xi_0^5\,J, \qquad J \;=\; \int_0^1 \lambda^4\left[\tfrac12 g u^2 + \frac{\Pi}{\gamma-1}\right]\mathrm{d}\lambda. \tag{4.4}$$
Numerically $\xi_0 = 1.151666$ for $\gamma = 5/3$ and $\xi_0 = 1.032777$ for $\gamma = 7/5$.
368: For (4.3): in the strong-shock limit Proposition 2 gives $\rho_2 = \rho_0(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$, $v_2 = D(\gamma-1)/(\gamma+1)$ and $p_2 = 2\rho_0D^2/(\gamma+1)$, with $D = \mathrm{d}R/\mathrm{d}t = \tfrac25R/t$ supplied by (4.1) itself. Change frame before using $v_2$. Definition 1 measures $v_2$ in the rest frame of the surface, while $v(r,t)$ in (4.2) is a laboratory-frame velocity, in the frame where the unshocked gas ahead is at rest. The two differ by $D$: the shocked gas moves outward at $v_{2,\rm lab} = D - v_2 = D[1 - (\gamma-1)/(\gamma+1)] = 2D/(\gamma+1)$, which is $D/2$ at $\gamma = 5/3$ while $v_2$ itself is $D/4$. Evaluating (4.2) at $r = R$ and dividing gives $u(1) = v_{2,\rm lab}\,t/R = \tfrac25\cdot 2/(\gamma+1)$, $g(1) = \rho_2/\rho_0$ and $\Pi(1) = p_2/[\rho_0(R/t)^2] = 2(4/25)/(\gamma+1)$, which are (4.3). So §4 introduces no new physical input : the boundary condition of the blast wave is Proposition 2 plus similarity.
370: For (4.4): the total energy inside the shock is
$$ E = \int_0^R\left[\tfrac12\rho v^2 + \frac{p}{\gamma-1}\right]4\pi r^2\,\mathrm{d}r = 4\pi\rho_0\frac{R^5}{t^2}\,J $$
after substituting (4.2) and $r = \lambda R$. But $R^5/t^2 = \xi_0^5E/\rho_0$ by (4.1), so $E$ cancels from both sides and what is left is $1 = 4\pi\xi_0^5J$. The energy integral does not determine the energy; it determines the constant. ∎
377: Two independent verifications are worth stating, because a constant obtained by numerical integration of an eliminated system is exactly the sort of number that can be wrong without looking wrong.
379: First, internal. The elimination that produced $u'$ used two of the three equations and discarded them. Differentiating the integrated solution numerically and substituting it back into the discarded continuity and entropy equations gives residuals no larger than $7\times10^{-9}$ anywhere between $\lambda = 0.1$ and $\lambda = 0.9$, for both values of $\gamma$. That tests the algebra, not merely the convergence of the integrator.
381: Second, external. Tang & Chevalier (2017) state that for $\gamma = 5/3$ the constant in $R = (\xi Et^2/\rho)^{1/5}$ equals $2.026$, citing Taylor (1946), Sedov (1959) and Book (1994); that is $\xi_0 = 2.026^{1/5} = 1.151670$ against $1.151666$ computed here, a ratio of $0.999997$. Kamm & Timmes (2007) define their spherical standard problem by $\rho_0 = 1\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $\gamma = 1.4$ and $E = 0.851072\ \mathrm{erg}$, which puts the shock at $r = 1.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ at $t = 1.0\ \mathrm{s}$; that statement is the constant, $\xi_0 = 0.851072^{-1/5} = 1.032777$, and it agrees to all six figures.
383: A third comparison, and the one that matters for §5 . Taylor obtained $K \equiv 1/\xi_0^5$ for $\gamma = 1.40$ in 1941 by hand integration and printed $K = 0.856$. The exact value is $0.851072$. His approximate quadratures were therefore good to 0.58 per cent — and because $E$ is linear in $K$, that error propagates to his energy as itself, which is small compared with everything else that will go wrong in §5 .
385: The interior of the solution is as instructive as the constant. Nearly all the shocked mass sits in a thin shell just behind the front: for $\gamma = 5/3$ the mass median radius is $r/R = 0.9392$, the density at $r/R = 0.5$ has fallen to $1.53$ per cent of its post-shock value, and the pressure has flattened to a plateau at $0.306$ of the post-shock pressure and stays there all the way in. A hot, nearly empty, nearly uniform-pressure interior driving a thin dense shell — that is the picture, and §7 shows it becoming literally true as the snowplough phase arrives.

The confirmation and the refutation are one set of twenty-five numbers plotted twice, and only the second plot shows the structure.
552: 5. The anchor: Trinity, 16 July 1945
554: Every module in this book is checked against a published measurement, and the checks have been of uneven quality. Module 3 's solar comparison was partly circular, because the model it tested had gone into the model that produced the "measurement". Module 7 's laboratory check carried a standard deviation but its astronomical half carried none. This module's anchor has neither defect, and it is worth saying why before any number appears.

634: 6. The astrophysical check: Voyager 2 inside a shock
636: Every shock in this book so far has been inferred from a distance. In 2007 a spacecraft flew through one and measured it from the inside. Voyager 2's plasma experiment recorded a decrease in solar-wind speed from about 9 June 2007, culminating in several crossings of the solar-wind termination shock at a heliocentric radius of 84 AU between 30 August and 1 September 2007.
638: The paper reporting it supplies something rarer than the crossing itself: a control . The same instrument on the same spacecraft crossed Neptune's inbound bow shock in August 1989, eighteen years earlier, at 30 AU. So the comparison below is not merely theory against observation; it is one shock against another, through one instrument, with the theory saying what should differ and what should not.
640: 6.1 CONFIRMED: the ceiling, and a consistency test with no free parameter
642: From the figure caption comparing the two crossings, read directly: at Neptune's bow shock the solar-wind speed fell by a factor of four and the density increased by a factor of four, while the temperature increased by a factor of 100.
644: Fig. 4 draws all five comparisons of this section on one logarithmic axis, as measured divided by predicted, so that a confirmation and a refutation are the same kind of mark in the same place.
646: The factor of four in density is the ceiling (3.3) for $\gamma = 5/3$, and $\gamma = 5/3$ is not fitted — it is fixed by the gas being monatomic. Ratio of measured to predicted: $1.0$. That the density jump and the speed drop are the same number is mass conservation, (2.2), holding to the precision of the read — provided the bow shock is standing in the frame the speeds were measured in, which a planetary bow shock does to that precision, because (2.2) is a statement in the rest frame of the surface and the speeds are measured in the spacecraft's.
648: The temperature supplies a second, independent test of the same shock. A temperature ratio of 100 implies, by inverting (3.2) at $\gamma = 5/3$, an upstream Mach number $M_1 = 17.8$. That Mach number then predicts a density ratio of $3.96$ through the first of (3.1), against the measured 4. Consistency ratio $0.99$. One Mach number, two independent Rankine–Hugoniot relations, one answer: that is the shape of a real test, and it is what Proposition 2 is for.
650: Check 4, CONFIRMED. At Neptune's bow shock the measured density jump is the compression ceiling $(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1) = 4$ for a monatomic gas, with $\gamma$ fixed by the gas and not fitted; the measured speed drop is the same factor, as (2.2) requires; and the measured temperature jump, run back through (3.2) to a Mach number and forward again through (3.1), returns the density jump to $0.99$. Every input is read to one significant figure from a figure caption that quotes no uncertainty, so these ratios are claimed to two figures and no further.
654: 6.2 REFUTED: the closure, not the conservation laws
656: At the termination shock the same instrument found the temperature increasing by a factor of only ten and the density by only two. The authors state the consequence plainly: if all the flow energy were transferred to the thermal ions, as happens at planetary bow shocks, the heliosheath temperature would be $10^6$ K, whereas the observed temperature is $10^5$ K.
658: That $10^6$ K is not taken on trust; Proposition 7 reproduces it. The same paper reports the solar wind decelerating "from 400 to 300 km s$^{-1}$ in three steps" ahead of the crossing. Putting $v_s = 300\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and $\mu = 0.5$, for a fully ionised hydrogen plasma, into (7.1) at $\gamma = 5/3$ gives $T_2 = 1.01\times10^{6}$ K, which is their figure to within 2 per cent. So Check 5 below is this book's prediction against their measurement and not their prediction against their measurement.
662: Check 5, REFUTED. Observed over predicted, $10^5/10^6 = \mathbf{0.10}$: a factor of ten short. What fails is the closure — one fluid, one temperature — and not the conservation laws of Proposition 1.
664: The authors' explanation is what makes this a refutation of a specific hypothesis rather than a puzzle. The energy per proton falls by 80 per cent across the shock, and it goes to pickup ions — hot protons created when neutral interstellar atoms are ionised inside the heliosphere — rather than to the thermal plasma. A single-fluid ideal gas has nowhere to put that energy, so it predicts a hot thermal population that is not there. And Module 1 has already said why this is possible: the solar wind at 84 AU is collisionless, so no mechanism forces one temperature on all the ion populations. The failure was licensed by Module 1 and is observed here.

The table's caption states that the shock orientation is found as "the direction that minimizes the differences of the Rankine–Hugoniot conditions across the shock", and that the shock speed, the field angle and the Mach numbers are then calculated from that orientation. 732: 7. A supernova remnant through its phases
734: The Sedov solution is exact for a point explosion in a pressureless uniform gas, and a supernova remnant is neither at the start nor at the end of its life. This section puts brackets around the interval in which (4.1) is the right description, and the brackets are as much a result as the solution is.
736: Take the standard numbers: $E = 10^{51}$ erg, ambient hydrogen density $n_{\rm H} = 1\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, so $\rho_0 = 1.4\,m_u n_{\rm H} = 2.325\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ with helium at cosmic abundance, $\mu = 0.61$ for the fully ionised gas behind the shock, and $\gamma = 5/3$.
738: Proposition 7 (the post-shock temperature of a strong shock). In the limit $M_1 \gg 1$,
$$ T_2 \;=\; \frac{2(\gamma-1)}{(\gamma+1)^2}\,\frac{\mu m_u v_s^2}{k_B}, \tag{7.1}$$
which for $\gamma = 5/3$ is $T_2 = 3\mu m_u v_s^2/(16 k_B)$.
742: In the strong-shock limit Proposition 2 gives $p_2 = 2\rho_1v_s^2/(\gamma+1)$ and $\rho_2 = \rho_1(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$. The ideal gas law in the form $T = \mu m_u p/(k_B\rho)$ then gives
$$ T_2 = \frac{\mu m_u}{k_B}\frac{p_2}{\rho_2} = \frac{\mu m_u}{k_B}\cdot\frac{2\rho_1v_s^2}{\gamma+1}\cdot\frac{\gamma-1}{\rho_1(\gamma+1)}, $$
which is (7.1). At $\gamma = 5/3$ the prefactor is $2(2/3)/(64/9) = 3/16$. ∎

796: 8. The debt from Module 7: Rayleigh–Taylor at Tycho
798: Module 7 closed by recording a gap: its Rayleigh–Taylor half was checked against a laser experiment and against nothing in the sky, and no module from 1 to 10 was going to repay it. This section repays it, using the structure that §7 has just built, and the answer is a refutation .
800: A young supernova remnant is not one shock but three surfaces. The ejecta drive a forward shock (the blast wave) into the interstellar medium. The ambient gas pushes back on the ejecta through a reverse shock running into them. Between the two lies the contact discontinuity , separating shocked ejecta from shocked ambient gas. Shocked ejecta are the heavier fluid and sit inside; shocked ambient gas is lighter and sits outside; and the shell decelerates. In the frame of the decelerating interface that is exactly Module 7's unstable ordering, and the contact discontinuity grows Rayleigh–Taylor fingers.
828: The double-shocked shell of a young remnant, drawn as a quarter section. The mushrooms on the contact discontinuity are Rayleigh–Taylor fingers of ejecta reaching towards the blast wave. The quantity this section tests is their length — how far beyond the smooth one-dimensional contact discontinuity they reach — and not the position of any one surface.
831: 8.1 The comparison needs no distance and no age
833: Module 7 measured a mixing law, $h = \alpha A g t^2$, for a plane interface under a constant acceleration, and derived $g = m(1-m)R/t^2$ for a shell whose radius follows $R \propto t^m$. Putting the second into the first is the whole of this section's theory.
835: What Proposition 9 produces is a bound rather than a value, and that is what makes §8 a test and not a fit: if the observed width exceeds the bound, no choice of the two free quantities can rescue the prediction.
837: Proposition 9 (the mixing width of a decelerating shell). For a shell with $R \propto t^m$ whose interface carries Atwood number $A$ and mixing coefficient $\alpha$,
$$ h \;=\; \alpha A\, m(1-m)\, R_{\rm cd}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{h}{R_{\rm BW}} \;=\; \alpha\,A\,m(1-m)\,\frac{R_{\rm cd}}{R_{\rm BW}}. \tag{8.1}$$
Both $t$ and $R$ cancel. Moreover $A \le 1$ by its definition and $m(1-m) \le \tfrac14$ with equality at $m = \tfrac12$, so
$$ \frac{h}{R_{\rm BW}} \;\le\; \frac{\alpha}{4}\,\frac{R_{\rm cd}}{R_{\rm BW}} \tag{8.2}$$
for every shell, whatever its Atwood number and whatever its expansion index.
843: Substitute $g = m(1-m)R_{\rm cd}/t^2$ into $h = \alpha Agt^2$; the $t^2$ cancels identically, leaving $h = \alpha A m(1-m)R_{\rm cd}$, and dividing by $R_{\rm BW}$ gives (8.1). For (8.2): $A = (\rho_2-\rho_1)/(\rho_1+\rho_2) \le 1$ because both densities are positive, and $m(1-m)$ is a downward parabola in $m$ with maximum $\tfrac14$ at $m = \tfrac12$. ∎
845: Two consequences are worth stating before any number is put in. No distance and no age are needed : (8.1) is a ratio of radii, so the disagreement among published distances to Tycho — which is real — cannot affect it, and neither can the epoch at which the remnant is observed. And (8.2) turns the comparison into a test rather than a fit: if the observed width exceeds $\alpha/4$ times the radius ratio, no choice of $A$ or $m$ can rescue the prediction.

942: 8.3 What is refuted, and what is not
944: Refuted: the transfer of a plane-interface, constant-acceleration, two-uniform-fluid, forgotten-seed mixing law to a decelerating supernova shell. Not refuted: the law itself, which Module 7 checked in the regime in which it was measured and which is not under test here.
946: Three reasons the transfer fails, in order of size, and the module claims no more precision for them than it has:
949: $\alpha_B$ is the bubble coefficient. The observed quantity is a spike penetration — ejecta reaching outward into lighter gas — and at Atwood number near 1 spikes outrun bubbles, because a dense spike falling through a light fluid feels little drag while a light bubble rising through a dense one feels a great deal. The direction is certain. No number for $\alpha_{\rm spike}$ is printed anywhere in this module, because no paper this book holds gives one. That makes the $0.0073$ a lower bound on the prediction and the factor 11 an upper bound on the discrepancy.
950: The seed is not forgotten. The mixing law assumes the initial conditions are lost after about three merger generations — Module 7 quotes the assumption in the experimenters' own words. At a remnant's contact discontinuity the reverse shock feeds the interface continuously, and Wang & Chevalier find that reproducing Tycho's observed protrusions at all requires ejecta clumps with a density contrast of at least 100 over their surroundings. A structure that needs a seed of contrast 100 is not a structure that has forgotten its seed.
951: $g$ is not constant. $g = m(1-m)R/t^2$ falls as $t^{m-2}$, and $\iint g\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}t$ taken from $t = 0$ does not converge, so the coefficient in (8.1) depends on when the deceleration began. (8.1) is therefore a scaling, good to a factor of a few , and not a theorem applied. It is quoted that way here, which is why a factor 11 counts as a refutation and a factor 2 would not have.
954: One caveat that pushes the same way, and must be read before the 0.93 is used for anything. Warren et al. conclude that the measured ratio $0.93$ is "inconsistent with adiabatic hydrodynamical models, even when Rayleigh–Taylor instabilities are taken into account", and they explain the unexpectedly small gap by cosmic-ray acceleration at the forward shock, which raises the compression factor above 4 and pulls the blast wave inward towards the contact discontinuity. So part of the observed 0.16 is not Rayleigh–Taylor mixing at all. Nothing in Check 7 rests on the 0.93: taking their reading at face value and using only the pure-hydrodynamic simulation still leaves a factor 11.
958: What this section does not use, and why. Warren's strongest single azimuthal mode of the contact discontinuity, $n = 6$, has an amplitude of 3.3 per cent of the mean radius — a tempting comparator, and the right order of magnitude to make the prediction look better. It is refused, because the same sentence records that "the BW shows nearly the same value", and the blast wave is not Rayleigh–Taylor unstable. A number that both surfaces share cannot be measuring an instability that only one of them has. What is specific to the contact discontinuity is the high-wavenumber power, where it carries nearly an order of magnitude more than the blast wave, and its spectral slope, $P \propto k^{-1.5}$ against $k^{-2.2}$. Those are qualitative, and they are quoted as qualitative.
960: This changes Module 7 . Module 7 §10 used to record that its Rayleigh–Taylor half had no astronomical check and that Modules 1–10 would not repay it. That sentence has been replaced: Module 7 now points here, and states the verdict before the reader arrives — the check, once made, refuted the transfer rather than confirming the law.
962: 9. What this model captures, and what it misses
964: Every idealisation used above is named here, with the module that repays it where one exists and a plain statement of "not repaid in this book" where none does.
967: Zero thickness. The layer has structure — viscous stress, heat conduction, ionisation — and none of it appears in Proposition 1. Problem D2 bounds the error in air at eight orders of magnitude and finds the opposite verdict at the termination shock, where the layer is $10^5$–$10^6$ times thinner than a collisional mean free path. Not repaid: this book never solves the internal structure of a shock.
968: One fluid, one temperature. Refuted in §6.2 by the measurement itself. The electrons and the ions need not share a temperature, and at the termination shock a third population — pickup ions — takes 80 per cent of the energy. Module 1 supplies the criterion that says when this is allowed to happen; no module in this book carries a two-fluid or three-fluid shock.
969: Constant $\gamma$ across the front. Broken at Trinity, by Taylor's own account and in the direction his own argument predicts. Not repaid: a variable-$\gamma$ equation of state is beyond this book.
970: No magnetic field. Proposition 1 has no field, and §6.3 refuses a comparison for exactly that reason. Module 12 builds magnetohydrodynamics but stops short of oblique shock jump conditions, so this one is not repaid either , and the refusal in §6.3 is permanent rather than provisional.
971: A point explosion in a uniform, pressureless medium. Every word of that is idealisation. Finite source size and residual energy in radiation show up at $t = 0.10$ ms at Trinity; the neglected ambient pressure shows up after 5 ms; and the uniform medium is the assumption a circumstellar wind would break, which Tang & Chevalier treat with a density profile $\rho \propto r^{-s}$ that this module does not carry.
972: No radiative losses during the Sedov phase. That is precisely the assumption whose failure defines the end of the phase, in Proposition 8, and it is handled by bracketing rather than by correction.
973: A stated cooling function. (7.2) is two power laws, not a computed collisional-ionisation-equilibrium curve, and §7.1 shows the consequence: the transition radius is 57 per cent too large and the transition time a factor 3.1 too late.
974: Spherical symmetry. Dropped explicitly in §8 , where the interface is unstable and the fingers are the subject. Everywhere else in the module it is assumed and never tested.
975: The mixing law transferred from a plane interface. Refuted in §8 , by a factor 11, with the three reasons stated and no claim to have found the right law.
978: One thing this module does not miss is worth recording, because it is unusual. The check in §5 is against a table that no model touched, and the check in §6 is against an instrument that flew through the object. Neither has the circularity that Module 3 had to declare. What they have instead is a shortage of quoted uncertainties, and that is why §5.3 leans on Selby's $\sigma$ — the only honest standard deviation in the whole Trinity comparison.

1012: Solution
1013: Air. Adding viscosity gives the layer a width of a few mean free paths. In sea-level air $\lambda_{\rm mfp} \sim 6.5\times10^{-6}$ cm, so the layer is $\sim 2\times10^{-5}$ cm. 1014: The termination shock. The measured layer widths were 100 000–300 000 km, described by the observers as "a few times the ion inertial length", so $10^{10}$–$3\times10^{10}$ cm. The collisional mean free path for proton–proton Coulomb collisions, $\lambda_{\rm mfp} = (k_BT)^2/(\pi n e^4\ln\Lambda)$ with $n \approx 2\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $T \approx 10^5$ K read approximately off the published figure axes and $\ln\Lambda = 25$, is $2.28\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{cm} = 1524$ AU.
1015: The layer is therefore $8\times10^{5}$ to $2\times10^{6}$ times thinner than a mean free path .
