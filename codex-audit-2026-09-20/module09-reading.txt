120: 1. One equation, two flows
122: Module 3 put a star in hydrostatic balance and left it there. Module 2 derived the equation that governs what happens when the balance fails, and applied it to a flow whose radial structure was measured rather than predicted. This module does the thing neither of them did: it takes the steady spherical flow seriously as an equation to be solved, discovers that the equation has a singular point, and finds that almost everything worth knowing about stellar winds and about accretion onto compact objects follows from the behaviour of solutions near that point.
124: The claim in the title is literal and is worth stating before any algebra. Write continuity and Euler for a steady, spherically symmetric flow. Eliminate the density. What is left is one ordinary differential equation for the speed $v(r)$, and that equation contains no information about which way the gas is going: $v$ enters it through $v^2$ and through $\mathrm{d}\ln v/\mathrm{d}\ln r$, and a solution with $v \gt 0$ everywhere maps onto a solution with $v \lt 0$ everywhere. Take the outward branch and it is E. N. Parker's solar wind, published in 1958. Take the inward branch and it is H. Bondi's spherical accretion flow, published in 1952. They are the same calculation. Fig. 1 is that sentence drawn.
145: The module in one picture. One central mass, one steady spherically symmetric flow, one differential equation — and two arrows. The dashed circle is the critical radius, the single place where a solution may pass from subsonic to supersonic. Reverse the arrow and Parker's problem becomes Bondi's; the equation is unchanged, and so is the critical radius. Everything after §3 is a consequence of what happens on that dashed circle.
148: The singular point is the reason this is not a routine exercise. At one particular radius the coefficient of $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}r$ vanishes, and unless the right-hand side vanishes there too, the solution has infinite gradient and stops existing. That is a strong constraint, and §3 shows it selects exactly one value of an integration constant out of a continuum. The solution that satisfies it is the only one that starts subsonic at a stellar surface and ends supersonic at infinity — and a wind that does not end supersonic decelerates as $v \propto r^{-2}$, which is a qualitatively different object with a qualitatively different signature in the data. §7 uses exactly that: the sign of a measured velocity exponent separates the two, with no fitting.
150: The module then does what this book always does, and asks whether the calculation describes anything. It asks twice, at the two extremes of the mass range, and gets two different kinds of answer.
152: The anchor is the solar wind , §7 . Venzmer & Bothmer (2018) fitted four power laws to the combined Helios 1 and 2 data between $0.29$ and $0.98$ au, each exponent fitted independently of the others and with no fluid equation imposed on the fit. Those four numbers confirm two predictions of this module and refute a third, and the sharpest of them is not a confirmation or a refutation at all but a measured shortfall: steady Euler, fed the measured pressure gradient and given no wind model whatever, accounts for about one third of the acceleration the wind is observed to have at 1 au. The same table was used in Module 2 §10 to confirm an exact conservation law and refute the adiabatic closure. §7.1 says in print what is new here and what is not, because a reader is entitled to ask whether the book is checking one table twice.
154: The astrophysical check is Sagittarius A* , §8 , and it produces the largest ratio in the book so far. The Bondi rate, computed from a measured black-hole mass and Chandra's measured gas density and temperature, exceeds the rate that Faraday rotation permits by a factor of $40$ — and the module then spends as much space on the conditions attached to that $40$ as on the number itself, because one of them cuts it to $4$. Two further comparisons, which do not depend on those conditions, survive at factors of $401$ to $4011$ and $2\times10^{7}$. The refutation is of the physical premises, not of the algebra.
156: One thing must be said at the outset, because the module's first draft got it wrong and the correction is instructive. It is often written that the steady equations determine the accretion rate, and that the coefficient Bondi computed is an eigenvalue forced by the mathematics. Bondi says the opposite, in his own summary, and §5 quotes him. The steady equations bound the rate; choosing the transonic solution is a physical argument about which flow nature realises, and it is his argument rather than a theorem. That distinction does not weaken §8 — two of its three legs never touch the coefficient — but a refutation built on a misattributed theorem would not be worth having.
158: 2. Why a static corona is impossible
160: Parker's 1958 paper does not begin with a wind. It begins by assuming there is none, and showing that the assumption cannot be maintained. The argument occupies his Sect. II, it needs nothing beyond Module 3 , and it is the cleanest demonstration in this book that an equilibrium can be internally consistent and still be impossible.
162: Definition 1 (the configuration). A single fluid, spherically symmetric about a central mass $M$, with no rotation, no magnetic field, no heat source and no radiation force. The gas is ideal, with pressure $P = n_{\rm tot} k_{\rm B} T$ and density $\rho = \mu m_{\rm u} n_{\rm tot}$, where $n_{\rm tot}$ counts all free particles, electrons included, and $\mu$ is the mean mass per particle in units of the unified atomic mass unit $m_{\rm u}$. For the solar wind this module takes a helium-to-hydrogen ratio $y = n_{\rm He}/n_{\rm H} = 0.05$ by number with both species fully ionised, so that
$$ \rho = n_{\rm H} m_{\rm p}(1+4y), \qquad n_{\rm tot} = n_{\rm H}(2+3y), \qquad \mu = \frac{m_{\rm p}}{m_{\rm u}}\,\frac{1+4y}{2+3y} = 0.56220. \tag{2.1}$$
The base of the corona is placed at $r_0 = 1.03\,R_\odot$, where the Baumbach–Allen electron density of Source 6 is evaluated. All flow speeds are radial, $v \gt 0$ outward and $v \lt 0$ inward, and $\dot M \equiv 4\pi r^2 \rho |v|$ is quoted as a positive rate with its direction stated in words.
168: Proposition 1 (a static corona has a floor pressure at infinity). For the configuration of Definition 1 with $v = 0$ and $T$ constant, hydrostatic balance $\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}r = -GM\rho/r^2$ integrates to
$$ \frac{P(r)}{P(r_0)} \;=\; \exp\!\left[-\frac{GM\mu m_{\rm u}}{k_{\rm B}T}\left(\frac{1}{r_0}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\right], \tag{2.2}$$
and because $1/r \to 0$ rather than $-\infty$, the limit
$$ \frac{P(\infty)}{P(r_0)} \;=\; \exp\!\left[-\frac{GM\mu m_{\rm u}}{k_{\rm B}T r_0}\right] \tag{2.3}$$
is finite and strictly positive . A hydrostatic isothermal atmosphere in an inverse-square field does not run out of pressure.
174: Divide the balance by $P = \rho k_{\rm B}T/(\mu m_{\rm u})$ at constant $T$:
$$ \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln P}{\mathrm{d}r} \;=\; -\frac{GM\mu m_{\rm u}}{k_{\rm B}T}\,\frac{1}{r^2}. \tag{2.4}$$
Integrate from $r_0$ to $r$; the antiderivative of $-r^{-2}$ is $+r^{-1}$, which is bounded above as $r\to\infty$. Hence (2.2), and (2.3) on taking the limit. The exponent is finite because gravity, not the atmosphere, supplies the only scale, and $\int_{r_0}^{\infty}r^{-2}\,\mathrm{d}r = 1/r_0$ converges. ∎
180: Contrast this with the plane-parallel isothermal atmosphere of Module 3 Proposition 3, where $g$ is constant and $P \propto \mathrm{e}^{-z/H}$ falls to zero. The difference is entirely the geometry: $\int^\infty g\,\mathrm{d}z$ diverges in a uniform field and converges in an inverse-square one. That convergence is the whole argument. An atmosphere whose pressure tends to zero can sit in a vacuum forever; an atmosphere whose pressure tends to a positive constant needs something outside it pushing back with at least that much pressure, and if nothing does, it is not in equilibrium at all.

202: 2.2 Parker's own version of the same argument
204: Parker made this calculation in 1958 with different inputs, and the comparison is worth printing — not as a test of nature, but as a check that a 68-year-old argument has been reconstructed and not merely cited. His corona is not isothermal: it conducts, and its temperature declines outward, which makes his pressure fall faster than (2.2). From $N_0 = 3\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $T_0 = 1.5\times10^{6}$ K he gets $p_0 = 2N_0k_{\rm B}T_0 \approx 1.3\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$ and $p(\infty) = 0.55\times10^{-3}p_0 = 0.6\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$, against an interstellar pressure he takes as $1.4\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$ from "10 hydrogen atoms/cm³ at 100° K". His ratio is $4.3\times10^{7}$.
206: Check 1 — a consistency check, and not a check against nature. This module's $2.358\times10^{8}$ against Parker's $4.3\times10^{7}$: a factor $5.5$, and the two reasons for it are both stated by him. His corona conducts and cools outward, which lowers $p(\infty)$; and his assumed interstellar pressure is ten times lower than the one used here, which raises the ratio. Same argument, same conclusion, independently arrived at. Nothing is confirmed about the Sun by this comparison — it confirms only that the argument has been reproduced.
208: His conclusion, in his own words, is the sentence this module rebuilds: "Since we know of no general pressure at infinity which could balance the $p(\infty)$ computed from equation (9) with the expected values of $n$, we conclude that probably it is not possible for the solar corona, or, indeed, perhaps the atmosphere of any star, to be in complete hydrostatic equilibrium out to large distances."
210: 3. The critical point, and the six things a flow may do
212: The corona must move. The question is how, and the answer is more constrained than it has any right to be.
214: Definition 2 (the isothermal sound speed and the critical radius). For the configuration of Definition 1 at constant temperature,
$$ c_T \;\equiv\; \sqrt{\frac{k_{\rm B}T}{\mu m_{\rm u}}} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{P}{\rho}}, \qquad r_c \;\equiv\; \frac{GM}{2c_T^2}, \tag{3.1}$$
and the dimensionless variables are $x \equiv r/r_c$ and $u \equiv v/c_T$. $c_T$ is the isothermal sound speed and is written $c_T$ throughout , never $a$: $a$ is reserved elsewhere in this book for the cosmological expansion factor and does not appear in this module. The adiabatic sound speed of Module 4 is written $c$ and appears from §4 .
218: Note what $r_c$ is not. It is not put in by hand and it is not a fitting parameter: it is the radius at which the escape speed $\sqrt{2GM/r}$ equals $2c_T$, equivalently where $c_T^2 = GM/(2r)$, and it is fixed the moment $T$ is fixed. Problem C1 checks that identity numerically.
220: Proposition 2 (the critical-point equation, and its exact first integral). For a steady, spherically symmetric, isothermal flow of the configuration of Definition 1, continuity and Euler reduce to
$$ \left(u-\frac{1}{u}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x} \;=\; \frac{2}{x}-\frac{2}{x^2}, \tag{3.2}$$
whose left side vanishes only at $u = 1$ and whose right side vanishes only at $x = 1$. A solution may therefore pass through $u = 1$ only at $x = 1$ , and (3.2) integrates exactly to
$$ u^2 - 2\ln u \;=\; 4\ln x + \frac{4}{x} + C \tag{3.3}$$
with $C$ an integration constant that labels the solution.
226: Steady continuity is $4\pi r^2\rho v = \dot M$ constant, so $\mathrm{d}\ln\rho/\mathrm{d}r = -2/r - \mathrm{d}\ln v/\mathrm{d}r$. Steady radial Euler is
$$ v\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}r} \;=\; -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}r}-\frac{GM}{r^2} \;=\; -c_T^2\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln\rho}{\mathrm{d}r}-\frac{GM}{r^2}, \tag{3.4}$$
the second equality using $P = c_T^2\rho$ at constant $T$. Substituting the continuity relation for $\mathrm{d}\ln\rho/\mathrm{d}r$ gives
$$ \left(v-\frac{c_T^2}{v}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}r} \;=\; \frac{2c_T^2}{r}-\frac{GM}{r^2}, \tag{3.5}$$
which is the equation written under Fig. 1. Divide by $c_T^2/r_c$ and use $r_c = GM/2c_T^2$ to reach (3.2). Multiply (3.2) by $2$ and integrate term by term: $\int(2u-2/u)\,\mathrm{d}u = u^2-2\ln u$ and $\int(4/x-4/x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = 4\ln x+4/x$, giving (3.3). ∎
234: Equation (3.5) shows the mechanism directly and is worth reading before the dimensionless form. The bracket on the left changes sign at $v = c_T$. The right side changes sign at $r = GM/2c_T^2 = r_c$. If the two sign changes do not occur at the same place, $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}r$ is infinite where the bracket vanishes and the solution terminates. There is no freedom here: a flow that is to go from subsonic to supersonic must do it at $r_c$ and nowhere else.
238: $C = -3$: the two branches cross at the critical point. Following the subsonic branch in and the supersonic branch out gives the transonic wind ; following them the other way gives the transonic accretion solution.
239: $C \gt -3$: $R(x) \gt 1$ everywhere, so $h(u) \gt 1$ everywhere and $u$ never reaches 1. Two branches that never meet: the subsonic breeze , and the supersonic-everywhere solution.
240: $C \lt -3$: $R(x) \lt 1$ in a band about $x = 1$, where no $u$ satisfies (3.3) at all. Two double-valued families, each existing only outside the forbidden band and turning back on itself at its edge.
244: $h'(u) = 2u-2/u$ vanishes only at $u = 1$ on $u \gt 0$, and $h''(1) = 2+2 \gt 0$, so the stationary point is a minimum and it is unique; $h(1) = 1-0 = 1$. $R'(x) = 4/x-4/x^2 = 4(x-1)/x^2$ vanishes only at $x = 1$, and $R''(1) = -4+8 = 4 \gt 0$, so again a unique minimum, with $R(1) = 0+4+C$. Since $h \ge 1$ always, (3.3) has a solution at a given $x$ only where $R(x) \ge 1$, that is where $4\ln x+4/x \ge 1-C$. Equality at the minimum requires $4+C = 1$, i.e. $C = -3$, and then it holds only at $x = 1$. The three cases follow by comparing $R(1) = 4+C$ with $\min h = 1$. ∎
248: A note on the names. These six families are often numbered I to VI, and that numbering is usually attributed to Parker. It is not his. Parker (1958) was fetched and read for this module, and it contains no such enumeration: he writes the equations, imposes the transonic condition, and plots $v(r)$. The numbering is a later textbook convention, and because nothing here rests on it the families are named in words and no numeral is cited to anyone.
305: Level sets of $u^2-2\ln u-4\ln x-4/x = C$, with $u = v/c_T$ and $x = r/r_c$. Both sides of (3.3) have a single minimum — at $u = 1$ and at $x = 1$ — so a curve reaches $u = 1$ only when $C = -3$, and then only at $x = 1$. That one value of $C$ gives the two transonic solutions: the wind, which leaves a subsonic base and is supersonic at infinity, and the accretion solution, which does the reverse. $C \gt -3$ gives the breeze and the supersonic-everywhere family, neither of which crosses the sonic line; $C \lt -3$ gives the two double-valued families, which stop before reaching it at the edge of a forbidden band. The classification is drawn rather than asserted, and the families are named in words: Parker (1958) contains no Roman numbering.
308: Fig. 2 draws (3.3) as a contour plot in the $(x,u)$ plane, which is the honest way to present it: the families are level sets of one function, not six separate constructions, and the classification is visible rather than asserted. The transonic pair is the saddle-crossing contour, and it is the only contour that passes through the corner point $(1,1)$.

322: A test with no fitting in it. A transonic wind has $\mathrm{d}\ln v/\mathrm{d}\ln r \gt 0$; a breeze has $\mathrm{d}\ln v/\mathrm{d}\ln r \to -2$; a static corona has $v = 0$ and no exponent at all. The sign of a measured velocity exponent separates them , and a sign needs no model to interpret. §7.3 measures it.
324: Do not turn the breeze asymptote into a significance. The measured exponent is $+0.049\pm0.010$ and the breeze asymptote is $-2$, so the measurement sits $2.05$ above it in a quantity whose formal error is $0.010$. Dividing those gives $205\sigma$, and that number would be meaningless. The $-2$ is the breeze's behaviour as $r\to\infty$ , and $0.29$–$0.98$ au is not the asymptotic region for any $r_c$ in the plausible range. What the comparison carries is the qualitative separation — breezes decelerate, winds accelerate — and the significance to quote is the one on $\beta \gt 0$, which §7.3 gives as $4.9\sigma$.
326: 4. The polytropic critical point, and the bound $\gamma \lt 3/2$
328: The isothermal assumption of §3 is a strong one and §7 refutes it. Before that, it is worth asking what survives without it, because the answer is a bound that the data can be held against — and a bound derived before any data is looked at is worth more than a fit made after.
330: Definition 3 (the polytropic flow). The configuration of Definition 1 with $P = K\rho^{\gamma}$ for constant $K$ and $\gamma$, so that the local sound speed is $c(r) = \sqrt{\gamma P/\rho}$, with $c_c$ its value at the critical point and $c_\infty$ its value at infinity. The isothermal case of §3 is $\gamma \to 1$, in which $c \to c_T$ is constant. The index $\gamma$ here is an effective one describing the run of $P$ against $\rho$ along the flow; it is not the ratio of specific heats of the gas, which is $5/3$ for the fully ionised hydrogen–helium mixture of (2.1) throughout.
332: Repeating the derivation of Proposition 2 with $P = K\rho^\gamma$ replaces $c_T^2$ by $c^2(r)$ in (3.5) and leaves the structure intact: the bracket still vanishes at $v = c$, the right side still vanishes where $2c^2/r = GM/r^2$, and the two must still coincide. What changes is that $c$ is now a function of position, so the critical radius is determined implicitly. The new content is in the slope there.
338: the product of the roots is negative if and only if $\gamma \lt 3/2$ , so one root is positive and an accelerating transonic solution — a wind — exists exactly on that range;
339: for $3/2 \lt \gamma \le 5/3$ both roots are negative: a transonic solution exists but decelerates outward, which is an accretion flow and not a wind;
340: the discriminant vanishes at $\gamma = 5/3$ , where the two roots coincide at $-1/2$, and the roots become complex only above $5/3$, where no transonic solution exists at all.
344: Write the polytropic form of (3.5) as $N(r,v)\,\mathrm{d}\ln v/\mathrm{d}\ln r = D(r,v)$ with $N = v^2-c^2$ and $D = 2c^2-GM/r$, both vanishing at the critical point. Near it the slope is $0/0$, so apply L'Hôpital along the solution: $s = (\mathrm{d}D/\mathrm{d}\ln r)/(\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}\ln r)$, where both derivatives are taken following the flow and therefore involve $s$ itself. Continuity and $P = K\rho^\gamma$ give $\mathrm{d}\ln c^2/\mathrm{d}\ln r = (\gamma-1)\,\mathrm{d}\ln\rho/\mathrm{d}\ln r = -(\gamma-1)(2+s)$. With $c^2 = GM/2r$ at the critical point, substituting and clearing denominators yields (4.1); the algebra is a quadratic in $s$ and no branch is discarded. The product of the roots of $as^2+bs+c$ is $c/a$, here $(4\gamma-6)/(\gamma+1)$, which is negative precisely when $4\gamma \lt 6$. The discriminant is $b^2-4ac = 16(\gamma-1)^2-4(\gamma+1)(4\gamma-6) = 4(10-6\gamma)$ after expansion, vanishing at $\gamma = 5/3$. At that value $s = -b/2a = -4(\gamma-1)/[2(\gamma+1)] = -1/2$, twice. ∎

365: The bound is fixed before any measurement is consulted, and that is what gives §7.2 its force. Nothing about the Sun entered (4.1); it is a statement about the local structure of a differential equation at a singular point. A gas whose effective index happened to exceed $3/2$ would have no accelerating transonic solution, and the exponents that Venzmer & Bothmer fitted had no reason whatever to respect that.
367: The solution can also be integrated numerically from the critical point outward, which tests the integrator rather than the physics. Taking $\gamma = 1.394$, $T_0 = 6.0$ MK at $r_0 = 1.03\,R_\odot$ — which places $r_c$ at $0.769\,R_\odot$ — and integrating to $3.00$ au gives $v$ rising from $352.2$ to $505.3\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. A real corona with those parameters would be supersonic from its base upward and could not launch this solution at all. The numbers are printed because they demonstrate that the integrator reproduces Proposition 4, and for no other reason. §7.5 takes the same $\gamma$ seriously as a solar model and shows what goes wrong.
371: 5. Bondi accretion: the same solution, run inward
373: Reverse the arrow in Fig. 1 . The gas is now at rest far away, at density $\rho_\infty$ and sound speed $c_\infty$, and falls in. Equations (3.5) and (4.1) are unchanged, and by Proposition 4 the relevant branch for $\gamma \gt 3/2$ — including the adiabatic $5/3$ — is a decelerating-outward, that is accelerating-inward, transonic solution. This is Bondi's 1952 problem.
375: Proposition 5 (the accretion rate, and what fixes its coefficient). For a polytropic flow with $1 \lt \gamma \lt 5/3$ falling from rest at infinity, Bernoulli evaluated at the critical point gives
$$ c_c^2 \;=\; \frac{2c_\infty^2}{5-3\gamma}, \qquad r_c \;=\; \frac{GM(5-3\gamma)}{4c_\infty^2}, \tag{5.1}$$
and with the Bondi radius defined as $R_B \equiv 2GM/c_\infty^2$,
$$ \frac{r_c}{R_B} \;=\; \frac{5-3\gamma}{8}. \tag{5.2}$$
Substituting (5.1) into $\dot M = 4\pi r_c^2\rho_c c_c$ gives
$$ \dot M \;=\; 4\pi\lambda\,\rho_\infty\,\frac{(GM)^2}{c_\infty^3}, \qquad \lambda_c(\gamma) \;=\; \left[\frac{5-3\gamma}{4}\right]^{2}\left[\frac{2}{5-3\gamma}\right]^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}}. \tag{5.3}$$ The steady equations do not fix $\lambda$. They bound it: $\lambda \le \lambda_c$.
383: Bernoulli for a steady polytropic flow is $v^2/2 + c^2/(\gamma-1) - GM/r = c_\infty^2/(\gamma-1)$, the constant evaluated at infinity where $v = 0$. At the critical point $v = c_c$ and $c_c^2 = GM/2r_c$, so $GM/r_c = 2c_c^2$ and the relation becomes $c_c^2/2 + c_c^2/(\gamma-1) - 2c_c^2 = c_\infty^2/(\gamma-1)$, which rearranges to $c_c^2(5-3\gamma) = 2c_\infty^2$, the first of (5.1); the second follows from $r_c = GM/2c_c^2$. Then (5.2) is immediate from the definition of $R_B$. For the rate, $\rho_c/\rho_\infty = (c_c^2/c_\infty^2)^{1/(\gamma-1)}$ from $P = K\rho^\gamma$, and substituting $r_c$, $\rho_c$ and $c_c$ into $\dot M = 4\pi r_c^2\rho_c c_c$ collects into (5.3). The inequality is Bondi's: his eq. (18) derives $\lambda_c$ as the largest value for which a solution exists, and his (19) states the rate "cannot exceed" the corresponding flux. ∎
387: Two checks on (5.2), one of them requiring no algebra. At $\gamma = 1$ the sound speed is constant, so the critical radius is $GM/2c_\infty^2$ while $R_B = 2GM/c_\infty^2$, a ratio of exactly $1/4$ — and $(5-3)/8 = 1/4$. At $\gamma = 5/3$ the ratio is zero: the adiabatic flow has no interior critical point at all , which is precisely why $\lambda$ is a limit there rather than a solution property, and why §8 's Sgr A* calculation quotes $\lambda = 1/4$ without a sonic radius.
391: What Bondi proved, in his own words, because this is easy to overstate. His Summary reads: "The factor of proportionality is not determined by the steady-state equations, though it is confined within certain limits. Arguments are given suggesting that the case physically most likely to occur is that with the maximum rate of accretion." His Sect. 4 derives $\lambda \le \lambda_c$ and his eq. (19) says the rate "cannot exceed" $4\pi\lambda_c(GM)^2c_\infty^{-3}\rho_\infty$. So (5.3) is an upper bound that the steady equations enforce, together with a physical argument — his, not a theorem — that the bound is attained. §8 is written so that its conclusion does not depend on that argument.

435: Proposition 7 (the mass-loss rate, and its sensitivity to $T_0$). For the transonic isothermal solution launched from $(r_0,\rho_0)$, $\dot M = 4\pi r_0^2\rho_0 v(r_0)$ with $v(r_0)$ obtained from (3.3). To leading order in $r_c/r_0 \gg 1$,

469: 7.1 What is new here, and what Module 2 already did
471: A reader who has come this far will notice that Module 2 §10 anchored on this same table, and is entitled to ask whether the book is checking one measurement twice and counting it as two results. It is not, and the distinction is worth setting out before any number is computed.
473: Module 2 used the density and velocity exponents to test continuity , which forces $\alpha-\beta = 2$ exactly for a steady spherical flow; the mean fits give $1.961$ and the median fits $2.035$, bracketing the prediction. It then used the density and temperature exponents to test the adiabatic closure , which predicts a temperature exponent of $-\alpha(\gamma-1) = -1.340$ against a measured $-0.792\pm0.028$, a $19.6\sigma$ refutation recorded in its §10.5 .
475: This module uses the same four numbers to test three different things: the critical-point bound $\gamma \lt 3/2$ of Proposition 4, which is a statement about a singular point and not about a closure; the sign of the velocity exponent, which distinguishes the solution families of Proposition 3; and the momentum equation itself, fed the measured pressure gradient, which neither earlier test touched. §7.4(i) does re-use Module 2's refutation of a closure, and it cites that result rather than re-deriving it.
477: One further caveat, inherited from Module 2 and not repaired here. The fits describe the mean or median of a distribution of streams at each radius — slow dense wind and fast tenuous wind mixed — not any single stream. Module 2's §10 set out the condition under which power-law exponents carry across such a mixture, namely radial self-similarity of the mixing weights, and the same condition is assumed here. It is an assumption, and §7.6 names it again where it bites hardest.

522: 7.5 Check 6 — the single-polytrope repair does not work either
524: The obvious repair is to abandon $\gamma = 1$ and use the measured $\gamma_{\rm eff} = 1.3940$ throughout. It fails, in three independent ways, and each one is computed rather than argued.
526: First, a polytropic wind with $\gamma \gt 1$ has a minimum launch temperature — below it the gas runs out of enthalpy before reaching the critical point — and it follows from Proposition 5 in three lines. Bernoulli for a base at rest fixes the constant $B = c_0^2/(\gamma-1) - GM/r_0$, with $c_0^2 = \gamma k_{\rm B}T_0/(\mu m_{\rm u})$. Evaluating the same constant at the critical point, where $v = c_c$ and $c_c^2 = GM/2r_c$, gives $B = c_c^2(5-3\gamma)/[2(\gamma-1)]$. For $1 \lt \gamma \lt 5/3$ that bracket is positive, so a critical point exists if and only if $B \gt 0$ : the gas must carry more enthalpy at the base than the potential well costs. That is $c_0^2/(\gamma-1) \gt GM/r_0$, or
530: with $T_{\rm ceiling} = 6.26$ MK from (6.2) — the minimum launch temperature and Proposition 8's ceiling are the same quantity, scaled by $2(\gamma-1)/\gamma$. At $r_0 = 1.03\,R_\odot$ that minimum is $T_0 \gt 3.540$ MK for $\gamma = 1.3940$, and $3.804$ MK for the median $1.4362$; the same formula gives $1.1385$ MK at the $\gamma = 1.1$ of Problem D2 . Both exceed the top of the measured 2–3 MK range , by factors of $1.18$ and $1.27$, and exceed its bottom by $1.77$ and $1.90$. The isothermal limit is the check on (7.4): as $\gamma \to 1$ the minimum goes to zero, which is right, because the isothermal wind never runs out of enthalpy.
532: Second, force the solution to reproduce the observed 1-au speed. With $\gamma = 1.3940$ that requires $T_0 = 5.39$ MK, and the resulting solution has a critical radius of $r_c = 1.020\,R_\odot$.
534: That critical radius lies below its own base. The model was launched from $r_0 = 1.03\,R_\odot$ and places its critical point at $1.020\,R_\odot$, inside it. The flow would be supersonic from the base upward, contradicting the premise — a subsonic base — on which the solution was constructed. No observation is needed to reject it. Second , (7.2) is nonlinear in $v$ and the quantities substituted into it are ensemble medians of a mixed flow, not properties of one stream. The honest statement is a measured shortfall. What supplies the rest — wave pressure, a turbulent cascade, or something else — is the question Module 2 §10.5 deferred to Module 10 on this same data set, and it is deferred again here.
652: And the composition does not rescue it. Venzmer & Bothmer treat the wind as a pure proton plasma — their Sect. 1, p. 2: "In the analyses, we treat the solar wind as a proton plasma — the average helium abundance is about 4.5 % and in slow wind at solar cycle minimum is even less than 2 %." Their density is therefore a proton density on a proton-plasma assumption, and the 5 per cent helium of (2.1) is this module's own choice and not theirs. Redoing (7.3) for a pure proton plasma, where $\rho = n_{\rm p}m_{\rm p}$ and $n_{\rm tot} = 2n_{\rm p}$, so that $\mu = m_{\rm p}/(2m_{\rm u}) = 0.50364$ against $m_{\rm u}$ — half of $m_{\rm p}/m_{\rm u}$, and not $1/2$, which would give $1.1244$ and a ratio of $2.57$ — raises $c_T^2$ by a factor $1.1163$ to $1.5964\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-2}}$ and the prediction to $0.01890\pm0.00068$, moving the ratio from $2.98$ to $\mathbf{2.59}$ and the shortfall from $3.2\sigma$ to $3.0\sigma$. The composition is worth about 12 per cent in $c_T^2$ and less than half a unit in the ratio. It is not the missing force either.

Given that the base density is itself an extrapolation below its source's fitted range ( §2.1 ), this comparison constrains very little, and saying so is more useful than quoting the one temperature at which the two happen to agree.

825: 8.3 What is refuted, stated exactly
827: Not the algebra. §5.1 verified (5.3) against a direct assembly from the critical point to eight figures and against Bondi's own table to every legible digit. And note that the algebra was never doing as much work as is often claimed: Bondi proves $\lambda \le \lambda_c$, so even a perfect implementation does not fix the coefficient. A refutation resting on "the algebra is right, so the physics must be wrong" would be resting on a theorem that does not exist.
829: What fails are the three physical premises of Proposition 5, each of which Sgr A* violates in a way that is separately documented:
832: No angular momentum. The gas has some. It circularises near $100\,r_S$ — five decades inside $R_B$, marked on Fig. 4 — rather than falling radially in, and a circularised flow must transport angular momentum outward before any mass reaches the centre. That is Module 11 's subject and nothing in this module can address it.
833: No outflow. Bondi's solution has $\dot M$ independent of radius by construction. The observed density profile near Sgr A* is shallower than the $r^{-3/2}$ that free-fall requires, which means $\dot M$ falls with decreasing radius: most of the gas captured at $R_B$ never arrives.
834: Adiabatic and radiatively unimportant. (8.2) shows the flow radiates $2\times10^{7}$ times less efficiently than a thin disc. That is the defining property of a radiatively inefficient accretion flow, and the literature Baganoff et al. review in their Sect. 11 exists because of it.
837: Each premise, on its own, would reduce the rate. All three act in the same direction, and together they span the factor of 40 — or 4, or 401, depending on which leg is being used — without difficulty. The value of the calculation is not that it predicts the rate. It is that it predicts a rate which is definitely wrong, by a definite amount, in a definite direction , and the size of the discrepancy is the measure of how much physics the spherical picture omits.

The same index, the same equation and the same critical-point condition describe a wind leaving the Sun at $372\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and an accretion flow arriving at a compact object with a coefficient of $0.995$. Only the sign of $v$ differs.
896: Solution
897: With $\dot M = 9.843\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$ and $v = 435.6\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$: $\tfrac12\dot Mv^2 = 9.3384\times10^{26}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$, which is $2.440\times10^{-7}\,L_\odot$. The gravitational term $GM_\odot\dot M/R_\odot = 1.878\times10^{27}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}} = 4.91\times10^{-7}\,L_\odot$ — twice as large as the kinetic term , so most of the energy invested in the wind goes into lifting it out of the potential well rather than into its final speed. Verscharen et al.'s measured $F_E \approx 1$ au² g s⁻³ sr⁻¹ over the poles converts to $2.812\times10^{27}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}} = 7.35\times10^{-7}\,L_\odot$. The kinetic and gravitational terms sum to $2.81\times10^{27}$, and the agreement to three digits is a coincidence of a source figure quoted as "about $1$": the energy flux should exceed that sum by the enthalpy flux, $\tfrac52 c_T^2\dot M \approx 3.5\times10^{25}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$, about $1.3$ per cent, plus a conductive flux this module does not compute.
898: All three are within a factor of three of one another and all are $\sim10^{-6}$ of $L_\odot$. The solar wind is energetically negligible for the Sun and structurally decisive for the heliosphere , which is the same conclusion C2 reaches about mass. It also tells us why the coronal heating problem is hard: the energy required is so small a fraction of the luminosity that almost any mechanism could supply it, and the smallness gives no leverage for choosing between mechanisms.
899: A mixture that must be declared. $\dot M$ here is Verscharen et al.'s polar rung and $v$ is Venzmer & Bothmer's ecliptic fit. They describe different wind streams at different latitudes. The mixture is defensible for an order-of-magnitude energy budget — the two differ by less than a factor of two in every relevant quantity — but the answer is an order of magnitude and not a measurement, and it should not be quoted to three digits elsewhere.
903: Solution
904: Taking the cloud as fully ionised with $\mu \approx 0.6$ against $m_{\rm H}$, $c_\infty = \sqrt{k_{\rm B}T/\mu m_{\rm H}} = 9.81\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so $v/c_\infty = 2.65$ and motion dominates . The density is $\rho = n_{\rm H}m_{\rm p}(1+4y) = 4.683\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$: $n_{\rm H}$ is the hydrogen density and $1+4y = 1.4$ is the helium mass, so this $\rho$ is $2.33$ times the $n_{\rm H}\mu m_{\rm H}$ that the sound speed is built from, and the distinction is worth $0.37$ dex in the answer. Then $R_B = 2GM/(c_\infty^2+v^2) = 3.437\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm} = 2.3$ au, and the rate from D1's formula at $\gamma = 1$, where $\lambda_c = \mathrm{e}^{3/2}/4 = 1.1204$, is $5.411\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}} = 8.59\times10^{-17}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$.
905: Against the outward wind at $9.84\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$, the ratio is 182 : the Sun sheds mass $182$ times faster than it gathers it, so accretion is irrelevant to its mass budget by more than two orders of magnitude.
906: And the accretion does not happen at all, for a second and independent reason: $R_B = 2.3$ au lies deep inside the heliosphere, whose boundary is near $120$ au. Interstellar gas never reaches that radius; the solar wind sweeps it aside first. Two separate arguments kill the same effect , which is worth noticing — the ratio 182 would be the answer even if the heliosphere did not exist.
907: The ionisation assumption. $\mu = 0.6$ takes the cloud as fully ionised, which it is not; it is partly ionised, and a neutral cloud would give $\mu = 1.27$ and $c_\infty = 6.75\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Because $v^2 \gg c_\infty^2$ the denominator is dominated by the motion, so $\dot M$ moves by only 11 per cent and no conclusion changes. The assumption is stated rather than buried because a reader checking the sound speed would otherwise find it unexplained.
911: Solution
912: With $\mu = 2.33$ against $m_{\rm H}$ for molecular gas, $c_\infty = 0.2662\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ — cold gas is very quiet. Taking $\gamma = 1$ so $\lambda_c = \mathrm{e}^{3/2}/4 = 1.1204$, $R_B = 2GM/c_\infty^2 = 3.746\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{cm} = 250\,413$ au $= 1.2140$ pc, and $\dot M = 5.124\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}} = 8.133\times10^{-4}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$.
913: The Eddington luminosity is $L_{\rm Edd} = 4\pi GMm_{\rm p}c/\sigma_{\rm T} = 1.257\times10^{39}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$, and at $\eta_{\rm rad} = 0.1$ the corresponding rate is $\dot M_{\rm Edd} = 1.399\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$. The Bondi rate exceeds it by a factor of $\mathbf{3664}$, and at that rate the hole would double its mass in $1.23\times10^{4}$ yr.
914: What it implies is that the calculation has refuted itself. A flow accreting three thousand times the Eddington rate would radiate far above $L_{\rm Edd}$, and the resulting radiation pressure would drive the gas away — violating the premise, used in Proposition 5, that radiation is dynamically unimportant. The honest reading is not "black holes in molecular clouds double in ten thousand years"; it is that spherical Bondi accretion is self-inconsistent in this regime, and a calculation that includes radiation is required. Module 13 supplies it.
915: $n = 10^{4}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ is taken as the total particle density here, not the H₂ density. Reading it as H₂ instead would raise $\rho$ by 20 per cent and $\dot M$ with it. $L_{\rm Edd}$ is quoted from Module 13 and not derived in this module; its $m_{\rm p}$ is a genuine proton mass and not a mean molecular weight.
919: Solution
920: $L_{\rm Edd} = 5.405\times10^{44}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$ at $4.30\times10^{6}\,M_\odot$, so $L_X/L_{\rm Edd} = 3.70\times10^{-12}$. At $\eta_{\rm rad} = 0.1$, $\dot M_{\rm Edd} = 9.545\times10^{-2}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$, giving $\dot M_{\rm Bondi}/\dot M_{\rm Edd} = 8.405\times10^{-5}$ and, for Marrone's equipartition upper bound, $2.095\times10^{-6}$.
921: The pattern is the point. The Bondi rate is already $10^{-4}$ of Eddington, so Sgr A* is not accretion-starved in any sense that radiation pressure could explain — nothing is pushing the gas away. Yet the luminosity is $10^{-12}$ of Eddington: $8.405\times10^{-5}$ against $3.700\times10^{-12}$ is a factor $2.27\times10^{7}$, which is $7.4$ orders of magnitude, and at $\eta_{\rm rad} = 0.1$ the two columns would have been equal. Splitting that factor isolates the discrepancy: $40.1$ of it lives in the rate itself, between the Bondi value and Marrone et al.'s equipartition upper limit, and the remaining $5.7\times10^{5}$ — $5.75$ orders of magnitude — lives in the radiative efficiency, which (8.2) puts at $4\times10^{-9}$ against a thin disc's $0.1$. Sgr A* is faint mainly because the gas that does arrive radiates almost nothing, not because little gas arrives.
