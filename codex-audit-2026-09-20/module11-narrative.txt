65: Accretion Discs
66: Angular momentum, not gravity, is what stops gas from falling in — and the molecular viscosity that could carry it away is twelve decades too slow. Four checks, of which two refute. The prescription that replaces molecular viscosity has one free parameter, and the same parameter measured in two classes of disc differs by a factor of ten to forty, while the simulations of the very instability Module 12 derives produce at most a fifth of what the observations need.

92: Module 9 built spherical accretion and then, in its own closing table, refused the object it had been aimed at. Sagittarius A*'s gas does not fall radially inward: it carries angular momentum, and it circularises near $100\,r_S$ — five decades inside the Bondi radius — where it must then transport that angular momentum outward before any mass can reach the centre. Module 9's words were that this "is Module 11 's subject and nothing in this module can address it".
94: Module 12 then stated the difficulty in one line and left it. A Keplerian disc has $\Omega \propto R^{-3/2}$, so its specific angular momentum $\ell = \Omega R^2 \propto R^{1/2}$ increases outward, which by the Rayleigh criterion makes it stable. A stable disc transports no angular momentum and therefore does not accrete. Module 12 assumed the shear $q = 3/2$ that this statement rests on, and said in print that deriving it needs a disc model, which is this module.
96: The plan of this module is one problem and one number. The problem is that a disc is too stable . The number is how badly molecular viscosity fails to fix it: twelve decades , which is not a discrepancy to be argued about but a statement that some other physics carries the angular momentum. What replaces molecular viscosity is a parametrisation with one free number in it, and the last two sections are about how big that number is and whether it deserves to be called a constant.
100: 1. Gas with angular momentum cannot fall in
102: Take a parcel of gas at rest far from a mass $M$, and give it a small specific angular momentum $\ell$ about that mass. As it falls, $\ell$ is conserved, and the centrifugal term $\ell^2/2R^2$ in its effective potential grows as $R^{-2}$ while the gravitational term $-GM/R$ grows only as $R^{-1}$. There is therefore a radius at which the two balance and the infall stops:
106: Inside $R_{\rm circ}$ the gas cannot go without losing angular momentum. It does not stop moving — it orbits. What was a radial infall becomes a disc, and the question changes from what holds the gas up? to what lets it down?

122: 2. Disc kinematics, and the stability that is the problem
124: Everything in this section is kinematics. No pressure, no viscosity, no magnetic field: only what a point mass does to a ring of gas.
188: Three quantities of a Keplerian disc, each divided by its own value at the inner radius. Two fall outward and one rises, and the one that rises is the one the Rayleigh criterion tests. Because $\ell^2 = GMR$, the discriminant $\mathrm{d}(\ell^2)/\mathrm{d}R$ equals $GM$ at every radius — positive, and independent of radius. The disc is stable everywhere, and that is the problem this module exists to solve.
193: Two consequences are worth stating before moving on. First, $\kappa_{\rm ep} = \Omega$ for a Keplerian disc is a degeneracy, not a coincidence: it is why a Keplerian orbit closes on itself and does not precess, and it is why the radial and orbital periods of a planet are the same. Second, the same equality makes the Toomre criterion of §10 simpler than it looks in the galactic-dynamics literature, where $\kappa_{\rm ep}$ and $\Omega$ differ.

273: 4. The viscous torque
275: If angular momentum is to move outward, something must exert a torque. In a fluid with kinematic viscosity $\nu$ the stress between neighbouring annuli is a shear stress, and it does exactly that.
283: The sign is worth dwelling on, because it is the opposite of the intuition that friction between orbits should drag the outer material inward. The inner disc rotates faster , so it is the inner disc that does work on the outer one. Angular momentum moves outward and mass moves inward, and each is what the other requires.
285: 5. How a disc spreads
304: 6. The α prescription, and what it is not
306: Section 3 established that molecular viscosity is twelve decades too small. Shakura and Sunyaev's response in 1973 was not to find a bigger viscosity but to parametrise one, and to bound it.
312: The argument behind the bound is short. Whatever carries the stress is a motion within the disc; if it is turbulence, its eddies cannot be larger than $H$ without leaving the disc, and cannot be faster than $c_T$ without shocking and dissipating. So $\nu \lesssim c_T H$, and $\alpha \lesssim 1$. That is the whole of it: the prescription is not a derivation and this module will not pretend otherwise.
316: Their own footnote on the same page is worth carrying too: $\alpha$ "is assumed to be constant along the disk in our calculations", and the disc's observational appearance — its spectrum and its surface temperature — "do not strongly depend on the chosen value of $\alpha$". §8 proves the second half of that sentence, and §11 is about what it costs.
318: 7. Vertical structure, and what "thin" means
327: The approximation $g_z \simeq \Omega^2 z$ has a price and it is small. At two scale heights on the dwarf-nova row, $z = 4.5698\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{cm}$ and $z/R = 0.04570$, the thin-disc form overstates the exact vertical gravity by 0.3134 per cent — and $95.45$ per cent of a Gaussian column lies inside $2H$. The error grows as $(z/R)^2$, so it is the aspect ratio and not the height that prices it.
337: For the dwarf-nova row at $\alpha = 0.2$ the three are $1.1206\times10^{2}$, $5.6032\times10^{2}$ and $1.0732\times10^{6}$ seconds, with $(R/H)^2 = 1.9154\times10^{3}$. That ordering is what makes a thin disc a well-posed object. It can be treated as vertically hydrostatic and in local thermal balance while it evolves radially, because the radial evolution is the slowest thing happening.
345: Module 3 §11 listed "no rotation" among its idealisations and said it "excludes centrifugal flattening and the rotational support of discs", pointing here. Equation (7.2) is that repayment , and it is worth saying why it and not something more obvious. A Keplerian disc satisfies $\Omega^2R^3/GM = 1$ exactly at every radius, but that identity is Kepler's law restated and says nothing about a disc; it is true of a single planet. What distinguishes a disc from Module 3's hydrostatic star is the ratio of the two support mechanisms : radially the disc is supported by rotation and by nothing else, vertically by pressure and by nothing else, and the aspect ratio (7.2) is the flattening. A sphere has $H/R = 1$; the dwarf-nova row has $0.02285$ and the protoplanetary row $0.044751$.

437: 9. What a disc radiates, and the price of a Newtonian book
449: Half the binding energy, and not all of it. The specific binding energy of a circular orbit at $R_{\rm in}$ is $GM/2R_{\rm in}$, not $GM/R_{\rm in}$: the other half is the kinetic energy of the orbit, which the disc still carries when the material crosses the inner boundary and has not radiated.
451: This book is Newtonian, and six gravitational radii from a black hole is not a place where Newtonian gravity is right. Bardeen, Press and Teukolsky's eq. (2.21) gives the marginally stable orbit as $r_{\rm ms} = 6M$ for a non-rotating hole, and their eq. (2.12) gives the energy per unit rest mass of an equatorial circular orbit. Evaluated at $a = 0$ and $r = 6M$ that formula returns $4/(3\sqrt2) = \sqrt{8/9}$ exactly, so the relativistic efficiency is $1 - \sqrt{8/9} = 0.057191$. Their eq. (2.14), the extreme-Kerr simplification, at $r_{\rm ms} = M$ gives $1/\sqrt3$ and hence $1 - 1/\sqrt3 = 0.422650$.

568: 11. What supplies the stress, and how big α is
570: Definition 2 named a number and bounded it. It did not say what physical process carries the stress, and for twenty years nobody could. 653: 12. What this theory captures, and what it misses
655: The thin $\alpha$-disc captures a great deal for what it is: a one-parameter closure applied to a vertically averaged, axisymmetric, Keplerian flow.
