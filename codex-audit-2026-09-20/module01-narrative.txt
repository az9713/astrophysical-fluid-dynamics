92: Module 1 — Why a Fluid at All?
93: The continuum condition for astrophysical gases: the mean free path, the Knudsen number, and the four systems in which the fluid picture fails outright.
114: 1. The problem: a fluid emptier than any vacuum ever built
116: Everything in this book rests on four equations — conservation of mass, of momentum, of energy, and an equation of state — applied to a substance treated as a continuum. Later modules use them to compute the radius of a supernova remnant (Module 8), the mass at which an interstellar cloud must collapse (Module 5), and the rate at which a black hole can swallow gas (Module 9). Before any of that, one question has to be settled, and it is not a formality.
118: A continuum has a density at every point. Real gas does not: it is a finite number of particles with empty space between them. The fluid equations are an approximation whose validity has to be earned, and in astrophysics the gas is thin enough that earning it is not obvious.
120: Here is the scale of the problem. A specialist vacuum laboratory can routinely pump a chamber down to about $10^{-13}$ torr. At room temperature that pressure corresponds to a number density
124: where $P$ is the pressure, $T$ the absolute temperature, and $k_B$ Boltzmann's constant. The warm neutral interstellar medium — the gas that fills most of the volume of the Milky Way's disc — has a number density of roughly $0.5\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. It is therefore about $6.4\times10^{3}$ times more rarefied than that laboratory vacuum. Even against the lowest reported laboratory pressures, near $10^{-15}$ torr and $32\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, the interstellar medium is still 64 times emptier.
128: So the object we are about to call a fluid is, by a wide margin, emptier than the emptiest thing anyone has ever built. And yet the fluid equations describe it well. The purpose of this module is to say exactly why, to produce the single number that decides the question, and to identify the systems where the answer comes out the other way.
130: The logic runs in one direction and each step depends on the last. A fluid description is a claim that the velocity distribution of the particles is fixed by a handful of local numbers ( §2 ). Collisions are what make that claim true ( §3 ). How far a particle travels between collisions is the mean free path, computed for neutral gas in §4 and for ionised gas in §5 . Comparing that length to the size of the structure you want to resolve gives the Knudsen number, and the size of the Knudsen number decides which equations you are entitled to write down ( §6 ).

134: Start with the complete description and then say what the fluid description throws away.

We take that result as given; what we need here is not the theorem but its rate , because a gas is only Maxwellian if the collisions get there before the flow carries the gas somewhere else.

Equation (3.1) says only this: collisions push $f$ towards the local Maxwellian, and they do it on a timescale $\tau$. It is not exact — it replaces a complicated integral operator by a single relaxation rate — but it reproduces the scaling we need, and that scaling is the whole content of this section.
187: Proposition 1 is the hinge of the entire module, so it is worth saying in words what the algebra did. A particle arriving at a point carries the properties of the place where it last collided, one mean free path away. If the gas changes appreciably over that distance, the arriving particles are a mixture of populations from measurably different conditions, and their combined velocity distribution is not the Maxwellian of the point they have arrived at. The mismatch is proportional to how much the gas changes over one mean free path, which is $\lambda/L$. If $\lambda/L$ is small, every particle arriving at a point comes from somewhere effectively identical, and the local Maxwellian is exact for all practical purposes.
189: Everything now reduces to computing $\lambda$, which is a different calculation for neutral gas and for ionised gas.

250: 5. The mean free path II — a gas of charges
252: Most astrophysical gas is ionised, and the argument of §4 breaks at the first step. The Coulomb force has no range: two charges interact at any separation, so there is no distance $d$ inside which a collision happens and outside which it does not. Worse, the naive cross-section diverges. We have to define what counts as a collision.

296: For $b \gg b_{90}$, equation (5.1) linearises to $\theta \approx 2b_{90}/b$. Single distant encounters are weak, but they are numerous, because the number of targets at impact parameters between $b$ and $b+db$ grows as $2\pi b\,db$. 300: The integral is $\ln(b_{\max}/b_{\min})$. Every decade of impact parameter contributes equally: the weakness of distant encounters is exactly cancelled by how many of them there are. 316: 5.3 The Coulomb mean free path
332: The mass-independence is worth a moment, because it is easy to misread. Neither $b_{90}$ nor $\lambda$ contains the particle mass: $b_{90}=e^2/3k_BT$ depends on the charge and the temperature alone. So at a common temperature an electron and a proton in the same plasma travel the same distance between large-angle deflections, even though the electron moves $\sqrt{m_p/m_e}=42.9$ times faster. What differs by that factor is the time , not the distance: the electron completes its 22 kpc in $1/43$ of the time the proton takes. For ions of charge $Ze$ the distance shortens as $Z^{-4}$, since $b_{90}\propto Z^2$ enters squared.
334: Read the scaling before the numbers. The mean free path goes as $T^2/n$: hot, thin plasma is nearly collisionless, and cold, dense plasma is strongly collisional. The $T^2$ is the more surprising half. It comes from $b_{90}\propto T^{-1}$ squared — a fast particle spends less time near any given target and is deflected less — and it is steep enough that a factor of 10 in temperature changes the mean free path by 100.
338: 6. The Knudsen number and the Chapman–Enskog hierarchy
342: The second half of that definition is not a technicality, and it is where most errors of judgement enter. The Knudsen number is a property of the question, not of the gas. The photospheric gas of §4.1 has one mean free path, 66 µm, but two Knudsen numbers. Measured against the solar radius it is $9.6\times10^{-14}$; measured against the pressure scale height of about 150 km, which is the scale on which convection organises itself and therefore the scale a model of granulation must resolve, it is $4.4\times10^{-10}$. Both are minute, so here the distinction changes nothing. In §7 we meet a case where it changes everything.

Engineering practice in rarefied gas dynamics draws the same boundaries and adds a fourth regime above $\mathrm{Kn}\approx10$, where particles cross the whole system without meeting each other. This book does not use that last distinction, because the astrophysical question is only ever whether a fluid description exists at all.
360: Modules 2 to 11 of this book use the Euler equations. That is the first row, and it is the right choice for most astrophysical problems — but only because the Knudsen numbers come out where they do, which is the subject of the next section. The transport coefficients in the second row are worth deriving now, because their scaling produces one of the more consequential results in the book.
378: Two consequences are worth stating plainly. First, the density-independence of viscosity is not an artefact of the plasma case: it is Maxwell's result for any gas. He derived it in 1860, did not believe the experiments then available, and tested it himself with Katherine Maxwell in 1865–66, finding the viscosity of air constant over a wide range of pressure. Second, the $T^{5/2}$ law makes hot plasma a very good conductor of heat. Raising the temperature from $10^6$ to $10^8$ K raises $\kappa$ by $(100)^{5/2}=10^5$ from the power alone, or by $8.8\times10^{4}$ once the accompanying rise in $\ln\Lambda$ from 32.5 to 37.1 is included. This is why unsuppressed thermal conduction would erase the temperature structure observed in cluster cores within a fraction of the cluster's age, and it is the reason the question of what suppresses it is a live one. We return to it in Module 12, where magnetic fields provide the answer.

There, rarefaction wins. The conclusion survives anyway, and that is the real point — $6.5\times10^{-6}$ still sits more than three decades below the $\mathrm{Kn}\approx10^{-2}$ ceiling of the Euler regime. The fluid description holds for interstellar gas not because that gas resembles air but because it has three decades of margin to spend.

543: 8. Where the fluid picture fails, and what rescues it
545: 8.1 The solar wind, and the magnetic rescue
547: At 1 AU the solar wind has $\mathrm{Kn}\approx1.9$. By the hierarchy of §6 there should be no fluid description of it at all, and the situation is more extreme than $\mathrm{Kn}\approx2$ on its own suggests. Take the radial scalings of problem K1 , under which $\lambda(r) = 1.94\,r^{-2/3}$ AU with $r$ measured in AU, and integrate the collision rate along the outflow. The expected number of Coulomb collisions a proton suffers between the corona and Earth's orbit is
$$ \int_0^{1}\frac{dr}{\lambda(r)} \;=\; \frac{1}{1.94}\int_0^1 r^{2/3}\,dr \;=\; \frac{3}{5\times1.94} \;=\; 0.31. $$
Fewer than one proton in three arriving at Earth has collided even once since it left the Sun. And yet magnetohydrodynamic models of the solar wind reproduce its large-scale structure — the spiral field, the stream interaction regions, the propagation of coronal mass ejections — with considerable success. Something other than collisions is confining the particles.

586: 8.2 When the particles are stars, and when they are dark matter
588: Two systems are routinely called fluids in casual speech and are not fluids under any reading of Definition 2.
590: Stars in a galaxy. Take the solar neighbourhood: a stellar number density of about $0.1\ \mathrm{pc^{-3}}$, a velocity dispersion near $20\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, and a geometric cross-section for physical contact of $\sigma=\pi(2R_\odot)^2 = 6.1\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{cm^2}$. Proposition 2 then gives a mean free path of $1.6\times10^{6}$ Gpc and a mean time between stellar collisions of $7.7\times10^{19}$ years, which is $5.5\times10^{9}$ Hubble times. Stars do not touch.

605: The three rows land in three different places, and the spread is the point. A galaxy is collisionless by a factor of a million: the stellar orbits it has today are the orbits it was born with, and no fluid description of them is admissible. A globular cluster relaxes in about an eighth of a Hubble time, so over its life it is collisional — which is why globular clusters undergo core collapse and mass segregation, processes that have no analogue in galaxies. The galaxies within a cluster sit almost exactly on the boundary.
607: Dark matter. Here the constraint runs the other way: we do not know the cross-section, and its smallness is an observational result. The merging cluster 1E 0657-56, the Bullet Cluster, separates its collisional component (the X-ray-emitting intracluster gas, which shocked and lagged) from its collisionless component (the galaxies and the lensing mass, which passed through). Randall and collaborators (2008) turned that separation into an upper limit on the dark matter self-interaction cross-section per unit mass, $\sigma/m \lt 0.7\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$ at 68% confidence. Since $\lambda = 1/(\rho\,\sigma/m)$ for a medium of mass density $\rho$, and a cluster of $10^{15}\,M_\odot$ within 1 Mpc has a mean density $\rho = M/(\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3) = 1.6\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, which we round to $2\times10^{-26}$, even the extreme allowed value gives
611: so $\mathrm{Kn}\ge23$ against the megaparsec size of a cluster. The core is denser than that mean by about a factor of six, and taking $\rho=10^{-25}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ there gives $\lambda\ge4.6$ Mpc and $\mathrm{Kn}\ge4.6$ — smaller, but still greater than one in the region where a collision would have been most likely. Dark matter is collisionless, and the Bullet Cluster measurement is essentially a direct measurement of that fact.
613: All three systems — galaxies, dark matter, and the collisionless solar wind along $\mathbf{B}$ — are governed by the Boltzmann equation (2.1) with the right-hand side set to zero. That is the collisionless Boltzmann, or Vlasov, equation, and the discipline built on it is stellar dynamics rather than fluid dynamics. This book does not develop it. It is named here so that the boundary of what follows is explicit.

630: The first row deserves more than a pointer, because it fails inside the very system we used to validate §5.3 . Electrons and protons at the same temperature carry very different momenta, and energy transfer between the two species is slow. Sarazin (1988) gives the equilibration times for intracluster conditions in the ratio $1 : 43 : 1870$ for electron–electron, proton–proton and proton–electron exchange respectively — the middle ratio being $\sqrt{m_p/m_e}=42.9$ and the last being close to $m_p/m_e = 1836$. At $T_m\approx10^8$ K and $n_e\approx10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ the proton–electron equilibration time is about $6\times10^{8}$ years. A merger shock crossing a cluster takes of order $10^{9}$ years, so the gas behind such a shock is genuinely two-temperature: the protons are heated first and the electrons lag. Since X-ray observations measure the electron temperature, a single-fluid reading of the data underestimates the true thermal energy in exactly the regions where mergers are most violent.
632: That is a fair summary of the position this module leaves us in. The fluid equations are licensed for almost every system in the table of §7 , the licence is quantitative and checkable, and the places where it lapses are identified rather than hidden. Module 2 writes the equations down.
