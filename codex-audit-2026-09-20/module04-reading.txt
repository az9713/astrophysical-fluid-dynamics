113: 1. The problem: a speed of sound low by 15.5 per cent, and a star that rings
115: Module 3 set the velocity to zero and found the structure that the pressure gradient must have to hold a gas up against gravity. This module asks what happens when that equilibrium is disturbed a little. The answer is sound, and the method that finds it, linear perturbation theory, is the method that every instability in Modules 5 to 7 reuses.
117: The first calculation of the speed of sound from mechanics was Newton's. He treated the air as a gas whose pressure is proportional to its density at fixed temperature, and his result, in modern notation, is
121: with $P$ the pressure, $\rho$ the mass density, $k_B$ Boltzmann's constant, $T$ the temperature, $\mu$ the mean molecular weight and $m_u$ the atomic mass unit, as in Module 3, equation (3.2). For dry air at $0\ ^\circ$C, (1.1) gives $280.02\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The measured speed is $331.45\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Newton's value is $0.8448$ of it, low by $15.5$ per cent. The error is not in the measurement and not in the arithmetic. It is in the relation assumed between pressure and density, the closure, and §3.1 finds it.

129: The module follows the three-part shape of this book. §2 and §3 derive the terrestrial result and check Newton and Laplace against the measured speed of sound in air. §4 and §5 change the regime: gravity stratifies the gas, which introduces a cutoff frequency tied to the scale height of Module 3, and a star is a closed cavity, which quantises the frequencies. §6 and §7 take the result to the Sun. The isothermal closure is refuted by a bound computed from the tabulated model alone. The adiabatic closure is consistent with the measurement in the interior. In the outer $1.7$ per cent of the radius, where the model table stops, a perfect gas with $\Gamma_1 = 5/3$ falls short of what the measurement requires.
131: 2. Linear perturbation theory
133: The equations to be perturbed are those of an inviscid, adiabatic fluid. Module 2 derived them: continuity (6.3), the Euler equation (7.3), and the adiabatic energy equation in (8.4). With the Lagrangian derivative $D/Dt \equiv \partial/\partial t + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla$ of Module 2, (6.1), they read
139: in which $\mathbf{u}$ is the bulk velocity and $\mathbf{g}$ the gravitational acceleration. Module 2 wrote the adiabatic condition for a gas with a fixed ratio of specific heats. A gas whose ionisation changes as it is compressed needs a more general coefficient.
141: Definition 1 (first adiabatic exponent). The first adiabatic exponent is
$$ \Gamma_1 \;\equiv\; \left(\frac{\partial\ln P}{\partial\ln\rho}\right)_{s}, \tag{2.3}$$
the logarithmic response of the pressure to a compression at fixed specific entropy $s$. An adiabatic flow is one in which each parcel keeps its entropy, $Ds/Dt=0$, and then
$$ \frac{DP}{Dt} \;=\; \frac{\Gamma_1P}{\rho}\,\frac{D\rho}{Dt}. \tag{2.4}$$
For an ideal gas of fixed composition and fixed ionisation, $\Gamma_1 = \gamma$, the ratio of specific heats: $5/3$ for a monatomic gas and $7/5$ for the diatomic molecules of air. Where a compression changes the ionisation, part of the work done goes into ionising atoms rather than into raising the pressure, and $\Gamma_1\lt\gamma$.
147: Equation (2.4) follows from (2.3) by the chain rule along a parcel's path, since $s$ is constant along that path. Module 2's condition $D(P/\rho^\gamma)/Dt = 0$ is (2.4) with $\Gamma_1=\gamma$ constant. The last sentence of Definition 1 is quoted, not derived; the thermodynamics of an ionising gas is not developed in this book, and the statement is used in §7.3 only as the candidate explanation of a measured shortfall.
149: Equations (2.1), (2.2) and (2.4) are nonlinear, because products such as $\rho\,(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}$ appear. Close to an equilibrium the products of small quantities are smaller still, and dropping them gives linear equations.
151: Definition 2 (linear perturbation). Let $\rho_0(\mathbf{x})$, $P_0(\mathbf{x})$ and $\mathbf{u}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ be a static solution of (2.1), (2.2) and (2.4), so that $\nabla P_0 = \rho_0\mathbf{g}$ (Module 3, (2.2)). A perturbation writes each field as its equilibrium value plus a small departure,
$$ \rho = \rho_0 + \rho_1, \qquad P = P_0 + P_1, \qquad \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}_1, \tag{2.5}$$
with $|\rho_1|\ll\rho_0$, $|P_1|\ll P_0$, and $|\mathbf{u}_1|$ small compared with the sound speed. The linearised equations are obtained by substituting (2.5) and keeping only terms of first order in the subscript-1 quantities. The gravitational field $\mathbf{g}$ is held fixed; the perturbation of gravity by $\rho_1$ is neglected.
155: Proposition 1 (the linearised equations). About a static equilibrium, and with $\mathbf{g}$ fixed, the linearised forms of (2.1), (2.2) and (2.4) are
$$ \frac{\partial\rho_1}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho_0\mathbf{u}_1) \;=\; 0, \tag{2.6}$$
$$ \rho_0\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_1}{\partial t} \;=\; -\nabla P_1 + \rho_1\,\mathbf{g}, \tag{2.7}$$
$$ \frac{\partial P_1}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u}_1\cdot\nabla P_0 \;=\; c^2\left(\frac{\partial\rho_1}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u}_1\cdot\nabla\rho_0\right), \qquad c^2 \;\equiv\; \frac{\Gamma_1P_0}{\rho_0}. \tag{2.8}$$
160: Continuity (2.1) is equivalent to $\partial\rho/\partial t + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u}) = 0$ (Module 2, Proposition 5). Substituting (2.5) gives $\partial\rho_1/\partial t + \nabla\cdot(\rho_0\mathbf{u}_1) + \nabla\cdot(\rho_1\mathbf{u}_1) = 0$, using $\partial\rho_0/\partial t = 0$. The last term is a product of two first-order quantities and is dropped, which leaves (2.6). In (2.2), the left side is $(\rho_0+\rho_1)[\partial\mathbf{u}_1/\partial t + (\mathbf{u}_1\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}_1]$, whose only first-order term is $\rho_0\,\partial\mathbf{u}_1/\partial t$. The right side is $-\nabla P_0 - \nabla P_1 + \rho_0\mathbf{g} + \rho_1\mathbf{g}$, and the zeroth-order terms cancel by hydrostatic equilibrium, which leaves (2.7). In (2.4), $DP/Dt = \partial P_1/\partial t + \mathbf{u}_1\cdot\nabla P_0 + \mathbf{u}_1\cdot\nabla P_1$, whose last term is second order; $D\rho/Dt$ is treated the same way. The coefficient $\Gamma_1P/\rho$ multiplies a first-order quantity, so only its zeroth-order value $\Gamma_1P_0/\rho_0 = c^2$ is kept. That gives (2.8). ∎
164: The dropped term $(\mathbf{u}_1\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}_1$ is the one that matters most. For a wave of speed $c$ and wavelength $\lambda$, $\partial\mathbf{u}_1/\partial t$ is of order $|\mathbf{u}_1|c/\lambda$ and $(\mathbf{u}_1\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}_1$ is of order $|\mathbf{u}_1|^2/\lambda$. Their ratio is $|\mathbf{u}_1|/c$, the Mach number of the fluid motion. Linear theory is therefore the theory of motions whose speed is small compared with the sound speed. When the ratio approaches one, the wave steepens into a shock, which is the subject of Module 8.
166: The quantity $c$ defined in (2.8) has the dimensions of a speed. §3 shows that it is the speed of sound.
168: 3. Sound in a uniform gas
170: Take the simplest equilibrium: no gravity, so that hydrostatic balance gives $\nabla P_0 = \mathbf{0}$, and a uniform density $\rho_0$. Then $P_0$, $\rho_0$ and $c$ are constants.
172: Proposition 2 (the wave equation). In a uniform static gas with $\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{0}$, the linearised equations (2.6)–(2.8) imply
$$ \frac{\partial^2\rho_1}{\partial t^2} \;=\; c^2\,\nabla^2\rho_1, \tag{3.1}$$
and the same equation for $P_1$. A plane wave $\rho_1 \propto \exp[i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x} - \omega t)]$ with wavevector $\mathbf{k}$ and angular frequency $\omega$ solves (3.1) if and only if
$$ \omega^2 \;=\; c^2\,|\mathbf{k}|^2. \tag{3.2}$$
The phase speed $\omega/|\mathbf{k}|$ and the group speed $d\omega/d|\mathbf{k}|$ both equal $c$, for every wavelength.
178: With $\nabla P_0 = \nabla\rho_0 = \mathbf{0}$, (2.8) reduces to $\partial P_1/\partial t = c^2\,\partial\rho_1/\partial t$. Integrating in time, and choosing $P_1=\rho_1=0$ before the disturbance arrives, gives $P_1 = c^2\rho_1$. Take the time derivative of (2.6), which is $\partial^2\rho_1/\partial t^2 = -\rho_0\nabla\cdot(\partial\mathbf{u}_1/\partial t)$, and substitute the divergence of (2.7) with $\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{0}$, which is $\rho_0\nabla\cdot(\partial\mathbf{u}_1/\partial t) = -\nabla^2P_1$. The result is $\partial^2\rho_1/\partial t^2 = \nabla^2P_1 = c^2\nabla^2\rho_1$, which is (3.1). Because $P_1 = c^2\rho_1$ with $c$ constant, $P_1$ obeys the same equation. For the plane wave, $\partial^2/\partial t^2 \to -\omega^2$ and $\nabla^2\to -|\mathbf{k}|^2$, so (3.1) holds if and only if $\omega^2 = c^2|\mathbf{k}|^2$. Then $\omega/|\mathbf{k}| = c$, and $d\omega/d|\mathbf{k}| = c$. ∎
182: This book writes a perturbation as proportional to $\exp[i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\omega t)]$ and every dispersion relation in the form $\omega^2 = \ldots$. With that convention a real $\omega$ is an oscillation and $\omega^2\lt 0$ is exponential growth. Module 5 will write the Jeans relation in the same form, where the sign of the right-hand side decides stability.
184: The proof used only one property of $c^2$: that it is the constant ratio $P_1/\rho_1$ in the wave. Which ratio that is depends on whether the gas exchanges heat during an oscillation. That is the whole of the disagreement between Newton and Laplace.

216: 4. Sound in a stratified atmosphere: the acoustic cutoff
218: A star is not uniform. Gravity stratifies it, and Module 3 derived the simplest stratified state: the isothermal plane-parallel layer, whose pressure and density both fall as $e^{-z/H}$ with height $z$, where $H = k_BT/(\mu m_u g)$ is the pressure scale height of Module 3, Proposition 3. This section perturbs that state. The result is a new frequency, below which sound cannot propagate upward.
220: Restrict attention to vertical motion, $\mathbf{u}_1 = u(z,t)\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}$, with all perturbations depending on $z$ and $t$ only. Waves with a horizontal wavenumber add a second restoring force, buoyancy, and are treated in Module 6.
222: Proposition 3 (vertical sound waves in an isothermal atmosphere). Let the equilibrium be a plane-parallel isothermal layer of ideal gas with uniform $\mu$, $g$ and $\Gamma_1$, so that
$$ \rho_0(z) = \rho_0(0)\,e^{-z/H}, \qquad P_0 = g H\rho_0, \qquad c^2 = \Gamma_1 gH, \tag{4.1}$$
with $c$ uniform. For vertical perturbations, the velocity obeys
$$ \frac{\partial^2u}{\partial t^2} \;=\; c^2\left(\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial z^2} - \frac{1}{H}\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\right). \tag{4.2}$$
The substitution $u = e^{z/2H}\,\psi$ turns (4.2) into
$$ \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial t^2} \;=\; c^2\,\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial z^2} \;-\; \omega_{\rm ac}^2\,\psi, \qquad \omega_{\rm ac} \;\equiv\; \frac{c}{2H}, \tag{4.3}$$
and a wave $\psi\propto\exp[i(kz-\omega t)]$ solves (4.3) if and only if
$$ \omega^2 \;=\; c^2k^2 + \omega_{\rm ac}^2. \tag{4.4}$$
231: The equilibrium (4.1) is Module 3's Proposition 3 with $P_0 = \rho_0k_BT/(\mu m_u) = gH\rho_0$ by the definition of $H$; then $c^2 = \Gamma_1P_0/\rho_0 = \Gamma_1gH$ is uniform. Two derivatives of the equilibrium are needed: $d\rho_0/dz = -\rho_0/H$ and $dP_0/dz = -\rho_0 g$. In one dimension, with $\mathbf{g} = -g\hat{\mathbf{z}}$, the linearised equations (2.6)–(2.8) are
$$ \frac{\partial\rho_1}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial(\rho_0u)}{\partial z}, \qquad \rho_0\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial P_1}{\partial z} - g\rho_1, \qquad \frac{\partial P_1}{\partial t} - \rho_0gu = c^2\left(\frac{\partial\rho_1}{\partial t} - \frac{\rho_0u}{H}\right). $$ Step 1: eliminate $\rho_1$ from the energy equation. Expanding continuity, $\partial\rho_1/\partial t = -\rho_0\,\partial u/\partial z + \rho_0u/H$. Substituting into the energy equation,
$$ \frac{\partial P_1}{\partial t} \;=\; \rho_0gu - c^2\rho_0\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} + \frac{c^2\rho_0u}{H} - \frac{c^2\rho_0u}{H} \;=\; -\rho_0\left(c^2\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} - gu\right). $$
The two terms in $c^2\rho_0u/H$ cancel. They would cancel for any $\rho_0(z)$, since both come from $u\,d\rho_0/dz$. Step 2: differentiate the momentum equation in time and substitute both results:
$$ \rho_0\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial t^2} \;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[\rho_0\left(c^2\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} - gu\right)\right] - g\left(-\rho_0\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} + \frac{\rho_0u}{H}\right). $$
Expand the first term with $d\rho_0/dz = -\rho_0/H$:
$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[\rho_0\left(c^2u_z - gu\right)\right] \;=\; \rho_0\left[c^2u_{zz} - gu_z - \frac{c^2u_z}{H} + \frac{gu}{H}\right], $$
where subscripts denote partial derivatives. Adding the second term, $\rho_0[gu_z - gu/H]$, the terms in $g$ cancel in pairs and leave $\rho_0[c^2u_{zz} - c^2u_z/H]$. Dividing by $\rho_0$ gives (4.2). Before this expansion the result holds for any stratification: the terms in $g$ cancel by hydrostatic balance and uniform $g$ alone, and Steps 1 and 2 give $\rho_0\,\partial^2u/\partial t^2 = \partial(\Gamma_1P_0\,\partial u/\partial z)/\partial z$ for any $\rho_0(z)$ and $\Gamma_1(z)$. The isothermal equilibrium enters only in the expansion itself: with $c$ uniform and $d\rho_0/dz = -\rho_0/H$ with $H$ uniform, the right side becomes $\rho_0c^2(u_{zz} - u_z/H)$, an equation with constant coefficients. Step 3 needs those constant coefficients; in a non-isothermal layer $H$ and $c$ vary with $z$, and the substitution no longer produces a constant $\omega_{\rm ac}$. Step 3: remove the first-derivative term. With $u = e^{z/2H}\psi$,
$$ u_z = e^{z/2H}\left(\psi_z + \frac{\psi}{2H}\right), \qquad u_{zz} = e^{z/2H}\left(\psi_{zz} + \frac{\psi_z}{H} + \frac{\psi}{4H^2}\right). $$
So $u_{zz} - u_z/H = e^{z/2H}\,(\psi_{zz} - \psi/4H^2)$: the terms in $\psi_z$ cancel, and $\psi/4H^2 - \psi/2H^2 = -\psi/4H^2$. The left side of (4.2) is $e^{z/2H}\psi_{tt}$. Dividing through by $e^{z/2H}$ gives $\psi_{tt} = c^2\psi_{zz} - (c^2/4H^2)\psi$, which is (4.3) with $\omega_{\rm ac}^2 = c^2/4H^2$. Step 4. For $\psi\propto\exp[i(kz-\omega t)]$, $\psi_{tt} = -\omega^2\psi$ and $\psi_{zz} = -k^2\psi$, so (4.3) holds if and only if $-\omega^2 = -c^2k^2 - \omega_{\rm ac}^2$, which is (4.4). ∎

260: Growth of the amplitude with height. Since $\rho_0\propto e^{-z/H}$, the factor $e^{z/2H}$ in $u = e^{z/2H}\psi$ equals $[\rho_0(0)/\rho_0(z)]^{1/2}$. 262: Energy in an evanescent wave. For $\omega\lt\omega_{\rm ac}$ the energy density $\tfrac12\rho_0u^2\propto|\psi|^2\propto e^{-2\kappa z}$ decays upward. The velocity amplitude varies as $e^{(1/2H-\kappa)z}$, and by (4.5) $\kappa\lt\omega_{\rm ac}/c = 1/2H$, so the velocity of an evanescent wave still grows with height, more slowly than that of a propagating one. "Evanescent" describes the energy, not the velocity (Problem C2).

337: 5. Standing waves and the large separation
339: A wave trapped between two reflecting boundaries interferes with its own reflection, and only frequencies at which the reflections reinforce survive. The spacing of those frequencies is set by the time sound takes to cross the cavity.
341: Definition 3 (acoustic radius). The acoustic radius of a star of radius $R$ is the sound-crossing time from centre to surface,
$$ \tau \;\equiv\; \int_0^R\frac{dr}{c(r)}. \tag{5.1}$$
For a one-dimensional cavity $0\le x\le L$ the same integral over $x$ is its acoustic length.
345: Proposition 4 (radial modes of a uniform sphere). Let a sphere of radius $R$ contain a uniform gas with sound speed $c$ and no gravity, and let the pressure perturbation vanish at the surface, $P_1(R)=0$. The spherically symmetric solutions of (3.1) that are finite at the centre have frequencies
$$ \nu_n \;=\; \frac{n\,c}{2R}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots, \tag{5.2}$$
so consecutive modes are separated by exactly $c/(2R) = 1/(2\tau)$.
349: By Proposition 2, $P_1 = c^2\rho_1$ obeys (3.1). For a spherically symmetric standing wave $P_1 = f(r)\cos\omega t$, and the radial Laplacian is $\nabla^2P_1 = r^{-1}\,\partial^2(rP_1)/\partial r^2$. Writing $f = F(r)/r$, (3.1) becomes $F'' + k^2F = 0$ with $k = \omega/c$, whose general solution is $F = A\sin kr + B\cos kr$. Finiteness of $f = F/r$ at $r=0$ requires $F(0)=0$, so $B=0$ and $f = A\sin(kr)/r$. The surface condition $f(R)=0$ requires $\sin kR = 0$ with $A\ne0$, so $kR = n\pi$ for a positive integer $n$. Then $\omega = n\pi c/R$ and $\nu = \omega/2\pi = nc/(2R)$. The spacing is $c/(2R)$, and for uniform $c$ (5.1) gives $\tau = R/c$, so $c/(2R) = 1/(2\tau)$. ∎
353: The surface condition $P_1 = 0$ is an idealisation of a free surface. A different reflecting condition changes the phase of the reflection. In the uniform column of Proposition 5, $u=0$ instead of $P_1=0$ at one end gives $\nu_n = (n+\tfrac12)/(2\tau)$: every frequency shifts by $1/(4\tau)$ and the spacing is unchanged. In the sphere the rigid condition gives $\tan kR = kR$, whose roots approach $(n+\tfrac12)\pi$ only as $n$ grows, so there the spacing is unchanged only asymptotically. In a real star $c$ varies with radius. The next proposition shows, in one dimension, that the spacing is still $1/(2\tau)$ at high frequency, with $\tau$ the integral (5.1).
355: Proposition 5 (spacing in a cavity of varying sound speed). Let a one-dimensional column $0\le x\le L$ with no gravity have uniform $P_0$, a density $\rho_0(x)$ and an adiabatic exponent $\Gamma_1(x)$, so that $c(x)^2 = \Gamma_1(x)P_0/\rho_0(x)$. Let $P_1 = 0$ at both ends. Define the acoustic coordinate $s(x) = \int_0^x dx'/c$, with total $\tau = s(L)$, and the impedance $Z = \rho_0c$. Then the pressure amplitude $p(s)$ of a mode of angular frequency $\omega$ satisfies, with $p = Z^{1/2}q$,
$$ \frac{d^2q}{ds^2} + \left[\omega^2 - V(s)\right]q \;=\; 0, \qquad V \;=\; a^2 - \frac{da}{ds}, \quad a \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\frac{d\ln Z}{ds}, \tag{5.3}$$
with $q(0) = q(\tau) = 0$. If $Z$ is uniform, $V=0$ and $\nu_n = n/(2\tau)$ exactly (uniform $Z$ requires only $\Gamma_1\rho_0$ constant, so $c$ may still vary). If $V$ is bounded, the mode with $n-1$ interior zeros satisfies
$$ \left|\,\nu_n - \frac{n}{2\tau}\,\right| \;\le\; \frac{1}{4\pi^2\tau\,\nu_n}\int_0^\tau|V|\,ds, \tag{5.3a}$$
so the frequencies approach $n/(2\tau)$ as $\nu_n$ grows, and consecutive modes are separated by $1/(2\tau)$ up to a remainder of order $1/\nu_n$.
361: With $\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\nabla P_0 = \mathbf{0}$, (2.7) gives $\rho_0\,\partial u/\partial t = -\partial P_1/\partial x$. Equation (2.8) has a term $c^2u\,d\rho_0/dx$ on the right. Substituting $\partial\rho_1/\partial t = -\rho_0\,\partial u/\partial x - u\,d\rho_0/dx$ from (2.6) cancels it and leaves $\partial P_1/\partial t = -\rho_0c^2\,\partial u/\partial x = -\Gamma_1(x)P_0\,\partial u/\partial x$. For $P_1 = p(x)\cos\omega t$ and $u = v(x)\sin\omega t$, the two equations are $\rho_0\omega v = -p'$ and $\omega p = \Gamma_1P_0 v'$ (with $\Gamma_1 = \Gamma_1(x)$ here and below), where primes denote $d/dx$. Eliminating $v$ gives $(p'/\rho_0)' + \omega^2 p/(\Gamma_1P_0) = 0$. Change variable to $s$, with $d/dx = c^{-1}d/ds$. Then $p'/\rho_0 = (\rho_0c)^{-1}dp/ds = Z^{-1}p_s$, and the equation becomes $c^{-1}\,d(Z^{-1}p_s)/ds + \omega^2p/(\rho_0c^2) = 0$. Multiplying by $c$ and using $\rho_0c = Z$ gives $d(Z^{-1}p_s)/ds + \omega^2Z^{-1}p = 0$, that is,
$$ p_{ss} - 2a\,p_s + \omega^2p \;=\; 0. $$
Substitute $p = Z^{1/2}q$, so $p_s = Z^{1/2}(q_s + aq)$ and $p_{ss} = Z^{1/2}[q_{ss} + 2aq_s + (a^2 + a_s)q]$. Then $p_{ss} - 2ap_s = Z^{1/2}[q_{ss} + (a_s - a^2)q]$, and the equation is (5.3). Since $Z\gt0$, $p=0$ at an end if and only if $q=0$ there. If $Z$ is uniform, $a = 0$ and $V = 0$, so $q = \sin\omega s$, and $q(\tau) = 0$ requires $\omega\tau = n\pi$, that is $\nu_n = n/(2\tau)$. If $V$ is bounded, write $q = r\sin\theta$ and $q_s = \omega r\cos\theta$ with $r\gt0$. Differentiating the first relation and comparing with the second gives $r_s\sin\theta = r\cos\theta\,(\omega - \theta_s)$; differentiating the second and using (5.3) gives $r_s\cos\theta = r\sin\theta\,(\theta_s - \omega + V/\omega)$. Multiplying the first by $\cos\theta$, the second by $\sin\theta$, and subtracting,
$$ \theta_s \;=\; \omega - \frac{V}{\omega}\sin^2\theta. $$
Take $\theta(0) = 0$. Where $q = 0$, $\sin\theta = 0$ and $\theta_s = \omega\gt0$, so $\theta$ crosses each multiple of $\pi$ once and upward, and $q(\tau) = 0$ with $n-1$ interior zeros means $\theta(\tau) = n\pi$. Integrating from $0$ to $\tau$,
$$ \left|\,\omega_n\tau - n\pi\,\right| \;=\; \frac{1}{\omega_n}\left|\int_0^\tau V\sin^2\theta\,ds\right| \;\le\; \frac{1}{\omega_n}\int_0^\tau|V|\,ds, $$
and dividing by $2\pi\tau$ with $\omega_n = 2\pi\nu_n$ gives (5.3a). The spacing $\nu_{n+1}-\nu_n$ differs from $1/(2\tau)$ by at most the sum of the two remainders, which is also of order $1/\nu_n$. ∎
373: The bound (5.3a) shows the rate: the departure from $n/(2\tau)$ falls as $1/\nu_n$, which is the form of the second-order term in (5.4). A sphere adds two things that the column lacks: the centre, where the radial equation is singular, and gravity, which makes the upper reflection occur at the cutoff of §4 rather than at a rigid end. Tassoul (1980) carried out the asymptotic analysis for a star. For modes of radial order $n$ and angular degree $\ell$ it gives

403: 7. The solar checks
405: The measured spacing (1.2) implies an acoustic radius $\tau = 1/(2\Delta\nu)$. With $\Delta\nu_{\rm obs} = 135.1\pm0.1\ \mu$Hz it is $3701.0\pm2.7$ s. With the asymptotic value $138.61\ \mu$Hz of (5.5) it is $3607.2$ s. Each check compares a closure with these two totals.
407: 7.1 Check 2: the isothermal Sun, refuted by a bound
409: An isothermal closure makes $c^2 = P/\rho$ at every depth. By (6.1), the tabulated interior alone then takes $3825.4$ s to cross. The untabulated layer adds a positive time, so the whole star takes longer, and
413: Check 2: the isothermal closure is refuted. Whatever the outer layer contains, an isothermal Sun with the tabulated $P$ and $\rho$ has $\Delta\nu\le130.70\ \mu$Hz. The measured spacing is $135.1\ \mu$Hz; the bound lies $4.40\ \mu$Hz below it, $3.25$ per cent of the measured value. The asymptotic spacing is $138.61\ \mu$Hz; the bound lies $7.91\ \mu$Hz below it, $5.71$ per cent of that value.
415: The conclusion does not depend on the second-order correction. Mosser et al.'s correction moves the comparison value away from the bound. A correction of the opposite sign would have to exceed $3.25$ per cent to save the isothermal closure, and the measured statistical uncertainty is $0.07$ per cent. The strength of the check comes from its being a bound. A best estimate for the layer would need a model of the layer; an upper limit needs only the fact that crossing times add.
417: The check refutes the isothermal closure given the model's $P$ and $\rho$ . It does not show that a different structure could not have an isothermal sound speed and the measured $\Delta\nu$. What it shows is that the Sun's measured spacing and the standard model's stratification together exclude Newton's closure, as the $331.45\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ excluded it in air.
419: 7.2 Check 3: the adiabatic interior, consistent
421: With $c^2 = \tfrac{5}{3}P/\rho$ the table takes $2963.2$ s, and the corresponding bound $\Delta\nu\le168.74\ \mu$Hz lies above both measured and asymptotic values, so the bound does not refute the adiabatic closure. The outer layer must then supply the remainder:
425: Check 3: consistent, not confirmed. With $\Gamma_1 = 5/3$ in the tabulated interior, the outer $1.69$ per cent of the radius must supply $644.0$ s of the $3607.2$ s acoustic radius (the asymptotic value), $17.9$ per cent of it, or $737.8$ s of $3701.0$ s ($19.9$ per cent) with the measured value. The remainder is positive, as the closure requires. A negative remainder would have refuted it.
427: A positive remainder is a weak test. It would be satisfied by any closure whose interior crossing time is shorter than the total, and it says nothing about whether $644$ s is the right amount. §7.3 asks whether a simple gas can supply that amount.
429: The adiabatic assumption is also an assumption about heat. By (3.4) it requires $\omega\chi/c^2\ll1$, with $\chi$ now the radiative diffusivity of the stellar gas. That criterion is not evaluated for the Sun in this module. It is expected to hold deep in the star, where photons diffuse slowly, and to fail near the photosphere, where they escape; neither statement is computed here, and the size of the failure is a subject of Module 13.

556: 8. What this model captures, and what it misses
558: The equations of this module are the linearised equations (2.6)–(2.8), applied to a uniform gas, to an isothermal atmosphere, and through the asymptotic relation (5.4) to a star. The table names each idealisation and where it is repaid.
561: Idealisation made here What it costs Where it is repaid
562: Linear: $|\mathbf{u}_1|\ll c$ Excludes steepening. A wave propagating upward above the cutoff grows as $\rho_0^{-1/2}$, by a factor of $33.0$ over $1000$ km of the solar atmosphere, and leaves the linear regime Module 8 (shocks)
563: Adiabatic perturbations Confirmed in air by Check 1. In the Sun, fails near the photosphere where radiation carries heat out of a compression within a period; this damps the modes and shifts their frequencies Module 13 (radiation hydrodynamics)
564: Vertical waves only in §4 Omits buoyancy, which acts on waves with a horizontal wavenumber and supports a second family of oscillations, gravity waves Module 6 (convection), through the buoyancy frequency
565: Gravity held fixed Omits the perturbation of gravity by $\rho_1$. Expected to be small for the high-order modes near $\nu_{\max}$ (not computed here); decisive for long wavelengths, where it drives collapse Module 5 (Jeans instability)
566: Isothermal atmosphere in §4.1 The cutoff depends on $T^{-1/2}$, and the real atmosphere has no single temperature. The measured $5000\ \mu$Hz needs $4669$ K Not repaid in this module
567: Asymptotic relation (5.4), quoted for a sphere Proved here only for a uniform sphere and a one-dimensional column. The second-order term moves the measured spacing by $\zeta_\odot\simeq2.6$ per cent Not repaid; Tassoul (1980) and Mosser et al. (2013)
568: $\Gamma_1$ assumed, not computed Check 4 shows $5/3$ is not enough in the outer layer but cannot measure the $\Gamma_1$ there. A solar model tabulated to the surface with its own $\Gamma_1$ would turn Check 3 into a direct test of $\Delta\nu$; Model S of Christensen-Dalsgaard et al. (1996) is one candidate, not examined here Not repaid in this module
569: No magnetic field, no rotation Omits magnetic tension and pressure as restoring forces, and the rotational splitting of mode frequencies Module 12 (magnetohydrodynamics, Alfvén waves)
572: One limitation belongs to the checks. Checks 2 to 4 combine a measured spacing with a model's $P$ and $\rho$. They test closures given that structure. Module 3's Check 3 showed that the same model misplaces the base of the convection zone by $0.0147\,R$, so its stratification is not exact. The isothermal refutation is $3.25$ per cent in $\Delta\nu$ against a bound, and a structural error in the model would have to shift the tabulated crossing time by at least that much in the right direction to reverse it. That has not been computed here.
