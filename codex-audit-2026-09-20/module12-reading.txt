116: 1. When is a fluid magnetised?
118: "Magnetised" is not one question. It is two , and the same gas can answer them differently: can the field push the gas? — which is the plasma beta — and does a particle remember the field between collisions? — which is the magnetisation $\omega_{\rm c}\tau$. The first is settled for all five census rows in this section; the second is a number this module does not need until §8 , where it is computed for the intracluster medium and comes out at $10^{12.71}$ , and it is defined here so that §8 's $(\omega_{\rm c}\tau)^{-2}$ arrives with its symbol already known.
120: Definition 1 (magnetic pressure, the plasma beta, and the Alfvén speed). For a field of strength $B$ in a gas of number density $n$ at temperature $T$ and mass density $\rho$, define
$$ p_{\rm mag} \equiv \frac{B^2}{8\pi}, \qquad \beta \equiv \frac{p_{\rm gas}}{p_{\rm mag}} = \frac{8\pi nk_{\rm B}T}{B^2}, \qquad v_{\rm A} \equiv \frac{B}{\sqrt{4\pi\rho}}. \tag{1.1}$$
$\beta$ asks whether the field can push the gas. $v_{\rm A}$ is a combination of $B$ and $\rho$ with the dimensions of a speed; that it is the speed of anything is not assumed here and is proved in Proposition 6 . Both $p_{\rm mag}$ and the tension that accompanies it are derived, not posited, in §3 .
124: Definition 2 (the magnetisation). $\omega_{\rm c}\tau$, where $\omega_{\rm c} = ZeB/mc$ is the gyrofrequency and $\tau$ the collision time, is the gyro-phase in radians that a particle turns between collisions. The collision time meant throughout this module is Braginskii's — his equations (2.5e) for electrons and (2.5i) for ions — because this module's §8 defines its transport coefficients on it. For the intracluster medium of §8 it comes out at $4.3\times10^{12}$, which is why §8 's suppression factor is $10^{-26}$ and not $10^{-2}$. §8.1 prints the one other collision time this book uses, says exactly how the two differ, and accounts for the difference exactly, as $\sqrt{3/2}$ times a ratio of Coulomb logarithms.
126: Proposition 1 (the two speeds are one ratio). For an ideal gas with ratio of specific heats $\gamma$,
$$ \frac{v_{\rm A}}{c_s} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma\beta}} \tag{1.2}$$
identically, where $c_s = \sqrt{\gamma p_{\rm gas}/\rho}$. The mass per particle cancels.
130: $v_{\rm A}^2/c_s^2 = (B^2/4\pi\rho)\big/(\gamma p_{\rm gas}/\rho) = B^2/(4\pi\gamma p_{\rm gas})$. The density has cancelled, and $B^2/4\pi = 2p_{\rm mag}$, so the ratio is $2p_{\rm mag}/(\gamma p_{\rm gas}) = 2/(\gamma\beta)$. ∎
134: This is why $\beta$ is the census variable and $v_{\rm A}$ is not. The Alfvén speed needs a mean mass per particle and therefore a composition; $\beta$ needs neither. The table below prints both, and prints (1.2) beside the ratio of two separately computed speeds, so that if a $\mu$ is ever changed in one place and not the other the last column stops reading $1.0000$.

222: 2. The induction equation, and why flux freezing is a number
224: The field obeys Maxwell's equations; the gas obeys Module 2 's. What couples them is Ohm's law, and what comes out is a single equation for $\mathbf B$ with exactly two terms.
226: Proposition 2 (the induction equation). For a fluid of uniform electrical conductivity $\sigma_{\rm e}$ moving non-relativistically,
$$ \frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} \;=\; \nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B) \;+\; \eta_{\rm m}\nabla^2\mathbf B, \qquad \eta_{\rm m}\equiv\frac{c^2}{4\pi\sigma_{\rm e}}, \tag{2.1}$$
with $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ preserved for all time if it holds once.
230: Ohm's law in the frame of the moving fluid is $\mathbf j = \sigma_{\rm e}(\mathbf E + \mathbf u\times\mathbf B/c)$, so $\mathbf E = \mathbf j/\sigma_{\rm e} - \mathbf u\times\mathbf B/c$. Ampère's law without the displacement current gives $\mathbf j = (c/4\pi)\nabla\times\mathbf B$. Substituting both into Faraday's law $\partial\mathbf B/\partial t = -c\,\nabla\times\mathbf E$,
$$ \frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} = \nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B) - \frac{c^2}{4\pi\sigma_{\rm e}}\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B), $$
and $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B) - \nabla^2\mathbf B = -\nabla^2\mathbf B$. Taking the divergence of (2.1) gives $\partial(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)/\partial t = \eta_{\rm m}\nabla^2(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)$, a diffusion equation for $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B$; if it starts at zero it stays there. ∎
236: Dropping the displacement current is an ordering, and here is the ordering. $|\partial_t\mathbf E/c|$ against $|\nabla\times\mathbf B|$ is of order $(U/c)^2$ once $E \sim UB/c$ is used, so the term is smaller than the one kept by $(U/c)^2$. For the fastest flow in this module's census, the solar wind at $435.6\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, that is $2.1\times10^{-6}$. It is an ordering and not an identity , and it fails for any relativistic flow — which is why this module's equations do not apply to a pulsar magnetosphere or a jet, and §11 says so.
238: Proposition 3 (the magnetic Reynolds number, and why size is the variable). The ratio of the two terms of (2.1) on a flow of speed $U$ over a scale $L$ is
$$ \mathrm{R_m} \;\equiv\; \frac{UL}{\eta_{\rm m}}. \tag{2.2}$$
With Braginskii's $\sigma_\parallel = 1.96\,n_{\rm e}e^2\tau_{\rm e}/m_{\rm e}$ and his $\tau_{\rm e}\propto T^{3/2}/(n\ln\Lambda)$, the density cancels and
$$ \eta_{\rm m} \;\propto\; \frac{\ln\Lambda}{T^{3/2}} \tag{2.3}$$
depends on the temperature and the Coulomb logarithm alone . Moreover
$$ \frac{L^2/\eta_{\rm m}}{L/U} \;=\; \frac{UL}{\eta_{\rm m}} \;=\; \mathrm{R_m} \tag{2.4}$$
identically: the ohmic decay time measured in the system's own dynamical times is the magnetic Reynolds number.
246: The inductive term is of order $UB/L$ and the resistive term of order $\eta_{\rm m}B/L^2$; their ratio is $UL/\eta_{\rm m}$. For (2.3), $\sigma_\parallel \propto n\tau_{\rm e}$ and $\tau_{\rm e}\propto T^{3/2}/(n\ln\Lambda)$, so $\sigma_\parallel\propto T^{3/2}/\ln\Lambda$ and $\eta_{\rm m} = c^2/4\pi\sigma_\parallel \propto \ln\Lambda/T^{3/2}$. Equation (2.4) is arithmetic: $(L^2/\eta_{\rm m})(U/L) = UL/\eta_{\rm m}$. ∎
250: Equation (2.3) is the reason astrophysical plasmas are good conductors, and it is not the reason a reader expects. A large plasma is a good conductor through its size, not through its density. Two gases at the same temperature have the same resistivity whether one is a million times denser than the other; what differs is $L$, and $L$ enters $\mathrm{R_m}$ linearly. The table makes the point, and its last column is (2.4) used as a self-check on the column above it.

And §7 's mass-to-flux criterion is an equilibrium statement whose missing mechanism is precisely this one: a subcritical cloud leaks flux through ambipolar diffusion and becomes supercritical , and that is how a cloud the field can hold today collapses tomorrow. 262: Proposition 4 (Alfvén's theorem). If $\eta_{\rm m} = 0$, the magnetic flux
$$ \Phi(t) \;=\; \int_{S(t)}\mathbf B\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf S \tag{2.5}$$
through any surface $S(t)$ carried with the fluid is constant in time.
266: A surface moving with the fluid changes the flux through it in two ways: the field changes in place, and the boundary sweeps through the field. The transport theorem for a surface integral gives
$$ \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t} = \int_{S}\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf S \;-\; \oint_{\partial S}(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol\ell, $$
where the second term is the sweeping, written with the sign that follows from $\mathrm{d}\mathbf S = \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf u\,\mathrm{d}t$ on the swept strip. Stokes's theorem turns the line integral into $\int_S\nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf S$, so
$$ \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t} = \int_{S}\Big[\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} - \nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)\Big]\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf S = \int_{S}\eta_{\rm m}\nabla^2\mathbf B\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf S $$
by (2.1), which vanishes when $\eta_{\rm m} = 0$. The surface was arbitrary. ∎
274: Flux freezing is a measurement, not an idealisation. The smallest $\mathrm{R_m}$ in the table is the solar corona's, $1.024\times10^{12}$, which is $\mathbf{12.0}$ decades. By (2.4) that is the number of dynamical times a coronal loop would need in order to lose its field by ohmic decay. The word "decades" is printed beside the exponent that produced it , because a phrase like "many orders of magnitude" cannot be checked and would have been wrong: this book's own build sessions have three times printed an order-of-magnitude phrase that no saved run supports, and a phrase, unlike an exponent, is read by no mechanical check.
276: The result of Proposition 4 is Alfvén's, in a one-page note of 1942. That paper was not fetched for this book and is cited for the result only , in the same way Module 1 cites Maxwell (1867); its volume, pages and date were confirmed at Crossref and nothing else about it is claimed here. The proof above is this module's own.
278: 3. Magnetic pressure and magnetic tension
280: The field acts on the gas through the Lorentz force on the current it carries. What that force is , mechanically, is the content of this section, and it is what Module 2 §11 , Module 3 §11 , Module 4 §8 , Module 5 §8 and Module 7 §5 have each been promised.
282: Proposition 5 (the Lorentz force splits into a pressure and a tension). The force per unit volume on a conducting fluid is
$$ \mathbf f = \frac{1}{c}\,\mathbf j\times\mathbf B = \frac{1}{4\pi}(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf B = -\nabla\!\left(\frac{B^2}{8\pi}\right) + \frac{(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B}{4\pi}. \tag{3.1}$$
The first term is an isotropic pressure $p_{\rm mag} = B^2/8\pi$. The second is a tension $B^2/4\pi$ along the field; where a field line has radius of curvature $R_c$ it contributes a force per volume $B^2/(4\pi R_c)$ directed toward the centre of curvature.
286: Use the identity $\nabla(\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf B) = 2(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B + 2\mathbf B\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B)$, so that
$$ (\nabla\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf B = -\mathbf B\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B) = (\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B - \tfrac12\nabla B^2. $$
Dividing by $4\pi$ gives (3.1). For the curvature, write $\mathbf B = B\hat{\mathbf b}$ with $\hat{\mathbf b}$ a unit vector. Then
$$ \frac{(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B}{4\pi} = \frac{B}{4\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\ell}\big(B\hat{\mathbf b}\big) = \hat{\mathbf b}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\ell}\!\left(\frac{B^2}{8\pi}\right) + \frac{B^2}{4\pi}\frac{\partial\hat{\mathbf b}}{\partial\ell}, $$
where $\partial/\partial\ell$ is the derivative along the field line. The first piece is a pressure gradient along $\hat{\mathbf b}$, which exactly cancels the field-parallel part of $-\nabla(B^2/8\pi)$ — so the magnetic pressure acts only across the field, never along it . The second piece uses the Frenet relation $\partial\hat{\mathbf b}/\partial\ell = -\hat{\mathbf n}/R_c$ with $\hat{\mathbf n}$ pointing away from the centre of curvature, giving a force $B^2/(4\pi R_c)$ toward it. ∎
294: The cancellation in that proof is the whole physical content, and it is easy to read past. A magnetic field is not a gas with pressure $B^2/8\pi$. Along its own direction the pressure term is cancelled exactly, and a straight uniform field exerts no force at all. What a field resists is being squeezed (the pressure, acting across the lines) and being bent (the tension, acting along them). Every result in §4 to §9 is one of these two, or both.

307: 4. Three wave speeds, and what one of them costs Module 7
309: Module 4 derived sound from a gas whose only restoring force was its own pressure, and its captures-and-misses table names magnetic pressure and tension as the restoring forces it left out. Adding them turns one wave into three.
311: Proposition 6 (the three MHD wave speeds). Linearising the ideal MHD equations about a uniform state with field $\mathbf B_0$, for a plane wave with wavevector at angle $\theta$ to $\mathbf B_0$, the phase speeds are
$$ v_{\rm Alfv\acute en} = v_{\rm A}\cos\theta, \qquad v_{\pm}^2 = \tfrac12\left(v_{\rm A}^2 + c_s^2\right) \pm \tfrac12\sqrt{\left(v_{\rm A}^2+c_s^2\right)^2 - 4v_{\rm A}^2c_s^2\cos^2\theta}, \tag{4.1}$$
with $v_+$ the fast and $v_-$ the slow magnetosonic speed. At $\theta = 0$, $v_+ = \max(v_{\rm A},c_s)$ and $v_- = \min(v_{\rm A},c_s)$. At $\theta = 90^\circ$ the slow and Alfvén speeds both vanish and only the fast wave survives, at $\sqrt{v_{\rm A}^2+c_s^2}$.
315: Write $\rho = \rho_0+\rho_1$, $\mathbf u = \mathbf u_1$, $\mathbf B = \mathbf B_0 + \mathbf B_1$ and keep first order. Continuity and the adiabatic closure give $p_1 = c_s^2\rho_1$; the induction equation (2.1) at $\eta_{\rm m}=0$ gives $\partial_t\mathbf B_1 = \nabla\times(\mathbf u_1\times\mathbf B_0)$; and the momentum equation with (3.1) gives
$$ \rho_0\,\partial_t\mathbf u_1 = -\nabla p_1 - \nabla\!\left(\frac{\mathbf B_0\cdot\mathbf B_1}{4\pi}\right) + \frac{(\mathbf B_0\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B_1}{4\pi}. $$
Substituting $\propto\exp[i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x-\omega t)]$ turns continuity into $\rho_1 = \rho_0(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1)/\omega$ and the induction equation into
$$ \mathbf B_1 = \frac{\mathbf B_0(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1) - \mathbf u_1(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf B_0)}{\omega}. $$
Putting both into the momentum equation and writing $\mathbf v_{\rm A} \equiv \mathbf B_0/\sqrt{4\pi\rho_0}$, so that $|\mathbf v_{\rm A}| = v_{\rm A}$, gives one vector equation in $\mathbf u_1$ alone:
$$ \omega^2\mathbf u_1 = \left(c_s^2+v_{\rm A}^2\right)(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1)\,\mathbf k \;+\; (\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v_{\rm A})\Big[(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v_{\rm A})\mathbf u_1 - (\mathbf v_{\rm A}\cdot\mathbf u_1)\mathbf k - (\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1)\mathbf v_{\rm A}\Big]. \tag{4.1a}$$
Now choose axes: $\mathbf B_0 = B_0\hat{\mathbf z}$ and $\mathbf k = k(\sin\theta,\,0,\,\cos\theta)$, so $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v_{\rm A} = kv_{\rm A}\cos\theta$. The $y$ component decouples. $u_y$ appears in neither $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1$ nor $\mathbf v_{\rm A}\cdot\mathbf u_1$, and $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf v_{\rm A}$ have no $y$ component, so every term on the right of (4.1a) except the first inside the bracket vanishes for it: $\omega^2u_y = k^2v_{\rm A}^2\cos^2\theta\,u_y$. This motion is perpendicular to both $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf B_0$, so it compresses nothing — $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1 = 0$ means $\rho_1 = 0$ and $p_1 = 0$ — and the only restoring force left is the tension. That is the Alfvén wave, and the proof shows what it is: a transverse wave on a string under tension $B^2/4\pi$ with mass per unit length $\rho$, whose speed $\sqrt{\rm tension/\rho}$ is exactly $v_{\rm A}$ — which is the promised justification for calling $v_{\rm A}$ a speed. The $x$ and $z$ components remain, and they lie in the plane of $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf B_0$. Write $v \equiv \omega/k$ and $\Delta \equiv \sin\theta\,u_x + \cos\theta\,u_z$, so $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1 = k\Delta$. The $z$ component of (4.1a) is
$$ v^2u_z = (c_s^2+v_{\rm A}^2)\Delta\cos\theta + v_{\rm A}^2\cos^2\theta\,u_z - v_{\rm A}^2\cos^2\theta\,u_z - v_{\rm A}^2\cos\theta\,\Delta = c_s^2\cos\theta\,\Delta, $$
the two tension terms in $u_z$ cancelling exactly — a field does nothing to motion along itself , which is §3 's cancellation seen again. The $x$ component is
$$ v^2u_x = (c_s^2+v_{\rm A}^2)\Delta\sin\theta + v_{\rm A}^2\cos^2\theta\,u_x - v_{\rm A}^2\cos\theta\sin\theta\,u_z. $$
Collecting, and using $(c_s^2+v_{\rm A}^2)\sin^2\theta + v_{\rm A}^2\cos^2\theta = c_s^2\sin^2\theta + v_{\rm A}^2$,
$$ \begin{pmatrix} v^2 - c_s^2\sin^2\theta - v_{\rm A}^2 & -c_s^2\sin\theta\cos\theta \\ -c_s^2\sin\theta\cos\theta & v^2 - c_s^2\cos^2\theta \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u_x\\ u_z\end{pmatrix} = 0, $$
and a non-trivial solution needs the determinant to vanish:
$$ \big(v^2 - c_s^2\sin^2\theta - v_{\rm A}^2\big)\big(v^2 - c_s^2\cos^2\theta\big) - c_s^4\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta = 0. $$
The two $c_s^4\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta$ terms cancel, leaving
$$ v^4 - v^2\left(v_{\rm A}^2+c_s^2\right) + v_{\rm A}^2c_s^2\cos^2\theta = 0, $$
a quadratic in $v^2$ whose two roots are (4.1). At $\cos\theta = 1$ it factorises as $(v^2-v_{\rm A}^2)(v^2-c_s^2)=0$; at $\cos\theta = 0$ the constant term vanishes, so one root is $0$ and the other $v_{\rm A}^2+c_s^2$. ∎

360: 5. The anchor: the Parker spiral
362: Module 9 built a wind that leaves the Sun radially and is still accelerating at 1 au. The Sun rotates. A field line is frozen to the gas by Proposition 4 and its foot is carried round by the rotation while its body streams outward — so the line is drawn into a spiral, and the shape of that spiral is fixed by two numbers this book already has: the solar rotation rate and the wind speed. Nothing is fitted.
364: Proposition 7 (the Parker spiral). For a steady, spherically symmetric, radial wind of speed $v(r)$ from a source surface at $r_0$, on a star rotating at angular rate $\Omega$, the frozen-in field has
$$ B_r(r) = B_r(r_0)\left(\frac{r_0}{r}\right)^{2}, \qquad \frac{B_\phi}{B_r} = -\,\frac{\Omega\,(r-r_0)\sin\theta}{v}, \tag{5.1}$$
with $\theta$ the colatitude. The first follows from $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ alone and is exact ; the second is the kinematics of a line whose foot rotates.
368: The radial part. $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ integrated over a sphere of radius $r$ gives $\oint B_r\,\mathrm{d}S = 4\pi r^2\langle B_r\rangle = $ constant, since no flux is created between two spheres. Hence $B_r r^2$ is constant. No dynamics entered , which is why this half is exact and is the half the control in Check 1 tests. The azimuthal part. Work in the frame rotating with the star, in which the flow is steady. There the velocity is $\mathbf v' = v\hat{\mathbf r} - \Omega(r-r_0)\sin\theta\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$ — Parker's equation (24), with $r_0$ his $b$ — and in a steady state the field must be parallel to the flow , for otherwise the flow would sweep the field into a configuration that changes with time. Hence $\mathbf B \parallel \mathbf v'$, so
$$ \frac{B_\phi}{B_r} = \frac{v'_\phi}{v'_r} = -\frac{\Omega(r-r_0)\sin\theta}{v}, $$
which is Parker's equation (26). ∎
375: Parker's $(r-r_0)$ is carried, and the module prints what dropping it would cost. His (26) reads $B_\phi = B(\theta,\phi_0)(\omega/v_m)(r-b)(b/r)^2\sin\theta$. The familiar $\Omega r\sin\theta/v$ is his large-$r$ limit , stated as a limit, and his (27) places the $45^\circ$ surface at $r\simeq(v_m/\omega)\sin\theta$ only after $b$ has been dropped. Printing $\Omega r\sin\theta/v$ while citing (26) is the one thing that is not defensible , so this module does neither. At 1 au with $r_0 = 2.5\,R_\odot$, carrying $r_0$ lowers $-B_\phi/B_r$ from $0.9840$ to $0.9726$, by $1.16$ per cent; dropping it raises the ratio by $1.18$ per cent. The two figures are one term seen from its two ends, and $r_0/1\,\mathrm{au} = 0.01163$ is why. The source surface is written $r_0$ throughout, because $b$ is Module 1 's impact parameter and Module 10 's forcing parameter.
377: The observable consequence is the total field's radial falloff. Writing $x \equiv \Omega(r-r_0)\sin\theta/v$,
$$ |\mathbf B| = B_r\sqrt{1+x^2}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln|\mathbf B|}{\mathrm{d}\ln r} = -2 + \frac{x^2}{1+x^2}\left(\frac{r}{r-r_0} - \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln v}{\mathrm{d}\ln r}\right). \tag{5.2}$$ Fig. 2 draws both halves of this. At small $r$ the field is nearly radial, $x\ll1$, and the index is $-2$. At large $r$ it is nearly azimuthal, $x\gg1$, and the index tends to $-1$. Between the two, over the range a spacecraft actually flew, the index is something in between — and that is a measurement.

464: 6. The Alfvén surface, bracketed by two measurements
466: Close to the Sun the field is strong and the wind slow, so $v \lt  v_{\rm A}$ and the field controls the flow: it can enforce corotation, and it carries away angular momentum. Far out the wind is fast and the field weak, so $v \gt  v_{\rm A}$ and the flow drags the field, which is what §5 just measured. In between there is a surface where the two are equal, and it is the boundary between those two regimes.
468: Proposition 8 (the wind crosses its own radial Alfvén speed exactly once). For the wind of Proposition 7, mass conservation gives $\rho \propto r^{-2}v^{-1}$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ gives $B_r\propto r^{-2}$, so the radial Alfvén speed obeys
$$ v_{\rm A,r} \;=\; \frac{B_r}{\sqrt{4\pi\rho}} \;\propto\; \frac{r^{-2}}{\sqrt{r^{-2}v^{-1}}} \;=\; r^{-1}v^{1/2}. \tag{6.1}$$
If $v$ is non-decreasing outward, $v/v_{\rm A,r}\propto r\,v^{1/2}$ is strictly increasing, so the equation $v = v_{\rm A,r}$ has exactly one root $r_{\rm A}$.
472: $\dot M = 4\pi r^2\rho v$ constant gives $\rho\propto r^{-2}v^{-1}$; substituting into $B_r/\sqrt{4\pi\rho}$ with $B_r\propto r^{-2}$ gives (6.1). Then $v/v_{\rm A,r}\propto v\cdot r\,v^{-1/2} = r\,v^{1/2}$, a product of two non-decreasing positive factors one of which is strictly increasing. A strictly increasing continuous function crosses the value $1$ at most once, and does so at least once given that it is $\ll1$ at the base and $\gg1$ at 1 au. ∎
476: The Alfvén speed in (6.1) takes $B_r$ and not $|\mathbf B|$, and the first draft of this module's generator used $|\mathbf B|$. Two reasons, either of which settles it. (i) §5 has just proved that $|\mathbf B|$ does not scale as $r^{-2}$; $B_r$ does, by $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$, and it is the $r^{-2}$ scaling the extrapolation inward uses. (ii) The measured quantity is the Weber–Davis Alfvén radius, which Verscharen, Bale & Velli define on the radial Alfvén speed, because it is the radial components that enter the flow deflection they fit. Using $|\mathbf B|$ inflated every radius below by $\sqrt{1+x_1^2}$, a factor near $1.40$, and changed the module's headline.

547: 7. Magnetic support against collapse
549: Module 5 §8 is owed this: magnetic pressure and tension add support against collapse, and the statement that results is not an extra pressure term but a critical mass-to-flux ratio . Below it no field strength is enough; above it the cloud is held for as long as the flux stays with the gas.
551: Definition 3 (the mass-to-flux ratio). For a cloud of mass $M$ threaded by magnetic flux $\Phi$,
$$ \lambda_\Phi \;\equiv\; \frac{M/\Phi}{(M/\Phi)_{\rm crit}}, \qquad \left(\frac{M}{\Phi}\right)_{\rm crit} = \frac{c_\Phi}{\sqrt G}. \tag{7.1}$$
$\lambda_\Phi \gt  1$ is supercritical : the field cannot hold the cloud. The symbol carries its subscript because bare $\lambda$ is Module 1 's mean free path book-wide, and both appear in this module.
555: Proposition 9 (the critical ratio is a pure number). For a flux-frozen, self-gravitating cloud, the gravitational and magnetic energies both scale as $1/R$ at fixed $M$ and $\Phi$, so their ratio is independent of the radius and the criterion for collapse is a condition on $M/\Phi$ alone. The coefficient is
$$ c_\Phi = \frac{c_1\sqrt5}{3\pi} = 0.125745 \quad\text{(uniform sphere)}, \qquad c_\Phi = \frac{1}{2\pi} = 0.159155 \quad\text{(flattened cloud)}. \tag{7.2}$$
559: The form is derived. For a uniform sphere of mass $M$ and radius $R$ the gravitational energy is $W = -3GM^2/5R$. Flux freezing fixes $\Phi = \pi R^2\langle B\rangle$, so $\langle B\rangle = \Phi/\pi R^2$ and the magnetic energy inside the cloud is $\mathcal M = (B^2/8\pi)(4\pi R^3/3) = \Phi^2/(6\pi^2R)$. Both go as $1/R$ , so their sum is either positive or negative for all $R$ at once: compressing the cloud cannot change the sign. The virial condition $\mathcal M + W = 0$ therefore gives a critical ratio and not a critical radius,
$$ \frac{\Phi^2}{6\pi^2R} = \frac{3GM^2}{5R} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{M}{\Phi} = \frac{1}{3\pi}\sqrt{\frac{5}{2G}} = \frac{0.16777}{\sqrt G}. $$ That is the estimate this argument produces, and it is not the normalisation (7.2) uses. Mouschovias & Spitzer's equation (2) is written against $(1/3\pi)\sqrt{5/G} = 0.23726/\sqrt G$, which is $\sqrt2$ larger — it counts a magnetic energy twice the energy of the field inside the cloud, and where that second factor of two comes from is not derived here and is not derived anywhere in this book. Their $c_1$ is measured against their normalisation and not against the estimate above , so $c_1 = 0.53$ is not the size of the correction to this line: the ratio of their coefficient to the estimate above is $0.125745/0.16777 = 0.7495$. Every number in this module uses their formula with their $c_1$, so none of them turns on this. The coefficients are stated, not derived. Mouschovias & Spitzer (1976) show that the virial estimate is an approximation and fit a correction factor to exact equilibrium models: their equation (2) reads $M_{c\Phi} = (c_1/3\pi)(5/G)^{1/2}\Phi$ and their equation (4) gives $c_1 = 0.53$, whence $c_\Phi = 0.53\sqrt5/3\pi = 0.125745$. Nakano & Nakamura (1978) solve the flattened case and find the disc stable when $\sigma_0/B_0 \lt  (4\pi^2G)^{-1/2}$ — a column density over a field — so $c_\Phi = 1/2\pi$ exactly. ∎

Neither module puts a number on the conduction half : Module 1 's Problem K3 compares conduction with the sound-crossing time and finds it slow, and says in the same solution that the comparison the cooling-flow problem needs is against the radiative cooling time, which this book has not made. So the second problem arrives here as a scaling and a suspicion, not as a measured discrepancy. It said the two "have the same suspected resolution — magnetic suppression of transport across field lines — and Module 12 treats both together ." This section is that treatment, and it ends by refuting the classical form of the resolution.
599: Proposition 10 (perpendicular transport is suppressed by $(\omega_{\rm c}\tau)^{-2}$). In an unmagnetised gas a random walk of step $\lambda$ per collision time $\tau$ gives a diffusivity $\sim\lambda^2/\tau$. Across a field, a particle cannot travel $\lambda$ between collisions: it returns to within a gyroradius of where it started. The step is therefore $r_g$ rather than $\lambda$, and
$$ \frac{D_\perp}{D_\parallel} \;\sim\; \frac{r_g^2}{\lambda^2} \;=\; \frac{1}{(\omega_{\rm c}\tau)^2}, \tag{8.1}$$
since $\lambda = v\tau$ and $r_g = v/\omega_{\rm c}$. The same factor governs the conductivity and the viscosity, with different numerical coefficients.
603: Between collisions the guiding centre is displaced along $\mathbf B$ by $\lambda$ and across it by at most $2r_g$; the perpendicular displacement per collision is therefore $O(r_g)$ and uncorrelated from one collision to the next. A random walk of $N$ steps of size $s$ in time $N\tau$ has diffusivity $s^2/\tau$, so $D_\perp/D_\parallel = r_g^2/\lambda^2$. With $\lambda = v\tau$ and $r_g = v/\omega_{\rm c}$ the speed cancels and the ratio is $(\omega_{\rm c}\tau)^{-2}$. ∎
607: Braginskii (1965) does the calculation properly and supplies the coefficients. For $Z = 1$ his equations (2.12) and (2.13) on p. 217 give the electron conduction,
$$ \kappa_\parallel = 3.16\,\frac{n_{\rm e}T_{\rm e}\tau_{\rm e}}{m_{\rm e}}, \qquad \kappa_\perp = 4.66\,\frac{n_{\rm e}T_{\rm e}}{m_{\rm e}\omega_{\rm e}^2\tau_{\rm e}}, \qquad \frac{\kappa_\perp}{\kappa_\parallel} = \frac{4.66}{3.16}\frac{1}{(\omega_{\rm e}\tau_{\rm e})^2}, \tag{8.2}$$
and his equations (2.22) and (2.23) on p. 218 give the ion viscosity, which Module 10 §6.2 already uses, with $\eta_0^{\rm B} = 0.96\,n_iT_i\tau_i$ and $\eta_1^{\rm B} = (3/10)\,n_iT_i/(\omega_i^2\tau_i)$.

696: 9. The magnetorotational instability
698: Module 8 §6.3 names the magnetorotational instability in its list of what this module builds, so it is not optional. It is done locally : the shear is assumed, not derived from a disc model, because disc structure — the $\alpha$ prescription, the thin-disc solution — is Module 11.
700: The problem it solves is stated in one line. A Keplerian disc has angular velocity $\Omega\propto R^{-3/2}$, so its specific angular momentum $\ell = \Omega R^2 \propto R^{1/2}$ increases outward. By the Rayleigh criterion that is hydrodynamically stable : displace a ring outward and it has too little angular momentum to stay there, and it falls back. A purely hydrodynamic Keplerian disc does not transport angular momentum, and therefore does not accrete. Adding the weakest imaginable magnetic field destroys that stability.
702: Proposition 11 (the local MRI dispersion relation). Let $q \equiv -\mathrm{d}\ln\Omega/\mathrm{d}\ln R$, so $q = 3/2$ for a Keplerian disc, and let the epicyclic frequency be $\kappa_{\rm ep}^2 = 2(2-q)\Omega^2$. For an axisymmetric, incompressible perturbation with wavevector $\mathbf k\parallel\hat{\mathbf z}$ along a weak vertical field, writing $\sigma \equiv \gamma/\Omega$ and $x \equiv kv_{\rm A}/\Omega$,
$$ \sigma^4 + \sigma^2\!\left(\frac{\kappa_{\rm ep}^2}{\Omega^2} + 2x^2\right) + x^2\!\left(x^2 - 4 + \frac{\kappa_{\rm ep}^2}{\Omega^2}\right) = 0. \tag{9.1}$$
There is a growing root whenever $x^2 \lt  2q$. At $q = 3/2$ this gives instability for $kv_{\rm A} \lt  \sqrt3\,\Omega$, a maximum growth rate
$$ \gamma_{\max} = \frac{q}{2}\,\Omega = 0.75\,\Omega \quad\text{at}\quad kv_{\rm A} = \frac{\sqrt{15}}{4}\,\Omega = 0.96825\,\Omega, \tag{9.2}$$
and marginal stability at $kv_{\rm A} = \sqrt3\,\Omega = 1.73205\,\Omega$.
708: Work in a local frame corotating at $R_0$ with coordinates $(x_1,x_2,x_3)$ along $(\hat{\mathbf R},\hat{\boldsymbol\phi},\hat{\mathbf z})$, and linearise the ideal MHD equations for displacements $\boldsymbol\xi\propto\exp(\gamma t + ikx_3)$. In the shearing sheet the equations of motion for a displacement are $\ddot\xi_1 = 2\Omega\dot\xi_2 + 2q\Omega^2\xi_1 + f_1$ and $\ddot\xi_2 = -2\Omega\dot\xi_1 + f_2$: the Coriolis coupling is $2\Omega$ in both , and the background shear appears once, as the tidal term $2q\Omega^2\xi_1$. A vertical field contributes a tension $f_i = -(kv_{\rm A})^2\xi_i$ to each in-plane component — the field lines act as springs joining the fluid element to its unperturbed radius. So
$$ (\gamma^2 + k^2v_{\rm A}^2 - 2q\Omega^2)\,\xi_1 - 2\Omega\gamma\,\xi_2 = 0, \qquad 2\Omega\gamma\,\xi_1 + (\gamma^2 + k^2v_{\rm A}^2)\,\xi_2 = 0, $$
and the determinant condition is
$$ (\gamma^2 + k^2v_{\rm A}^2 - 2q\Omega^2)(\gamma^2 + k^2v_{\rm A}^2) + 4\Omega^2\gamma^2 = 0, $$
which is (9.1) after dividing by $\Omega^4$ and using $\kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2 = 2(2-q) = 4-2q$. The field-free limit is the check that this pair is the right one. At $k = 0$ it reduces to $\sigma^2(\sigma^2 + 4 - 2q) = 0$, so $\sigma^2 = 2q-4 = -\kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2$, which is $-1$ for a Keplerian disc: an oscillation at the epicyclic frequency and no growth. A Keplerian disc with no field is stable, as the opening of this section says, and any linearisation that does not reproduce that is wrong. The instability condition. Equation (9.1) is a quadratic in $\sigma^2$ with positive coefficient on $\sigma^2$, so it has a positive root for $\sigma^2$ if and only if its constant term is negative, that is if and only if $x^2 - 4 + \kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2 \lt  0$. Substituting $\kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2 = 2(2-q)$ gives $x^2 \lt  2q$, so $x \lt  \sqrt3$ at $q = 3/2$. The maximum. Differentiate (9.1) implicitly with respect to $x^2$ and set $\mathrm{d}\sigma^2/\mathrm{d}x^2 = 0$: the surviving condition is $2\sigma^2 + 2x^2 - 4 + \kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2 = 0$. With $q = 3/2$, $\kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2 = 1$, this is $\sigma^2 = (3 - 2x^2)/2$. Substituting into (9.1) and solving gives $x^2 = 15/16$ and $\sigma^2 = 9/16$, hence $\sigma = 3/4$ and $x = \sqrt{15}/4$. ∎
719: Two things about this result that the algebra hides. (i) There is no threshold field. The unstable band is $kv_{\rm A} \lt  \sqrt3\,\Omega$, so a weaker field moves the band to larger $k$ — shorter wavelengths — without closing it. The instability disappears only when the longest unstable wavelength no longer fits inside the disc. That is why the condition that matters in practice is $\lambda_{\rm marg} \lt  H$ and not a condition on $B$ alone. (ii) The growth rate does not depend on the field at all — Fig. 5 is flat-topped at $0.75\,\Omega$ whatever the disc.  $\gamma_{\max} = q\Omega/2$ contains no $B$, no $n$ and no $T$. A weaker field changes which wavelength grows fastest, not how fast it grows. In e-folds per orbit that is $2\pi(q/2) = 3\pi/2 = 4.712$ for every Keplerian disc — an identity, not a property of any particular one.
765: The growing root of (9.1) at $q = 3/2$. The curve rises from zero, peaks at $\gamma = 0.75\,\Omega$ where $kv_{\rm A} = \sqrt{15}/4\,\Omega$, and reaches zero at $\sqrt3\,\Omega$, beyond which there is no growth at all — a hard upper edge in wavenumber, which is a hard lower edge in wavelength. The two dotted lines mark, for the worked discs below, the wavenumber whose wavelength is exactly one scale height; both lie inside the unstable band, so both discs have unstable modes that fit. A disc is stabilised when its dotted line passes to the right of $\sqrt3$.

790: Proposition 12 (critical balance gives the same exponent, and an anisotropy K41 does not have). Suppose an Alfvénic cascade keeps the linear Alfvén crossing time and the nonlinear turnover time equal at every scale:
$$ k_\parallel v_{\rm A} \;\sim\; k_\perp\,\delta v_\perp. \tag{10.1}$$
If the perpendicular cascade is Kolmogorov, $\delta v_\perp \sim (\varepsilon/k_\perp)^{1/3}$, then
$$ k_\parallel \;\sim\; k_\perp^{2/3}L^{-1/3}, \qquad E(k_\perp) \;\propto\; k_\perp^{-5/3}. \tag{10.2}$$
796: Substituting $\delta v_\perp \sim \varepsilon^{1/3}k_\perp^{-1/3}$ into (10.1) gives $k_\parallel \sim k_\perp^{2/3}\varepsilon^{1/3}/v_{\rm A}$; writing $\varepsilon \sim v_{\rm A}^3/L$ at the outer scale, where the balance is imposed, gives $\varepsilon^{1/3}/v_{\rm A} = L^{-1/3}$ and hence the first of (10.2). For the second, the perpendicular energy spectrum follows from $\delta v_\perp^2 \sim k_\perp E(k_\perp)$ with $\delta v_\perp \propto k_\perp^{-1/3}$, so $E \propto k_\perp^{-5/3}$. ∎
800: The exponent is not the discriminant. The anisotropy is, and this book does not measure it. K41 and critical balance predict the same $-5/3$ from different premises, so a measured $-5/3$ selects neither. What distinguishes them is the first half of (10.2): K41 predicts isotropic eddies, and critical balance predicts eddies that grow more elongated along the field at every smaller scale, with $k_\parallel \propto k_\perp^{2/3}$. No anisotropy measurement was read for this book, and this module therefore declares the discriminating measurement outside it. Module 10 's four indices — $1.66$, $1.72$, $1.66$, $1.58$, mean $1.6550$ against $5/3$, ratio $0.9930$ — are reprinted here so that the two modules quote one set, and no new check is built on them .

This precludes application to the solar wind in which the outward flux significantly exceeds the ingoing one. 825: 11.1 The point Module 1 asked this module to return to
827: Module 1 §8 observed that the magnetic rescue of the fluid description is partial, and partial in a measurable way. A field constrains motion across itself and does nothing to motion along itself, so a magnetised collisionless plasma is not an ordinary fluid with a scalar pressure: it has $p_\parallel$ along the field and $p_\perp$ across it, and the two relax toward each other only through collisions the plasma is not having. The resulting description is the Chew–Goldberger–Low or double-adiabatic theory. Pressure anisotropy is routinely measured in the solar wind, and it drives the firehose and mirror instabilities, which scatter particles and so act as the plasma's substitute for collisions.
829: That is the return, and it is all of it. Single-fluid MHD as built in §2 and §3 assumes a scalar pressure and therefore cannot contain any of it. No instability threshold is printed here , because no CGL source was read for this book, and a threshold quoted from memory is the failure this project keeps finding in its own drafts. What can be said without a source is the structural point: the same anisotropy that Module 1 named is the leading candidate for the microinstabilities that §8 needed and could not compute, so two of this book's unpaid debts are probably one debt.
