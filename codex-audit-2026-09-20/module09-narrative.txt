120: 1. One equation, two flows
122: Module 3 put a star in hydrostatic balance and left it there. Module 2 derived the equation that governs what happens when the balance fails, and applied it to a flow whose radial structure was measured rather than predicted. This module does the thing neither of them did: it takes the steady spherical flow seriously as an equation to be solved, discovers that the equation has a singular point, and finds that almost everything worth knowing about stellar winds and about accretion onto compact objects follows from the behaviour of solutions near that point.
124: The claim in the title is literal and is worth stating before any algebra. Write continuity and Euler for a steady, spherically symmetric flow. Eliminate the density. What is left is one ordinary differential equation for the speed $v(r)$, and that equation contains no information about which way the gas is going: $v$ enters it through $v^2$ and through $\mathrm{d}\ln v/\mathrm{d}\ln r$, and a solution with $v \gt 0$ everywhere maps onto a solution with $v \lt 0$ everywhere. Take the outward branch and it is E. N. Parker's solar wind, published in 1958. Take the inward branch and it is H. Bondi's spherical accretion flow, published in 1952. They are the same calculation. Fig. 1 is that sentence drawn.
145: The module in one picture. One central mass, one steady spherically symmetric flow, one differential equation — and two arrows. The dashed circle is the critical radius, the single place where a solution may pass from subsonic to supersonic. Reverse the arrow and Parker's problem becomes Bondi's; the equation is unchanged, and so is the critical radius. Everything after §3 is a consequence of what happens on that dashed circle.
148: The singular point is the reason this is not a routine exercise. At one particular radius the coefficient of $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}r$ vanishes, and unless the right-hand side vanishes there too, the solution has infinite gradient and stops existing. That is a strong constraint, and §3 shows it selects exactly one value of an integration constant out of a continuum. The solution that satisfies it is the only one that starts subsonic at a stellar surface and ends supersonic at infinity — and a wind that does not end supersonic decelerates as $v \propto r^{-2}$, which is a qualitatively different object with a qualitatively different signature in the data. §7 uses exactly that: the sign of a measured velocity exponent separates the two, with no fitting.
150: The module then does what this book always does, and asks whether the calculation describes anything. It asks twice, at the two extremes of the mass range, and gets two different kinds of answer.
152: The anchor is the solar wind , §7 . Venzmer & Bothmer (2018) fitted four power laws to the combined Helios 1 and 2 data between $0.29$ and $0.98$ au, each exponent fitted independently of the others and with no fluid equation imposed on the fit. Those four numbers confirm two predictions of this module and refute a third, and the sharpest of them is not a confirmation or a refutation at all but a measured shortfall: steady Euler, fed the measured pressure gradient and given no wind model whatever, accounts for about one third of the acceleration the wind is observed to have at 1 au. The same table was used in Module 2 §10 to confirm an exact conservation law and refute the adiabatic closure. §7.1 says in print what is new here and what is not, because a reader is entitled to ask whether the book is checking one table twice.
154: The astrophysical check is Sagittarius A* , §8 , and it produces the largest ratio in the book so far. The Bondi rate, computed from a measured black-hole mass and Chandra's measured gas density and temperature, exceeds the rate that Faraday rotation permits by a factor of $40$ — and the module then spends as much space on the conditions attached to that $40$ as on the number itself, because one of them cuts it to $4$. Two further comparisons, which do not depend on those conditions, survive at factors of $401$ to $4011$ and $2\times10^{7}$. The refutation is of the physical premises, not of the algebra.
156: One thing must be said at the outset, because the module's first draft got it wrong and the correction is instructive. It is often written that the steady equations determine the accretion rate, and that the coefficient Bondi computed is an eigenvalue forced by the mathematics. Bondi says the opposite, in his own summary, and §5 quotes him. The steady equations bound the rate; choosing the transonic solution is a physical argument about which flow nature realises, and it is his argument rather than a theorem. That distinction does not weaken §8 — two of its three legs never touch the coefficient — but a refutation built on a misattributed theorem would not be worth having.
158: 2. Why a static corona is impossible
160: Parker's 1958 paper does not begin with a wind. It begins by assuming there is none, and showing that the assumption cannot be maintained. The argument occupies his Sect. II, it needs nothing beyond Module 3 , and it is the cleanest demonstration in this book that an equilibrium can be internally consistent and still be impossible.
180: Contrast this with the plane-parallel isothermal atmosphere of Module 3 Proposition 3, where $g$ is constant and $P \propto \mathrm{e}^{-z/H}$ falls to zero. The difference is entirely the geometry: $\int^\infty g\,\mathrm{d}z$ diverges in a uniform field and converges in an inverse-square one. That convergence is the whole argument. An atmosphere whose pressure tends to zero can sit in a vacuum forever; an atmosphere whose pressure tends to a positive constant needs something outside it pushing back with at least that much pressure, and if nothing does, it is not in equilibrium at all.

202: 2.2 Parker's own version of the same argument
204: Parker made this calculation in 1958 with different inputs, and the comparison is worth printing — not as a test of nature, but as a check that a 68-year-old argument has been reconstructed and not merely cited. His corona is not isothermal: it conducts, and its temperature declines outward, which makes his pressure fall faster than (2.2). From $N_0 = 3\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $T_0 = 1.5\times10^{6}$ K he gets $p_0 = 2N_0k_{\rm B}T_0 \approx 1.3\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$ and $p(\infty) = 0.55\times10^{-3}p_0 = 0.6\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$, against an interstellar pressure he takes as $1.4\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$ from "10 hydrogen atoms/cm³ at 100° K". His ratio is $4.3\times10^{7}$.
208: His conclusion, in his own words, is the sentence this module rebuilds: "Since we know of no general pressure at infinity which could balance the $p(\infty)$ computed from equation (9) with the expected values of $n$, we conclude that probably it is not possible for the solar corona, or, indeed, perhaps the atmosphere of any star, to be in complete hydrostatic equilibrium out to large distances."
210: 3. The critical point, and the six things a flow may do
212: The corona must move. The question is how, and the answer is more constrained than it has any right to be.
218: Note what $r_c$ is not. It is not put in by hand and it is not a fitting parameter: it is the radius at which the escape speed $\sqrt{2GM/r}$ equals $2c_T$, equivalently where $c_T^2 = GM/(2r)$, and it is fixed the moment $T$ is fixed. Problem C1 checks that identity numerically.
234: Equation (3.5) shows the mechanism directly and is worth reading before the dimensionless form. The bracket on the left changes sign at $v = c_T$. The right side changes sign at $r = GM/2c_T^2 = r_c$. If the two sign changes do not occur at the same place, $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}r$ is infinite where the bracket vanishes and the solution terminates. There is no freedom here: a flow that is to go from subsonic to supersonic must do it at $r_c$ and nowhere else.
248: A note on the names. These six families are often numbered I to VI, and that numbering is usually attributed to Parker. It is not his. Parker (1958) was fetched and read for this module, and it contains no such enumeration: he writes the equations, imposes the transonic condition, and plots $v(r)$. The numbering is a later textbook convention, and because nothing here rests on it the families are named in words and no numeral is cited to anyone.
305: Level sets of $u^2-2\ln u-4\ln x-4/x = C$, with $u = v/c_T$ and $x = r/r_c$. Both sides of (3.3) have a single minimum — at $u = 1$ and at $x = 1$ — so a curve reaches $u = 1$ only when $C = -3$, and then only at $x = 1$. That one value of $C$ gives the two transonic solutions: the wind, which leaves a subsonic base and is supersonic at infinity, and the accretion solution, which does the reverse. $C \gt -3$ gives the breeze and the supersonic-everywhere family, neither of which crosses the sonic line; $C \lt -3$ gives the two double-valued families, which stop before reaching it at the edge of a forbidden band. The classification is drawn rather than asserted, and the families are named in words: Parker (1958) contains no Roman numbering.
308: Fig. 2 draws (3.3) as a contour plot in the $(x,u)$ plane, which is the honest way to present it: the families are level sets of one function, not six separate constructions, and the classification is visible rather than asserted. The transonic pair is the saddle-crossing contour, and it is the only contour that passes through the corner point $(1,1)$.

326: 4. The polytropic critical point, and the bound $\gamma \lt 3/2$
328: The isothermal assumption of §3 is a strong one and §7 refutes it. Before that, it is worth asking what survives without it, because the answer is a bound that the data can be held against — and a bound derived before any data is looked at is worth more than a fit made after.
332: Repeating the derivation of Proposition 2 with $P = K\rho^\gamma$ replaces $c_T^2$ by $c^2(r)$ in (3.5) and leaves the structure intact: the bracket still vanishes at $v = c$, the right side still vanishes where $2c^2/r = GM/r^2$, and the two must still coincide. What changes is that $c$ is now a function of position, so the critical radius is determined implicitly. The new content is in the slope there.

371: 5. Bondi accretion: the same solution, run inward
373: Reverse the arrow in Fig. 1 . The gas is now at rest far away, at density $\rho_\infty$ and sound speed $c_\infty$, and falls in. Equations (3.5) and (4.1) are unchanged, and by Proposition 4 the relevant branch for $\gamma \gt 3/2$ — including the adiabatic $5/3$ — is a decelerating-outward, that is accelerating-inward, transonic solution. This is Bondi's 1952 problem.
387: Two checks on (5.2), one of them requiring no algebra. At $\gamma = 1$ the sound speed is constant, so the critical radius is $GM/2c_\infty^2$ while $R_B = 2GM/c_\infty^2$, a ratio of exactly $1/4$ — and $(5-3)/8 = 1/4$. At $\gamma = 5/3$ the ratio is zero: the adiabatic flow has no interior critical point at all , which is precisely why $\lambda$ is a limit there rather than a solution property, and why §8 's Sgr A* calculation quotes $\lambda = 1/4$ without a sonic radius.

469: 7.1 What is new here, and what Module 2 already did
471: A reader who has come this far will notice that Module 2 §10 anchored on this same table, and is entitled to ask whether the book is checking one measurement twice and counting it as two results. It is not, and the distinction is worth setting out before any number is computed.
473: Module 2 used the density and velocity exponents to test continuity , which forces $\alpha-\beta = 2$ exactly for a steady spherical flow; the mean fits give $1.961$ and the median fits $2.035$, bracketing the prediction. It then used the density and temperature exponents to test the adiabatic closure , which predicts a temperature exponent of $-\alpha(\gamma-1) = -1.340$ against a measured $-0.792\pm0.028$, a $19.6\sigma$ refutation recorded in its §10.5 .
475: This module uses the same four numbers to test three different things: the critical-point bound $\gamma \lt 3/2$ of Proposition 4, which is a statement about a singular point and not about a closure; the sign of the velocity exponent, which distinguishes the solution families of Proposition 3; and the momentum equation itself, fed the measured pressure gradient, which neither earlier test touched. §7.4(i) does re-use Module 2's refutation of a closure, and it cites that result rather than re-deriving it.
477: One further caveat, inherited from Module 2 and not repaired here. The fits describe the mean or median of a distribution of streams at each radius — slow dense wind and fast tenuous wind mixed — not any single stream. Module 2's §10 set out the condition under which power-law exponents carry across such a mixture, namely radial self-similarity of the mixing weights, and the same condition is assumed here. It is an assumption, and §7.6 names it again where it bites hardest.
479: 7.2 Check 3 — the effective index lands inside the transonic window
481: For a polytropic flow, $P \propto \rho^\gamma$ with an ideal gas $P \propto \rho T$ gives $T \propto \rho^{\gamma-1}$, so with $\rho \propto r^{-\alpha}$ and $T \propto r^{e_T}$,
485: The mean fits give $\alpha = 2.010\pm0.038$ and $e_T = -0.792\pm0.028$, hence $\gamma_{\rm eff} = 1.3940\pm0.0158$. The median fits give $\alpha = 2.093\pm0.046$ and $e_T = -0.913\pm0.039$, hence $1.4362\pm0.0210$.
489: The same number separates the wind from both idealised closures: $\gamma_{\rm eff}$ is $24.9\sigma$ above the isothermal $1$ and $17.3\sigma$ below the adiabatic $5/3$ for the mean fits ($20.8\sigma$ and $11.0\sigma$ for the median). The solar wind is neither, which is the physical content of Module 2 §10.5 restated as an index.
493: 7.3 Check 4 — the sign of the velocity exponent
495: Proposition 3 gives three qualitatively different objects and §3.1 gives their signatures: a transonic wind accelerates, so $\beta \gt 0$; a breeze decays as $r^{-2}$, so $\beta \lt 0$; a static corona has $v = 0$ and no exponent at all. The measurement is $\beta = +0.049\pm0.010$ (mean) and $+0.058\pm0.013$ (median).
499: The strength of this check is that it requires no model to interpret and no parameter to be chosen: one measures a sign. Its weakness is the same thing — a sign carries one bit. What it establishes is which of Proposition 3's families the wind belongs to, and nothing about whether the family's quantitative predictions hold. The next three subsections show that they do not.
501: 7.4 Check 5 — the isothermal wind, refuted three ways
503: (i) The temperature exponent. An isothermal wind has $T$ constant, so $e_T = 0$. The measurement is $-0.792\pm0.028$, a departure of $\mathbf{28.3\sigma}$ against the formal error and $15.8\sigma$ against the yearly scatter. Module 2 §10.5 reported the adiabatic closure failing at $19.6\sigma$ against the same exponent; that result is cited here and not re-derived. Both idealisations fail, in opposite directions, which is why §7.2 's intermediate index exists at all.

522: 7.5 Check 6 — the single-polytrope repair does not work either
524: The obvious repair is to abandon $\gamma = 1$ and use the measured $\gamma_{\rm eff} = 1.3940$ throughout. It fails, in three independent ways, and each one is computed rather than argued.
526: First, a polytropic wind with $\gamma \gt 1$ has a minimum launch temperature — below it the gas runs out of enthalpy before reaching the critical point — and it follows from Proposition 5 in three lines. Bernoulli for a base at rest fixes the constant $B = c_0^2/(\gamma-1) - GM/r_0$, with $c_0^2 = \gamma k_{\rm B}T_0/(\mu m_{\rm u})$. Evaluating the same constant at the critical point, where $v = c_c$ and $c_c^2 = GM/2r_c$, gives $B = c_c^2(5-3\gamma)/[2(\gamma-1)]$. For $1 \lt \gamma \lt 5/3$ that bracket is positive, so a critical point exists if and only if $B \gt 0$ : the gas must carry more enthalpy at the base than the potential well costs. That is $c_0^2/(\gamma-1) \gt GM/r_0$, or
530: with $T_{\rm ceiling} = 6.26$ MK from (6.2) — the minimum launch temperature and Proposition 8's ceiling are the same quantity, scaled by $2(\gamma-1)/\gamma$. At $r_0 = 1.03\,R_\odot$ that minimum is $T_0 \gt 3.540$ MK for $\gamma = 1.3940$, and $3.804$ MK for the median $1.4362$; the same formula gives $1.1385$ MK at the $\gamma = 1.1$ of Problem D2 . Both exceed the top of the measured 2–3 MK range , by factors of $1.18$ and $1.27$, and exceed its bottom by $1.77$ and $1.90$. The isothermal limit is the check on (7.4): as $\gamma \to 1$ the minimum goes to zero, which is right, because the isothermal wind never runs out of enthalpy.

656: 7.7 Check 8 — the mass flux, and why it is a weak test
658: Verscharen, Bale & Velli (2021) measured the radial mass flux per steradian, $F_m = r^2NMU_r$, from Ulysses: about $3.5\times10^{-16}$ au² g cm⁻² s⁻¹ sr⁻¹ over the poles during the first fast-latitude scan, $2.2\times10^{-16}$ during the third, and $1\times10^{-16}$ to $15\times10^{-16}$ in the equatorial region (their Sect. 3.1). Multiplying by $4\pi\,\mathrm{au}^2$ — a conversion computed here, not quoted from them, since they never state a total — gives $9.843\times10^{11}$, $6.187\times10^{11}$ and $2.812\times10^{11}$ to $4.218\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$ respectively. Helios flew in the ecliptic, so the equatorial range is the one to compare against; the polar rung is quoted because $4\pi\,\mathrm{au}^2$ times it is the familiar $10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$.
660: The Helios fits supply an independent cross-check. With $n_p = 7.57\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $v = 435.6\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ at 1 au, a pure proton plasma gives $\dot M = 4\pi\,\mathrm{au}^2 n_p m_{\rm p}v = 1.551\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$, that is $F_m = 5.52\times10^{-16}$; carrying the $1+4y = 1.2$ helium mass of (2.1) — this module's choice and not Venzmer & Bothmer's, and one that Module 2 §10 did not make when it read the same table — gives $1.861\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$ and $F_m = 6.62\times10^{-16}$. (Module 2 also reads a different row of that table, $n = 5.61\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ at $410.7\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so its $1.084\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$ differs from the $1.551\times10^{12}$ here by the density and the speed, not by the helium.)
662: The Parker prediction with the Baumbach–Allen base density gives $2.897\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$ at 1 MK — a ratio of $0.294$ to the polar rung — and $1.566\times10^{11}$ at the fitted $0.947$ MK, a ratio of $0.159$. At 2 MK it gives $5.385\times10^{13}$, a ratio of $54.7$.
666: The inverse is the more informative direction, because it exposes the base density rather than hiding behind it. To deliver the observed mass flux, an isothermal model needs a base electron density of $1.074\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ at 1 MK, $3.036\times10^{7}$ at 1.5 MK, or $5.778\times10^{6}$ at 2 MK — against the $3.161\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ that Baumbach–Allen supplies. Three temperatures within the plausible range demand base densities spanning a factor of $186$. Given that the base density is itself an extrapolation below its source's fitted range ( §2.1 ), this comparison constrains very little, and saying so is more useful than quoting the one temperature at which the two happen to agree.
668: 8. The astrophysical check: Bondi accretion onto Sgr A*
670: Proposition 5 needs three numbers: a mass, a gas density and a sound speed at the accretion radius. For the black hole at the centre of the Galaxy all three have been measured, which is rare, and a fourth quantity — the actual accretion rate — has been bounded independently, which is rarer. The comparison is therefore possible, and it is the largest ratio this book has produced.
672: The mass is the GRAVITY Collaboration's, from the orbits of individual stars: $M = 4.30\times10^{6}\,M_\odot$ to about $\pm0.25$ per cent, that is $8.5502\times10^{39}$ g. 721: 8.1 First, a check that is not a test of nature
723: Baganoff et al. computed the Bondi rate from their own numbers and got $\dot M_B \approx 3\times10^{-6}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ with $\lambda = 1/4$. 825: 8.3 What is refuted, stated exactly
827: Not the algebra. §5.1 verified (5.3) against a direct assembly from the critical point to eight figures and against Bondi's own table to every legible digit. And note that the algebra was never doing as much work as is often claimed: Bondi proves $\lambda \le \lambda_c$, so even a perfect implementation does not fix the coefficient. A refutation resting on "the algebra is right, so the physics must be wrong" would be resting on a theorem that does not exist.

837: Each premise, on its own, would reduce the rate. All three act in the same direction, and together they span the factor of 40 — or 4, or 401, depending on which leg is being used — without difficulty. The value of the calculation is not that it predicts the rate. It is that it predicts a rate which is definitely wrong, by a definite amount, in a definite direction , and the size of the discrepancy is the measure of how much physics the spherical picture omits.
