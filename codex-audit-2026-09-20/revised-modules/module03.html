1. The problem: a star that should fall in half an hour

Module 2 derived the Euler equation and showed that viscosity is negligible in stellar interiors. We now apply that equation to a stationary, self-gravitating gas. The resulting balance between pressure and gravity determines the structure of stars and atmospheres.

The simplest case is also the most consequential one, and one number shows why. Take the Sun's mass $M_\odot$ and radius $R_\odot$, remove its pressure, and let it fall. A pressureless uniform sphere of mean density $\bar\rho$ collapses to its centre in the free-fall time

$$ t_{\rm ff} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{32\,G\bar\rho}}, \tag{1.1}$$

derived in Problem D3. Here $G$ is the gravitational constant and $\bar\rho = 3M/(4\pi R^3)$. For the Sun $\bar\rho = 1.4081\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, so $t_{\rm ff} = 1770.3\ \mathrm{s} = 29.5$ minutes.

The Sun has survived for about $4.6$ Gyr, or $8.2\times10^{13}$ free-fall times. A pressure gradient must therefore oppose gravity with high accuracy. Hydrostatic equilibrium describes this leading balance and constrains the radial profiles of pressure and density.

A second timescale sharpens the question. The Kelvin–Helmholtz time

$$ t_{\rm KH} \;=\; \frac{GM^2}{RL} \tag{1.2}$$

The Kelvin–Helmholtz time is the time required for a star of luminosity $L$ to radiate an energy comparable to its gravitational binding energy. For the Sun it is $31.4$ Myr, more than $5\times10^{11}$ free-fall times. Secular evolution is therefore slow compared with dynamical adjustment, allowing the Sun to remain close to hydrostatic equilibrium as it evolves.

Sections 3 and 4 apply hydrostatic balance to the terrestrial atmosphere. Sections 5–8 extend the calculation to self-gravitating polytropes and derive the Lane–Emden equation. Sections 9 and 10 compare the resulting profiles with a standard solar model and with helioseismic measurements.

2. The equation, and how well it must hold

The Euler equation (7.3) of Module 2 is

$$ \rho\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\right] \;=\; -\nabla P + \rho\,\mathbf{g}, \tag{2.1}$$

in which $\rho$ is the mass density in $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $\mathbf{u}$ is the bulk velocity, $P$ is the scalar pressure in $\mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$ (Module 2 wrote it $p$), and $\mathbf{g}$ is the gravitational acceleration. This module writes the pressure as a capital $P$ because lower-case $p$ is not needed and $P$ is the stellar-structure convention.

Definition 1 (hydrostatic equilibrium). A fluid is in hydrostatic equilibrium when $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}$ everywhere and at all times considered. Then (2.1) reduces to $$ \nabla P \;=\; \rho\,\mathbf{g}. \tag{2.2}$$

Equation (2.2) says that the net force on each fluid element is zero: the pressure pushes up with the same force per unit volume with which gravity pulls down. For a spherically symmetric body, the gravitational acceleration at radius $r$ depends only on the mass inside that radius.

Proposition 1 (spherical hydrostatic equilibrium). In a spherically symmetric, static, self-gravitating fluid with density $\rho(r)$, let $$ m(r) \;\equiv\; \int_0^r 4\pi r'^2\rho(r')\,dr' \tag{2.3}$$ be the mass inside radius $r$. Then $$ \frac{dP}{dr} \;=\; -\,\frac{G\,m(r)\,\rho(r)}{r^2}, \qquad \frac{dm}{dr} \;=\; 4\pi r^2\rho. \tag{2.4}$$
The second equation of (2.4) is the derivative of the definition (2.3). For the first, apply Gauss's law for gravity, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{g} = -4\pi G\rho$, to a sphere of radius $r$. Spherical symmetry makes $\mathbf{g} = g_r(r)\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}$, so the divergence theorem gives $4\pi r^2 g_r = -4\pi G\,m(r)$, that is $g_r = -Gm(r)/r^2$. The mass outside $r$ contributes nothing, by the same theorem. Spherical symmetry also makes $\nabla P = (dP/dr)\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}$. Substituting both into (2.2) and taking the radial component gives the first equation of (2.4). ∎

Equation (2.4) is two equations in three unknown functions, $P$, $\rho$ and $m$. It is not closed. Some relation between $P$ and $\rho$ must be supplied, and the choice of that relation is the whole subject of §5. First we ask whether setting $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}$ is justified at all.

2.1 How precisely the balance holds

A star that contracts is not static. The question is how large an imbalance between the two terms on the right of (2.1) a contraction requires. The answer is a ratio of timescales, and it is small enough to justify (2.2) by a very wide margin.

Proposition 2 (the imbalance required by a slow contraction). Let a star of mass $M$ and radius $R$ contract so that its surface accelerates at $|\ddot R| = R/t^2$, which defines the contraction timescale $t$. Then the net force per unit mass at the surface, as a fraction of the surface gravity $GM/R^2$, is $$ \frac{|\ddot R|}{GM/R^2} \;=\; \frac{8}{\pi^2}\left(\frac{t_{\rm ff}}{t}\right)^{2}, \tag{2.5}$$ with $t_{\rm ff}$ given by (1.1).
The radial component of (2.1) for the surface element, which moves with the surface, is $\ddot R = -(1/\rho)\,dP/dr - GM/R^2$. The sum of the two force terms on the right is therefore $\ddot R$, and its size relative to the gravity term is $|\ddot R|/(GM/R^2) = (R/t^2)(R^2/GM) = R^3/(GMt^2)$. Now express $R^3/(GM)$ through the free-fall time. With $\bar\rho = 3M/(4\pi R^3)$, equation (1.1) gives $$ t_{\rm ff}^2 \;=\; \frac{3\pi}{32\,G}\cdot\frac{4\pi R^3}{3M} \;=\; \frac{\pi^2}{8}\,\frac{R^3}{GM}, $$ so $R^3/(GM) = (8/\pi^2)\,t_{\rm ff}^2$. Substituting gives (2.5). ∎

Apply (2.5) to the fastest contraction the Sun could sustain from its own luminosity, $t = t_{\rm KH}$. The coefficient-free factor is $(t_{\rm ff}/t_{\rm KH})^2 = 3.192\times10^{-24}$. The exact coefficient $8/\pi^2 = 0.8106$ multiplies it.

Result. For a deliberately slow, approximately homologous contraction on the Kelvin–Helmholtz time, equation (2.5) gives a characteristic secular acceleration $2.587\times10^{-24}$ times the surface gravity. This is a scale estimate for that assumed evolution, not an observational upper bound on oscillatory, convective or other local accelerations.

For a star evolving through approximately hydrostatic states on a timescale $t_{\rm evol}$, a characteristic secular acceleration is $R/t_{\rm evol}^2$. Its ratio to gravity is of order $(t_{\rm dyn}/t_{\rm evol})^2$. Taking a thermal evolution timescale therefore motivates a small secular acceleration. This estimate does not bound oscillatory or convective accelerations and is not a luminosity-derived error bar on hydrostatic balance. We use hydrostatic equilibrium to model the mean stellar structure, while treating motions and their timescales separately.

The nuclear timescale uses $0.007\times0.1\,Mc^2/L$: $0.7$ per cent of rest mass released by hydrogen burning, applied to the inner $10$ per cent of the mass. Both factors are conventional order-of-magnitude inputs, not results of this book. The solar $GM$, $R$ and $L$ are the IAU 2015 nominal values. The mean density uses the radius printed in the tabulated solar model of §9, $6.9598\times10^{10}$ cm, which differs from the IAU nominal $6.957\times10^{10}$ cm by $0.04$ per cent. The Kelvin–Helmholtz time uses the IAU value.

A sound wave carries the information that restores balance. The isothermal sound speed at the Sun's centre, $\sqrt{P_c/\rho_c}$, is $394.1\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, and the sound crossing time $R/c_s$ is $1765.1$ s, or $29.4$ minutes. It differs from $t_{\rm ff}$ by $0.3$ per cent. Both are order-of-magnitude estimates, so the closeness to $0.3$ per cent is partly accidental. That the two are of the same order is not: the virial theorem, Proposition 12 of §8, gives $3\int P\,dV = -\Omega$, and with $\Omega\sim -GM^2/R$ and $\int P\,dV\sim (P/\rho)M$ this makes $P/\rho\sim GM/R$, so $R/c_s\sim\sqrt{R^3/GM}\sim t_{\rm ff}$. Any departure from balance is corrected in about half an hour. Module 4 studies those sound waves directly.

3. The plane-parallel atmosphere and the scale height

Before the whole star, take a thin layer at its surface, or the atmosphere of a planet. If the layer is thin compared with the radius, the curvature of the surface and the change of $g$ with height can both be ignored.

Definition 2 (plane-parallel layer). A plane-parallel layer is a region in which all quantities depend only on a height coordinate $z$, measured upward, and the gravitational acceleration is uniform, $\mathbf{g} = -g\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ with $g\gt 0$ constant. Equation (2.2) then becomes $$ \frac{dP}{dz} \;=\; -\rho\, g. \tag{3.1}$$

Equation (3.1) needs a second relation between $P$ and $\rho$. The ideal gas law supplies one when the temperature is known:

$$ P \;=\; \frac{\rho\, k_B T}{\mu\, m_u}, \tag{3.2}$$

in which $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ the temperature, $m_u$ the atomic mass unit, and $\mu$ the mean molecular weight, the mean mass per free particle in units of $m_u$. For dry air $\mu = 28.9647$; for a fully ionised gas of hydrogen mass fraction $X$, helium mass fraction $Y$ and heavy-element fraction $Z$, $\mu = 1/(2X + \tfrac{3}{4}Y + \tfrac{1}{2}Z)$.

Proposition 3 (isothermal scale height). In a plane-parallel layer of ideal gas at uniform temperature $T$ and uniform $\mu$, $$ P(z) \;=\; P(0)\,e^{-z/H}, \qquad H \;\equiv\; \frac{k_B T}{\mu\, m_u\, g}. \tag{3.3}$$ The density falls with the same exponential, $\rho(z) = \rho(0)\,e^{-z/H}$. The length $H$ is the pressure scale height.
Solve (3.2) for the density, $\rho = \mu m_u P/(k_BT)$, and substitute into (3.1): $$ \frac{dP}{dz} \;=\; -\frac{\mu\, m_u\, g}{k_B T}\,P \;=\; -\frac{P}{H}. $$ Because $T$, $\mu$ and $g$ are constant, $H$ is constant, and this linear equation has the solution $P(0)e^{-z/H}$. The density is $\mu m_u P/(k_BT)$ with a constant prefactor, so it has the same dependence on $z$. ∎

The scale height is the height over which the pressure falls by a factor $e$. It is the height through which an energy $k_BT$, the thermal energy per particle to within a factor of order one, would lift one particle against its weight $\mu m_u g$. A hot gas or a light gas or a weak gravity gives a thick atmosphere.

Proposition 3 was proved under the assumption that $g$ is uniform. For a layer on a body of radius $r$ and mass $M$, $g = GM/r^2$, and it changes by a fractional amount of about $2H/r$ across one scale height. The layer's area also changes by the same fraction. So the plane-parallel form is valid when

$$ H \;\ll\; r. \tag{3.4}$$

4. The polytropic atmosphere: the terrestrial half

The Earth's lower atmosphere is not isothermal. Its temperature falls with height at a nearly constant rate, the lapse rate. This section shows that a constant lapse rate is exactly what a power-law relation between pressure and density produces, and so it introduces the closure that the rest of the module applies to stars.

Definition 3 (polytrope). A fluid is polytropic with polytropic index $n$ and polytropic constant $K$ if, throughout the region considered, $$ P \;=\; K\,\rho^{1+1/n}, \tag{4.1}$$ with $K$ and $n$ constant, $0\le n\le\infty$. The case $n\to\infty$ is the isothermal gas; $n=0$ is a gas of uniform density.

Definition 3 is a statement about the structure of a body, a relation between $P$ and $\rho$ at different places. It need not be a statement about how one parcel responds when it is compressed, although in §5 it will be one.

Proposition 4 (lapse rate of a polytropic atmosphere). In a plane-parallel layer of ideal gas (3.2) with uniform $\mu$ and $g$, obeying the polytropic relation (4.1), the temperature falls linearly with height at the rate $$ \left|\frac{dT}{dz}\right| \;=\; \frac{\mu\, m_u\, g}{k_B\,(n+1)}, \tag{4.2}$$ and the pressure and temperature are related by $$ \frac{P}{P(0)} \;=\; \left(\frac{T}{T(0)}\right)^{n+1}. \tag{4.3}$$
From (4.1), $\rho = (P/K)^{n/(n+1)}$. Substituting into the ideal gas law (3.2) and solving for $T$, $$ T \;=\; \frac{\mu m_u}{k_B}\,\frac{P}{\rho} \;=\; \frac{\mu m_u}{k_B}\,K^{n/(n+1)}\,P^{1/(n+1)}. $$ The prefactor is constant, so $T\propto P^{1/(n+1)}$, which is (4.3). Differentiate the logarithm of this relation with respect to $z$: $$ \frac{1}{T}\frac{dT}{dz} \;=\; \frac{1}{n+1}\,\frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dz} \;=\; -\frac{1}{n+1}\,\frac{\rho\, g}{P}, $$ using (3.1) in the second step. By (3.2), $\rho/P = \mu m_u/(k_BT)$, so the right-hand side is $-\mu m_u g/(k_B T(n+1))$. Multiplying by $T$ gives $dT/dz = -\mu m_u g/(k_B(n+1))$, a constant, which is (4.2). ∎

Inverting (4.2) turns a measured lapse rate $\Gamma \equiv |dT/dz|$ into a polytropic index, $n = \mu m_u g/(k_B\Gamma) - 1$. Two lapse rates of the Earth's atmosphere are worth converting.

The first is the lapse rate of the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA), $6.5\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$ up to the tropopause at $11$ km. It gives $n = 4.2558$.

The second is the dry adiabatic lapse rate, the rate at which a parcel of dry air cools when it rises without exchanging heat. A parcel of ideal gas compressed or expanded adiabatically obeys $P\propto\rho^{\gamma}$, with $\gamma$ the ratio of specific heats. If the atmosphere is well mixed, so that every level lies on the same adiabat, the structure obeys $P=K\rho^{\gamma}$ with one $K$, and comparison with (4.1) gives $1+1/n = \gamma$, or

$$ n \;=\; \frac{1}{\gamma-1}. \tag{4.4}$$

For the diatomic molecules of dry air, $\gamma = 7/5$ and $n = 2.5$. Substituting into (4.2) gives $\Gamma = g(\gamma-1)\mu m_u/(\gamma k_B) = g/c_p$, where $c_p = \gamma k_B/((\gamma-1)\mu m_u) = 1004.7\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ is the specific heat at constant pressure. Numerically $g/c_p = 9.761\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$, and inverting (4.2) returns $n = 2.5000$.

Fig. 1 compares the three. The real troposphere cools more slowly than the dry adiabat, by $35.9$ K over $11$ km. Latent heat can reduce the cooling rate of a saturated rising parcel. The standard atmospheric lapse rate, however, is a reference profile rather than the result of this parcel calculation alone. The index $4.26$ is therefore not an adiabatic index of any gas. It is an effective index that summarises the combined effect of adiabatic cooling and condensation heating on the structure.

Equation (4.3) also gives the pressure at the tropopause. With $n = 4.2558$, $T(0) = 288.15$ K and a tropopause temperature of $288.15 - 6.5\times11 = 216.65$ K, it gives $226.32$ hPa, in ratio $1.00001$ to the tabulated $226.32$ hPa.

This agreement is a check of the algebra, not a test of nature. The ISA is defined by its sea-level temperature and pressure, its lapse rate and the hydrostatic equation, and its tabulated tropopause values are computed from those constants. Agreement to $1.00001$ confirms that (4.2) and (4.3) are derived and evaluated correctly. It says nothing about whether the real atmosphere is polytropic. The tests against nature in this module are in §10.
The ISA constants are from ISO 2533:1975, identical to the US Standard Atmosphere 1976 below 11 km: $T(0)=288.15$ K, $P(0)=101325$ Pa, lapse rate $6.5\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$, tropopause at $11$ km geopotential altitude with $216.65$ K and $22632$ Pa. They are definitional and are trusted exactly. The attribution of the gap between $6.5$ and $9.761\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$ to latent heat is standard meteorology and is not computed here.

5. Why a polytrope: two indices fixed in advance

The spherical equations (2.4) need a closure, and Definition 3 is the simplest one. A closure is only useful if its index can be fixed before the star is examined. If $n$ were chosen to fit a model, agreement with that model would prove nothing. This section derives two indices from physics alone.

Proposition 5 (adiabatic convection gives $n = 3/2$). Suppose a region of an ideal monatomic gas has uniform specific entropy, so that every part of it lies on one adiabat $P = K\rho^{\gamma}$ with $\gamma = 5/3$. Then the region is a polytrope of index $n = 3/2$.
For an ideal gas whose particles have only the three translational degrees of freedom, the internal energy per particle is $\tfrac{3}{2}k_BT$ (Module 2, Proposition 1), so the specific heats per particle are $c_V = \tfrac{3}{2}k_B$ and $c_P = c_V + k_B = \tfrac{5}{2}k_B$, and $\gamma = c_P/c_V = 5/3$. Along an adiabat, $P\rho^{-\gamma}$ is constant; uniform specific entropy means the same constant $K$ applies everywhere in the region. So $P = K\rho^{5/3}$ throughout, and matching the exponent to (4.1), $1 + 1/n = 5/3$, gives $n = 3/2$. ∎

The physical input is the hypothesis of uniform entropy. Convection supplies it: rising and sinking parcels mix the gas, and where convection is efficient it carries the stratification to the adiabatic state. That is asserted here and derived in Module 6 through the Schwarzschild criterion. The index follows from the hypothesis with no free parameter. The monatomic assumption requires the gas to be fully ionised or neutral atomic; in a zone where hydrogen or helium is partly ionised, ionisation absorbs energy and $\gamma$ falls below $5/3$.

The second index comes from radiation pressure. A hot star contains a photon gas as well as a particle gas, and both push.

Definition 4 (gas-pressure fraction). In a gas of ideal particles and blackbody radiation at temperature $T$, the total pressure is $P = P_{\rm gas} + P_{\rm rad}$ with $$ P_{\rm gas} \;=\; \frac{k_B}{\mu m_u}\,\rho T, \qquad P_{\rm rad} \;=\; \frac{a_{\rm rad} T^4}{3}, \tag{5.1}$$ where $a_{\rm rad} = 7.5657\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{erg\,cm^{-3}\,K^{-4}}$ is the radiation constant. The gas-pressure fraction is $\beta \equiv P_{\rm gas}/P$, so $1-\beta = P_{\rm rad}/P$.
Proposition 6 (Eddington's standard model gives $n = 3$). If $\beta$ and $\mu$ are the same everywhere in a star, with $0\lt\beta\lt 1$, then $$ P \;=\; K\rho^{4/3}, \qquad K \;=\; \left[\frac{3}{a_{\rm rad}}\,\frac{1-\beta}{\beta^4}\right]^{1/3}\left(\frac{k_B}{\mu m_u}\right)^{4/3}, \tag{5.2}$$ so the star is a polytrope of index $n = 3$.
Eliminate $T$ between the two parts of (5.1). From $P_{\rm rad} = (1-\beta)P$, the temperature is $T = [3(1-\beta)P/a_{\rm rad}]^{1/4}$. From $P_{\rm gas} = \beta P$, $$ \beta P \;=\; \frac{k_B}{\mu m_u}\,\rho\,\left[\frac{3(1-\beta)P}{a_{\rm rad}}\right]^{1/4}. $$ Divide both sides by $\beta P^{1/4}$: $$ P^{3/4} \;=\; \frac{k_B}{\mu m_u}\,\frac{1}{\beta}\left[\frac{3(1-\beta)}{a_{\rm rad}}\right]^{1/4}\rho. $$ Raise both sides to the power $4/3$: $$ P \;=\; \left(\frac{k_B}{\mu m_u}\right)^{4/3}\beta^{-4/3}\left[\frac{3(1-\beta)}{a_{\rm rad}}\right]^{1/3}\rho^{4/3}, $$ which is (5.2) after collecting $\beta^{-4/3} = (\beta^{-4})^{1/3}$ inside the bracket. If $\beta$ and $\mu$ are constant, $K$ is constant and $P\propto\rho^{4/3}$; by (4.1), $1+1/n = 4/3$, so $n=3$. ∎

Note what the proof needs. It needs $\beta$ to be constant, and it needs $1-\beta$ to be non-zero, since otherwise $K$ vanishes and the relation says nothing. It does not need $1-\beta$ to be large. The hypothesis of constant $\beta$ has a direct test. Since $1-\beta = a_{\rm rad}T^4/(3P)$, constant $\beta$ means $T^4\propto P$, that is $d\ln T/d\ln P = 1/4$ at every depth. §10 measures that slope through the Sun, and measures the size of $1-\beta$ at its centre.

Both indices are now fixed. The number $3/2$ follows from uniform entropy in a monatomic gas, and the number $3$ follows from a uniform radiation share. Neither value was inferred from a solar model, and so each can be tested against one.

6. The Lane–Emden equation

Substituting the polytropic closure (4.1) into the spherical equations (2.4) produces one second-order equation for the density. Scaling out the dimensional constants leaves an equation that depends on $n$ alone.

Proposition 7 (the Lane–Emden equation). Let a spherical star in hydrostatic equilibrium (2.4) obey the polytropic relation (4.1) with index $n\gt 0$, and let $\rho_c$ be its central density. Define the dimensionless density variable $\theta$ and radius $\xi$ by $$ \rho \;=\; \rho_c\,\theta^{n}, \qquad r \;=\; a\,\xi, \qquad a^2 \;\equiv\; \frac{(n+1)\,K\,\rho_c^{1/n-1}}{4\pi G}. \tag{6.1}$$ Then $\theta(\xi)$ satisfies $$ \frac{1}{\xi^2}\,\frac{d}{d\xi}\left(\xi^2\,\frac{d\theta}{d\xi}\right) \;=\; -\,\theta^{n}, \tag{6.2}$$ with the boundary conditions $$ \theta(0) \;=\; 1, \qquad \theta'(0) \;=\; 0. \tag{6.3}$$ The surface of the star is the first zero of $\theta$, at $\xi = \xi_1$, where $R = a\,\xi_1$.
First combine the two equations of (2.4) into one. Multiply the first by $r^2/\rho$, so that $r^2\rho^{-1}\,dP/dr = -Gm$, and differentiate with respect to $r$. The second equation replaces $dm/dr$: $$ \frac{1}{r^2}\,\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{r^2}{\rho}\,\frac{dP}{dr}\right) \;=\; -4\pi G\rho. $$ Next write the pressure in terms of $\theta$. With $\rho = \rho_c\theta^n$, the closure (4.1) gives $P = K\rho_c^{1+1/n}\theta^{n+1}$, so $$ \frac{dP}{dr} \;=\; (n+1)\,K\rho_c^{1+1/n}\,\theta^{n}\,\frac{d\theta}{dr}, \qquad \frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dP}{dr} \;=\; (n+1)\,K\rho_c^{1/n}\,\frac{d\theta}{dr}. $$ Substituting and dividing by $4\pi G\rho_c$, $$ \frac{(n+1)\,K\rho_c^{1/n-1}}{4\pi G}\;\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{d\theta}{dr}\right) \;=\; -\theta^n. $$ The prefactor is $a^2$ by (6.1), and it has dimensions of length squared. Change variable to $\xi = r/a$. Then $d/dr = a^{-1}\,d/d\xi$ and $r^2 = a^2\xi^2$, so $r^{-2}\,d(r^2\,d\theta/dr)/dr = a^{-2}\,\xi^{-2}\,d(\xi^2\,d\theta/d\xi)/d\xi$. The factor $a^{-2}$ cancels the prefactor $a^2$, which gives (6.2). For the boundary conditions: $\theta(0)=1$ because $\rho(0)=\rho_c$ by definition. For the second, note that $m(r)\approx\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3\rho_c$ near the centre, so $dP/dr = -Gm\rho/r^2\approx -\tfrac{4}{3}\pi G\rho_c^2\, r \to 0$ as $r\to 0$. Since $dP/dr$ is proportional to $\theta^n\,d\theta/dr$ and $\theta(0)=1$, this requires $\theta'(0)=0$. The pressure, and therefore the density, first vanish where $\theta$ first vanishes, which is the surface. ∎

The scaling has removed $K$, $\rho_c$ and $G$. Every polytrope of index $n$, whatever its mass and radius, is the same function $\theta_n(\xi)$, stretched in radius by $a$ and in density by $\rho_c$. So one integration for each $n$ describes all stars of that index.

The point $\xi=0$ is a singular point of (6.2), because the term $2\theta'/\xi$ has a zero denominator. A numerical integration cannot start there, and it starts instead from a short series.

Proposition 8 (series near the centre). The solution of (6.2) with (6.3) has the expansion $$ \theta(\xi) \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{\xi^2}{6} \;+\; \frac{n\,\xi^4}{120} \;-\; \cdots. \tag{6.4}$$
Expanding (6.2) gives $\theta'' + 2\theta'/\xi = -\theta^n$. The equation is unchanged under $\xi\to-\xi$, and the conditions (6.3) are even, so the regular solution is an even function. Write $\theta = 1 + b_2\xi^2 + b_4\xi^4 + O(\xi^6)$. Then $\theta' = 2b_2\xi + 4b_4\xi^3$ and $\theta'' = 2b_2 + 12b_4\xi^2$, so $$ \theta'' + \frac{2\theta'}{\xi} \;=\; 6b_2 + 20b_4\,\xi^2 + O(\xi^4). $$ On the right, by the binomial expansion, $\theta^n = 1 + n\,b_2\,\xi^2 + O(\xi^4)$. Matching the constant terms gives $6b_2 = -1$, so $b_2 = -1/6$. Matching the $\xi^2$ terms gives $20b_4 = -n b_2 = n/6$, so $b_4 = n/120$. ∎

The case $n=0$ needs one remark before it can be used. With $n=0$, the substitution $\rho=\rho_c\theta^n$ makes $\rho$ uniform and says nothing about $\theta$, and $\rho_c^{1/n}$ in (6.1) is undefined. Since $P_c = K\rho_c^{1+1/n}$, the length in (6.1) can be written $a^2 = (n+1)P_c/(4\pi G\rho_c^2)$, and the closure gives $P = P_c\,\theta^{n+1}$. Both forms remain meaningful at $n=0$, and we take them as the definitions of $a$ and $\theta$ there. For uniform density, (2.4) gives $dP/dr = -\tfrac{4}{3}\pi G\rho_c^2 r$, so $P = P_c - \tfrac{2}{3}\pi G\rho_c^2 r^2$; with $\theta = P/P_c$ and $r = a\xi$, $a^2 = P_c/(4\pi G\rho_c^2)$, this is $\theta = 1-\xi^2/6$, which is the solution of (6.2) with $\theta^0 = 1$. So (6.2) and (6.3) hold at $n=0$ with this reading, and (7.2) below applies to it.

\n\n

Three values of $n$ have solutions in closed form. They are the test cases for the numerical integrator, and each shows a different kind of star.

Proposition 9 (exact solutions for $n=0$, $1$, $5$). The solutions of (6.2) and (6.3) for these three indices are $$ \theta_0 \;=\; 1 - \frac{\xi^2}{6}, \qquad \theta_1 \;=\; \frac{\sin\xi}{\xi}, \qquad \theta_5 \;=\; \left(1+\frac{\xi^2}{3}\right)^{-1/2}. \tag{6.5}$$ Their surfaces are at $\xi_1 = \sqrt6$ with $\theta_0'(\xi_1) = -\sqrt6/3$, and at $\xi_1 = \pi$ with $\theta_1'(\xi_1) = -1/\pi$. The $n=5$ solution has no zero, so its radius is infinite, but $-\xi^2\theta_5'\to\sqrt3$ as $\xi\to\infty$, so its mass is finite.
Case $n=0$. Then $\theta^0=1$ and (6.2) is $(\xi^2\theta')' = -\xi^2$. Integrate once: $\xi^2\theta' = -\xi^3/3 + C$. At $\xi=0$ the left side is zero, so $C=0$ and $\theta' = -\xi/3$. Integrate again with $\theta(0)=1$ to get $\theta_0 = 1-\xi^2/6$. It vanishes at $\xi_1=\sqrt6$, where $\theta' = -\sqrt6/3$. The density $\rho_c\theta^0$ is uniform, but the pressure $\propto\theta^{n+1} = \theta$ is not. Case $n=1$. Substitute $\theta = \chi/\xi$. Then $\xi^2\theta' = \xi\chi' - \chi$, and its derivative is $\xi\chi''$, so (6.2) becomes $\chi''/\xi = -\chi/\xi$, that is $\chi'' = -\chi$. The general solution is $\chi = A\sin\xi + B\cos\xi$. The term $B\cos\xi/\xi$ diverges at the origin, so $B=0$; and $\sin\xi/\xi\to1$ as $\xi\to0$, so $A=1$. The first zero of $\sin\xi$ after the origin is $\xi_1=\pi$. There $\theta_1' = (\xi\cos\xi - \sin\xi)/\xi^2 = -\pi/\pi^2 = -1/\pi$. Case $n=5$. Let $s = 1+\xi^2/3$. Differentiating $\theta_5 = s^{-1/2}$ gives $\theta_5' = -(\xi/3)\,s^{-3/2}$, so $\xi^2\theta_5' = -(\xi^3/3)\,s^{-3/2}$. Differentiating again, $$ (\xi^2\theta_5')' \;=\; -\xi^2 s^{-3/2} + \frac{\xi^3}{3}\cdot\frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{2\xi}{3}\,s^{-5/2} \;=\; -\xi^2 s^{-5/2}\left(s - \frac{\xi^2}{3}\right) \;=\; -\xi^2 s^{-5/2}. $$ Dividing by $\xi^2$ gives $-s^{-5/2} = -\theta_5^5$, which is (6.2). The conditions (6.3) hold by inspection. $\theta_5\gt0$ for every $\xi$, so there is no surface. For large $\xi$, $s^{-3/2}\to(\xi^2/3)^{-3/2} = 3^{3/2}\xi^{-3}$, so $-\xi^2\theta_5' \to (\xi^3/3)\cdot3^{3/2}\xi^{-3} = \sqrt3$. ∎

The analytic cases test the implementation and the surface-location procedure. Their errors do not by themselves bound the errors at $n=3/2$ and $n=3$. For those cases, numerical accuracy should be assessed by reducing the integration step and checking convergence of the first zero, its derivative and the derived mass coefficient. Until that check is supplied, the digits quoted here are computed values rather than an established error bound.

7. Properties of polytropes

The dimensionless solution fixes every structural ratio of a polytrope. This section extracts the ones that can be compared with a star of known mass and radius.

Proposition 10 (mass, radius, central condensation and central pressure). A polytrope of index $n\lt 5$ with first zero $\xi_1$ and surface slope $\theta_1' \equiv \theta'(\xi_1)$ has $$ R \;=\; a\,\xi_1, \qquad M \;=\; 4\pi a^3\rho_c\left(-\xi_1^2\theta_1'\right), \tag{7.1}$$ $$ D_n \;\equiv\; \frac{\rho_c}{\bar\rho} \;=\; \frac{\xi_1}{3\,|\theta_1'|}, \qquad W_n \;\equiv\; \frac{P_c\,R^4}{G M^2} \;=\; \frac{1}{4\pi(n+1)\,\theta_1'^{\,2}}, \tag{7.2}$$ and, for $n\neq3$, the mass–radius relation $$ R \;\propto\; K^{\,n/(3-n)}\,M^{(1-n)/(3-n)}. \tag{7.3}$$
The radius follows from $r=a\xi$ at the surface. For the mass, integrate (2.3) with $\rho=\rho_c\theta^n$ and $r=a\xi$: $$ M \;=\; 4\pi a^3\rho_c\int_0^{\xi_1}\xi^2\theta^n\,d\xi \;=\; 4\pi a^3\rho_c\int_0^{\xi_1}\left(-\frac{d}{d\xi}\big(\xi^2\theta'\big)\right)d\xi \;=\; 4\pi a^3\rho_c\left(-\xi_1^2\theta_1'\right), $$ using (6.2) in the middle step and $\xi^2\theta'=0$ at $\xi=0$. For $D_n$: $\bar\rho = 3M/(4\pi R^3) = 3\rho_c(-\xi_1^2\theta_1')/\xi_1^3 = 3\rho_c|\theta_1'|/\xi_1$, and dividing $\rho_c$ by this gives the first of (7.2). For $W_n$: the central pressure is $P_c = K\rho_c^{1+1/n}$. By (6.1), $K\rho_c^{1/n} = 4\pi G a^2\rho_c/(n+1)$, so $P_c = 4\pi G a^2\rho_c^2/(n+1)$. From (7.1), $\rho_c = M/(4\pi a^3\mu_1)$ with $\mu_1\equiv-\xi_1^2\theta_1'$. Substituting, $$ P_c \;=\; \frac{4\pi G a^2}{n+1}\cdot\frac{M^2}{16\pi^2a^6\mu_1^2} \;=\; \frac{GM^2}{4\pi(n+1)\,a^4\mu_1^2}. $$ With $a = R/\xi_1$, $a^4\mu_1^2 = R^4\xi_1^{-4}\cdot\xi_1^4\theta_1'^2 = R^4\theta_1'^2$, which gives the second of (7.2). For (7.3): from (6.1), $a^2\propto K\rho_c^{(1-n)/n}$, and from (7.1), $\rho_c\propto M/a^3$. Substituting, $a^2\propto K M^{(1-n)/n}a^{-3(1-n)/n}$, so $a^{(3-n)/n}\propto K M^{(1-n)/n}$. Raising to the power $n/(3-n)$ and using $R\propto a$ gives (7.3). ∎

8. What balance gives with no closure

Everything in §6 and §7 depends on the closure (4.1). Before testing a closure, it is worth asking what hydrostatic equilibrium implies with no closure at all. The answer is two exact results. One is an inequality, and one is an identity.

Proposition 11 (Chandrasekhar's lower bound on the central pressure). In a spherical star of mass $M$ and radius $R$ in hydrostatic equilibrium (2.4), with finite central density and non-negative surface pressure, $$ P_c \;\ge\; \frac{G M^2}{8\pi R^4}. \tag{8.1}$$
Define $F(r) \equiv P(r) + Gm(r)^2/(8\pi r^4)$. Differentiate, using both equations of (2.4): $$ \frac{dF}{dr} \;=\; -\frac{Gm\rho}{r^2} \;+\; \frac{2Gm}{8\pi r^4}\cdot4\pi r^2\rho \;-\; \frac{4Gm^2}{8\pi r^5} \;=\; -\frac{Gm^2}{2\pi r^5} \;\le\; 0. $$ The first two terms cancel exactly. So $F$ does not increase outward, and $F(0)\ge F(R)$. At the centre, $m\approx\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3\rho_c$ makes $m^2/r^4\propto r^2\to0$, so $F(0)=P_c$. At the surface, $F(R) = P(R) + GM^2/(8\pi R^4) \ge GM^2/(8\pi R^4)$. Combining gives (8.1). ∎

For the Sun, with the tabulated radius of §9, the bound is $4.4750\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$. The tabulated central pressure is $2.3380\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$, which exceeds the bound by a factor $522.5$. The bound is true and it excludes almost nothing. The factor of $522$ is the information that a closure must supply. In the notation of (7.2), the bound is $W\ge1/(8\pi) = 0.03979$. The tabulated Sun has $W = 20.7881$, and $n=3$ gives $W_3 = 11.0507$.

Proposition 12 (the virial theorem for a static star). Under the hypotheses of Proposition 11, with zero surface pressure $P(R)=0$, $$ 3\int_0^R P\,dV \;=\; -\,\Omega, \qquad \Omega \;\equiv\; -\int_0^M \frac{G\,m}{r}\,dm, \tag{8.2}$$ where $dV = 4\pi r^2 dr$ and $\Omega$ is the gravitational potential energy of the star.
Multiply the first equation of (2.4) by $4\pi r^3$ and integrate from $0$ to $R$. On the left, integrate by parts: $$ \int_0^R 4\pi r^3\frac{dP}{dr}\,dr \;=\; \Big[4\pi r^3 P\Big]_0^R - \int_0^R 12\pi r^2 P\,dr \;=\; -3\int_0^R P\,dV, $$ since the boundary term vanishes at $r=0$ and, because $P(R)=0$, at $r=R$. On the right, $$ -\int_0^R 4\pi r^3\,\frac{Gm\rho}{r^2}\,dr \;=\; -\int_0^R \frac{Gm}{r}\,4\pi r^2\rho\,dr \;=\; -\int_0^M\frac{Gm}{r}\,dm \;=\; \Omega, $$ using $dm = 4\pi r^2\rho\,dr$. Equating the two sides gives $-3\int P\,dV = \Omega$, which is (8.2). ∎

For an ideal monatomic gas, $\tfrac{3}{2}P$ is the internal energy per unit volume (Module 2, Proposition 1), so (8.2) says that the thermal energy $U = \int\tfrac{3}{2}P\,dV$ is $-\Omega/2$. The total energy is then $E = U + \Omega = \Omega/2$. A star that radiates loses energy, so $E$ and $\Omega$ decrease; a more negative $\Omega$ means a more compact star, and $U = -\Omega/2$ increases. The star contracts and its thermal energy rises, and half the released gravitational energy is radiated. That is why $t_{\rm KH}$ in (1.2), the binding energy divided by the luminosity, is the timescale of contraction.

Integrating the tabulated solar model of §9 gives $3\int P\,dV = 6.1594\times10^{48}$ erg and $-\Omega = 6.1564\times10^{48}$ erg, in ratio $1.00049$.

This agreement does not confirm hydrostatic equilibrium. The solar model was built by solving (2.4) numerically. Any correct reading of its table must satisfy (8.2), so a ratio of $1.00049$ confirms that the table was read and integrated correctly, and nothing more. The residual $4.9\times10^{-4}$ has not been decomposed; two candidate sources are the trapezoid rule and the table ending at $0.983\,R$ rather than at $P=0$. Module 2's solar-wind check did not have this circularity, because Venzmer & Bothmer fitted the density and speed exponents with no continuity constraint imposed. Here the tests must be of a closure, given the equation, and §10 is built that way.

9. The tabulated solar model

The comparison object for the checks is a standard solar model: a numerical solution of the equations of stellar structure, evolved from a uniform initial composition to the present solar age and adjusted to reproduce the present solar radius and luminosity. The one used here is BS2005-AGS,OP of Bahcall, Serenelli & Basu (2005), ApJ 621, L85–L88. Its table is stored as data/bs05_agsop.dat.

The table has $1284$ rows, from $r/R = 0.00161$ to $0.98308$. Each row gives the enclosed mass fraction, the radius fraction, $T$, $\rho$, $P$, the enclosed luminosity fraction, and six mass fractions. Its first row gives the central values used below: $T_c = 1.5480\times10^{7}$ K, $\rho_c = 150.50\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $P_c = 2.3380\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$. The surface helium mass fraction in the last row is $0.22905$, against $0.229$ printed in Table 1 of the paper, which confirms that the file is the model the paper describes.

Two quantities computed from the table are needed in §10. The first is an effective mean molecular weight. Inverting the ideal gas law (3.2) at the centre, $\mu = \rho k_B T/(P m_u)$, gives $\mu = 0.82851$. The composition in the same row, $X = 0.36462$, $Y=0.62029$, $Z = 0.01509$, gives $\mu = 0.83195$ for a fully ionised gas. The ratio is $0.99587$. The tabulated $\mu$ is an effective value which absorbs every contribution to $P$ beyond the ideal particle gas; radiation, the next quantity, accounts for $6\times10^{-4}$ of the $4.1\times10^{-3}$ difference, and the rest is not attributed here.

The second quantity is the radiation share. At the centre, $P_{\rm rad} = a_{\rm rad}T_c^4/3 = 1.4482\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$, so

$$ 1-\beta_c \;=\; \frac{P_{\rm rad}}{P_c} \;=\; 6.1940\times10^{-4}. \tag{9.1}$$

Radiation supplies $6.19\times10^{-4}$ of the pressure at the centre of the Sun. This pressure fraction tests the assumption that gas pressure dominates at the solar centre. It is the premise of Proposition 6 evaluated in the Sun.

The model table is trusted as a faithful copy of the published BS2005-AGS,OP model: its surface helium matches the paper, and the virial identity (8.2) holds to $4.9\times10^{-4}$. It is not trusted as a description of the real Sun; §10 shows where it fails. Its radii and masses are fractions, converted here with the table's own constants $R_\odot = 6.9598\times10^{10}$ cm and $L_\odot = 3.8418\times10^{33}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$, and with $M_\odot = GM_\odot/G$ from the IAU 2015 nominal $GM_\odot$. Its "central" values are those of the first row, at $r = 0.00161\,R$.

10. The checks

The circularity noted in §8 decides what can be tested. The solar model satisfies (2.4) by construction, so the table cannot test hydrostatic equilibrium. It can test a closure: given (2.4), is the relation between $P$ and $\rho$ in the model the one that §5 derived? Each of the two indices of §5 was fixed independently of the tabulated solar model. 10.2 Check 2: the convection zone as $n=3/2$

To test a polytropic index locally, define it locally. The first step of the proof of Proposition 4 used only the ideal gas law (3.2) and the closure (4.1), and gave $T\propto P^{1/(n+1)}$ when $\mu$ is constant. The logarithmic slope of $T$ against $P$ is therefore constant and fixes $n$.

Definition 5 (temperature gradient and effective index). The dimensionless temperature gradient of a stratified star is $$ \nabla \;\equiv\; \frac{d\ln T}{d\ln P}, \tag{10.1}$$ evaluated along the radius. The effective polytropic index at each radius is $$ n_{\rm eff} \;\equiv\; \frac{1}{\nabla} - 1. \tag{10.2}$$ For a polytrope of index $n$ with uniform $\mu$, $\nabla = 1/(n+1)$ and $n_{\rm eff}=n$ everywhere. For the adiabat of a monatomic ideal gas, $\nabla = \nabla_{\rm ad} = (\gamma-1)/\gamma = 0.4$ and $n_{\rm eff} = 3/2$. Where $\mu$ varies with radius, $T\propto\mu P/\rho$ adds a term $d\ln\mu/d\ln P$ to $\nabla$, and $n_{\rm eff}$ is then not the exponent in $P\propto\rho^{1+1/n}$.

The table quotes $P$, $\rho$ and $T$ to four significant figures, so a two-point difference of $\ln T$ against $\ln P$ is dominated by rounding. The script computes $\nabla$ as a least-squares slope over $\pm25$ rows. In the band $0.80$ to $0.92\,R$ the slope is flat, and its median is the plateau value.

10.3 Check 3: the base of the convection zone, against a measurement

Checks 1 and 2 compare a closure with a model. Neither compares anything with the Sun. The profile of Fig. 3 supplies a quantity that can be measured: the radius at which the stratification changes from radiative to adiabatic, the base of the convection zone.

The script locates it as the innermost radius, between $0.60$ and $0.95\,R$, at which $\nabla$ reaches $0.99$ of its plateau value $0.3957$. Applied to BS2005-AGS,OP, this gives $r_b = 0.7269\,R$. Table 1 of Bahcall, Serenelli & Basu (2005) gives $0.7280\,R$ for the same model, a ratio of $0.99851$. The locator therefore reproduces the model's published boundary to $0.15$ per cent.

Helioseismology measures the same boundary in the real Sun. The frequencies of the Sun's acoustic oscillations depend on the sound-speed profile, and the sound speed changes slope where $\nabla$ changes from its radiative to its adiabatic value. Basu & Antia (2004) obtain $r_b = (0.7133\pm0.0005)\,R$ from GONG p-mode frequencies, and $(0.7132\pm0.0005)\,R$ from MDI frequencies.

Check 3: the model is refuted by a measurement. The model's published base, $0.7280\,R$, lies $0.0147\,R$ above the measured $0.7133\pm0.0005\,R$. That is a fractional error of $2.06$ per cent and a departure of $29.4\sigma$. The base recovered by this module's locator, $0.7269\,R$, is $27.2\sigma$ from the measurement. Bahcall, Serenelli & Basu quote the measurement rounded to $0.713\pm0.001$; against that uncertainty the published base departs by $15.0\sigma$.

The number of standard deviations would mean little if the locator could move the base by a comparable amount. Across a grid of fitting windows of $\pm10$, $\pm25$ and $\pm50$ rows and thresholds of $0.98$, $0.99$ and $0.995$ of the plateau, the recovered base ranges from $0.7255$ to $0.7308\,R$. The spread, $0.0054\,R$, is $0.37$ of the model–measurement gap. The grid value nearest the measurement, $0.7255\,R$, is still $24.3\sigma$ from it.

State exactly what this check refutes. It does not refute hydrostatic equilibrium, and it does not refute the $n=3/2$ closure of the convection zone. Both are the instrument: they define the plateau and locate the boundary, and Check 2 confirms the closure in the region where it is claimed. What fails is an input of this particular model, the physics that sets where $\nabla$ first reaches $\nabla_{\rm ad}$. In a radiative region, that radius is set by the opacity and by the heavy-element abundance on which the opacity depends. BS2005-AGS,OP was built with the lower heavy-element abundances denoted "AGS" in its name, and Table 1 of Bahcall, Serenelli & Basu (2005) lists its base, $0.7280$, beside the measured $0.713\pm0.001$ that the paper quotes in its equation (1). This disagreement between low-abundance solar models and helioseismology is known as the solar abundance problem.

The measured base is from Basu & Antia (2004), ApJ 606, L85, with the GONG value adopted. The model's published base and surface helium are from Table 1 of Bahcall, Serenelli & Basu (2005). The sentence that the base is set by opacity and heavy-element abundance is standard stellar-structure physics. This module does not compute it, and Module 6 derives the criterion that sets the boundary. The inference of $r_b$ from oscillation frequencies is itself a model-dependent inversion, described in the source and not reproduced here.

Conceptual ↑ contents

C1. The tabulated solar model satisfies the virial theorem (8.2) to a ratio of $1.00049$. A student claims this confirms that the Sun is in hydrostatic equilibrium to five parts in ten thousand. Explain what the ratio does and does not show, and why Module 2's solar-wind check was not open to the same objection.
Solution
Proposition 12 derives (8.2) from (2.4) alone. Any structure that satisfies (2.4) with $P(R)=0$ satisfies (8.2). The solar model was computed by solving (2.4), so it satisfies (8.2) whether or not the real Sun does. The ratio $3\int P\,dV/(-\Omega) = 6.1594\times10^{48}/6.1564\times10^{48} = 1.00049$ shows that the table was read and integrated correctly. It is a test of the pipeline, not of nature. It would test hydrostatic equilibrium only if $P$, $\rho$ and $m$ had been measured independently of (2.4). In Module 2 the density exponent $\alpha$ and the speed exponent $\beta$ were fitted separately to Helios data, with no continuity constraint imposed in the fit, so $\alpha-\beta = 2$ was not built into the numbers compared.
C2. Check 1 found that $n=3$ predicts $\rho_c$ and $P_c$ in ratios $0.5069$ and $0.5316$ to the tabulated model, but $T_c$ in ratio $1.0486$. Explain why the temperature is so much better, and state what this implies about using a central temperature to validate a stellar model.
Solution
At fixed $\mu$, the ideal gas law (3.2) gives $T = \mu m_u P/(k_B\rho)$. So the predicted-to-tabulated temperature ratio is the pressure ratio divided by the density ratio: $0.5316/0.5069 = 1.0486$. The two structural errors have the same sign and nearly the same size, so their quotient is close to one. The implication is that $T_c$ is a weak test of a structure. A model can be wrong by a factor of two in both $\rho_c$ and $P_c$ and still give $T_c$ to five per cent, so agreement in $T_c$ alone does not show that the density and pressure are right.
C3. The intracluster medium of §3 has $H = 644$ kpc at $r = 1$ Mpc. Explain why the exponential atmosphere (3.3) does not describe it, with a number, and compare the solar photosphere.
Solution
Proposition 3 assumed $g$ uniform. For gravity dominated by a mass $M$ inside $r$, $g\propto r^{-2}$, so across one scale height $g$ falls by the factor $(r/(r+H))^2$. For the cluster, $H = 0.64\,r$, and the factor is $0.370$: gravity falls to $37$ per cent of its value within one e-folding of the pressure, so the constant $H$ used to define that e-folding has no single value. The spherical equation (2.4) must be used instead. At the solar photosphere, $H/R = 1.94\times10^{-4}$ and the factor is $0.999613$, so $g$ changes by $3.9\times10^{-4}$ across one scale height and (3.3) holds to that precision.

Derivational ↑ contents

D1. Use the mass–radius relation (7.3) to give the exponent of $M$ in $R\propto M^{x}$ for $n = 0$, $1$, $3/2$, $2$, $5/2$ and $4$ at fixed $K$. Interpret $n=0$, $n=1$ and $n=3/2$, and explain what happens at $n=3$.
Solution
By (7.3), $x = (1-n)/(3-n)$. This gives $x = +1/3$ for $n=0$, $0$ for $n=1$, $-1/3$ for $n=3/2$, $-1$ for $n=2$, $-3$ for $n=5/2$, and $+3$ for $n=4$. For $n=0$ the density is uniform and $K$ plays no role, so $M\propto R^3$ at fixed density and $R\propto M^{1/3}$, as for a solid. For $n=1$ the radius does not depend on the mass. For $n=3/2$, $R\propto M^{-1/3}$: a more massive star is smaller. That is the mass–radius relation of a white dwarf supported by non-relativistic degenerate electrons, whose equation of state has the form $P\propto\rho^{5/3}$ with $K$ fixed by fundamental constants. The form of that equation of state is quoted here, not derived. At $n=3$ the exponent is undefined. From (6.1) with $n=3$, $a^2\propto K\rho_c^{-2/3}$, so $a^3\rho_c\propto K^{3/2}$, and (7.1) gives $M\propto K^{3/2}$ with no dependence on $\rho_c$ or $R$. At fixed $K$ an $n=3$ polytrope has one mass only.
D2. A white dwarf has $M = 0.6\,M_\odot$ and $R = 0.013\,R_\odot$. Use Proposition 11 to bound its central pressure from below, and give the ratio of this bound to the Sun's.
Solution
$M = 1.1930\times10^{33}$ g. With $R_\odot = 6.9598\times10^{10}$ cm, $R = 9.0477\times10^{8}$ cm $= 9048$ km. Then $$ P_c \;\ge\; \frac{GM^2}{8\pi R^4} \;=\; 5.6405\times10^{21}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}. $$ The Sun's bound is $4.4750\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$. Since the bound scales as $M^2/R^4$, the ratio is $(0.6)^2/(0.013)^4 = 1.2605\times10^{7}$, which the direct division reproduces. The bound needs no equation of state. For the Sun the tabulated $P_c$ exceeds its bound by $522.5$, so the bound lies a factor $522.5$ below the actual value and cannot fix the structure.
D3. Derive the free-fall time (1.1). A uniform sphere of mass $M$ and radius $R$ has no pressure and starts at rest. Show that its outer shell reaches the centre in $t_{\rm ff} = \sqrt{3\pi/(32G\bar\rho)}$, and evaluate it for the Sun. By what factor does it exceed the estimate $\sqrt{R^3/GM}$?
Solution
With no pressure, each shell feels only the mass inside it, and shells do not cross, so the outer shell at radius $r(t)$ obeys $\ddot r = -GM/r^2$ with $r(0) = R$ and $\dot r(0)=0$. Energy conservation gives $\dot r^2 = 2GM(1/r - 1/R)$. Substitute $r = R\cos^2\eta$, so $dr = -2R\cos\eta\sin\eta\,d\eta$ and $1/r - 1/R = \tan^2\eta/R$. Then $$ dt \;=\; -\frac{dr}{|\dot r|} \;=\; \frac{2R\cos\eta\sin\eta\,d\eta}{\sqrt{2GM/R}\,\tan\eta} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{2R^3}{GM}}\,\cos^2\eta\,d\eta. $$ The shell reaches the centre at $\eta = \pi/2$, and $\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^2\eta\,d\eta = \pi/4$. So $t_{\rm ff} = (\pi/4)\sqrt{2R^3/(GM)} = \sqrt{\pi^2R^3/(8GM)}$. With $M = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3\bar\rho$ this is $\sqrt{3\pi/(32G\bar\rho)}$. For the Sun, $\bar\rho = 1.4081\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, so $t_{\rm ff} = 1770.3$ s $= 29.5$ min. The estimate $\sqrt{R^3/GM}$ is $1593.8$ s, and the ratio is $1.1107$, which is $\pi/(2\sqrt2)$ exactly.

K1. Compute the isothermal scale height (3.3) at the surfaces of Mars and Venus from $T = 210$ K, $\mu = 43.34$, $g = 371\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}$ for Mars, and $T = 737$ K, $\mu = 43.45$, $g = 887\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}$ for Venus. Compare them with the Earth's $8.435$ km, and say which inputs set the Venus-to-Mars ratio.
Solution
From $H = k_BT/(\mu m_u g)$: for Mars, $H = 1.380649\times10^{-16}\times210/(43.34\times1.6605\times10^{-24}\times371) = 10.859$ km. For Venus, $H = 15.900$ km. The ratios to the Earth are $1.2874$ for Mars and $1.8851$ for Venus, and Venus to Mars is $1.4642$. The two atmospheres are both mostly carbon dioxide and have $\mu$ within $0.25$ per cent of each other, so the ratio is set by $T/g$: the temperature ratio $3.5095$ divided by the gravity ratio $2.3908$ is $1.4679$, and the ratio of $\mu$ accounts for the remaining difference from $1.4642$. The planetary temperatures, molecular weights and surface gravities are representative round values supplied as inputs. They are not checked against a source here, and the problem tests the formula, not the planets.
K2. Model a neutron star of $M = 1.4\,M_\odot$ and $R = 12$ km as an $n=1$ polytrope. Find $K$, $\rho_c$, $\rho_c/\bar\rho$ and $P_c$, compare $\rho_c$ with nuclear saturation density, and check $P_c$ against Proposition 11.
Solution
For $n=1$, (6.1) gives $a^2 = 2K/(4\pi G) = K/(2\pi G)$, independent of $\rho_c$, and $R = a\xi_1 = \pi a$. So $K = 2\pi G R^2/\pi^2 = 2GR^2/\pi = 6.1185\times10^{4}$ in CGS units. From (7.2) with $\xi_1 = \pi$ and $|\theta_1'| = 1/\pi$, $D_1 = \pi^2/3 = 3.2899$, so $\rho_c = (\pi^2/3)\cdot3M/(4\pi R^3) = \pi M/(4R^3)$. With $M = 2.7838\times10^{33}$ g and $R = 1.2\times10^{6}$ cm, $\rho_c = 1.2653\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $\bar\rho = 3.8459\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$. Nuclear saturation density, $0.16$ nucleons per $\mathrm{fm^3}$ times $m_u$, is $2.657\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, so $\rho_c$ is $4.76$ times it. The central pressure is $P_c = K\rho_c^2 = 9.7951\times10^{34}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$. The bound of Proposition 11 is $9.9245\times10^{33}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$, and $P_c$ exceeds it by $9.87$. This must equal $8\pi W_1 = 8\pi\times0.39270 = \pi^2 = 9.87$, and it does. The saturation density $0.16\ \mathrm{fm^{-3}}$ is a standard nuclear-physics value quoted as an input, not checked against a source here. The $n=1$ polytrope is a toy: it ignores general relativity, which is not small in a neutron star.
K3. For an $n=3$ star, (7.1) fixes $K$ from $M$, and (5.2) then fixes $\beta$. Taking $\mu = 0.832$, solve for $1-\beta$ at $1$, $10$ and $50\,M_\odot$, and compare the $1\,M_\odot$ value with the Sun's central $1-\beta$ of (9.1).
Solution
For $n=3$, $a^2 = K\rho_c^{-2/3}/(\pi G)$, so $a^3\rho_c = (K/\pi G)^{3/2}$ and (7.1) gives $M = 4\pi(K/\pi G)^{3/2}\mu_1$ with $\mu_1 = 2.01824$. Inverting, $K = \pi G\,[M/(4\pi\mu_1)]^{2/3}$. Rearranging (5.2), $$ \frac{1-\beta}{\beta^4} \;=\; \frac{a_{\rm rad}}{3}\,K^3\left(\frac{\mu m_u}{k_B}\right)^{4}, $$ which is Eddington's quartic. Its left side decreases monotonically from $+\infty$ to $0$ as $\beta$ rises from $0$ to $1$, so it has one root, found by bisection. At $1\,M_\odot$, $K = 3.8409\times10^{14}$, the right side is $1.4329\times10^{-3}$, and $1-\beta = 1.4247\times10^{-3}$. At $10\,M_\odot$, $K = 1.7828\times10^{15}$, the right side is $1.4329\times10^{-1}$, and $1-\beta = 9.5784\times10^{-2}$. At $50\,M_\odot$, $K = 5.2130\times10^{15}$, the right side is $3.5821$, and $1-\beta = 4.1617\times10^{-1}$. So radiation carries $0.14$ per cent of the pressure at $1\,M_\odot$, $9.6$ per cent at $10\,M_\odot$ and $42$ per cent at $50\,M_\odot$. The share rises by a factor $67.2$ from $1$ to $10\,M_\odot$. Eddington's $1\,M_\odot$ value is $2.30$ times the tabulated central value $6.194\times10^{-4}$. Both are below one per cent. Proposition 6 does not require a large share, so this does not by itself refute $n=3$; Check 1 refutes it through the measured variation of $\nabla$. What K3 shows is that at one solar mass radiation pressure cannot be the agent that fixes the structure, while at $10$ and $50\,M_\odot$ it carries a share large enough to matter in (2.4).
