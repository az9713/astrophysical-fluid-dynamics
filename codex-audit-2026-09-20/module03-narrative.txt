92: Module 3 — Hydrostatic Equilibrium
93: Setting the velocity to zero in the Euler equation: the scale height, the polytropic atmosphere, the Lane–Emden equation, and a tabulated solar model in which one polytrope is confirmed, one is refuted, and the boundary between them disagrees with helioseismology.
116: 1. The problem: a star that should fall in half an hour
118: Module 2 derived the Euler equation, its equation (7.3), and ended with a table which showed that viscosity may be dropped in the interiors of stars. This module applies that equation in the simplest case it has: a gas that does not move.
120: The simplest case is also the most consequential one, and one number shows why. Take the Sun's mass $M_\odot$ and radius $R_\odot$, remove its pressure, and let it fall. A pressureless uniform sphere of mean density $\bar\rho$ collapses to its centre in the free-fall time
124: derived in Problem D3. Here $G$ is the gravitational constant and $\bar\rho = 3M/(4\pi R^3)$. For the Sun $\bar\rho = 1.4081\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, so $t_{\rm ff} = 1770.3\ \mathrm{s} = 29.5$ minutes.
126: The Sun does not collapse in 29.5 minutes. Its age of $4.6$ Gyr is $8.2\times10^{13}$ free-fall times. Something holds it up, and that something is the pressure gradient. The question this module answers is how precisely the pressure gradient must balance gravity, and what structure that balance forces on a star.
128: A second timescale sharpens the question. The Kelvin–Helmholtz time
132: is the time in which a star of luminosity $L$ would radiate its gravitational binding energy, to order of magnitude. For the Sun it is $9.9089\times10^{14}$ s, or $31.4$ Myr. If the Sun had no nuclear source it would contract on this timescale. It is $5.5973\times10^{11}$ times longer than the free-fall time. A star can change, but it changes on a timescale so much longer than the dynamical one that at each instant it is in balance.
134: The module follows the three-part shape of this book. §3 and §4 derive the terrestrial result: the scale height of the Earth's atmosphere and its lapse rate as a polytrope. §5 to §8 change the regime to a self-gravitating sphere and derive the Lane–Emden equation. §9 and §10 check three predictions against a tabulated standard solar model and against a helioseismic measurement. One prediction is confirmed, one is refuted, and the third finds a 29.4σ disagreement between the model and the measured Sun.
136: 2. The equation, and how well it must hold
138: The Euler equation (7.3) of Module 2 is
142: in which $\rho$ is the mass density in $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $\mathbf{u}$ is the bulk velocity, $P$ is the scalar pressure in $\mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$ (Module 2 wrote it $p$), and $\mathbf{g}$ is the gravitational acceleration. This module writes the pressure as a capital $P$ because lower-case $p$ is not needed and $P$ is the stellar-structure convention.
147: Equation (2.2) says that the net force on each fluid element is zero: the pressure pushes up with the same force per unit volume with which gravity pulls down. For a spherically symmetric body, the gravitational acceleration at radius $r$ depends only on the mass inside that radius.
158: Equation (2.4) is two equations in three unknown functions, $P$, $\rho$ and $m$. It is not closed. Some relation between $P$ and $\rho$ must be supplied, and the choice of that relation is the whole subject of §5 . First we ask whether setting $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}$ is justified at all.
160: 2.1 How precisely the balance holds
162: A star that contracts is not static. The question is how large an imbalance between the two terms on the right of (2.1) a contraction requires. The answer is a ratio of timescales, and it is small enough to justify (2.2) by a very wide margin.
174: Apply (2.5) to the fastest contraction the Sun could sustain from its own luminosity, $t = t_{\rm KH}$. The coefficient-free factor is $(t_{\rm ff}/t_{\rm KH})^2 = 3.192\times10^{-24}$; the exact coefficient $8/\pi^2 = 0.8106$ multiplies it.
178: Note the direction of this argument. The small imbalance is not assumed in order to simplify the equations. It is forced by the observed luminosity. By (2.5), an imbalance larger than about $10^{-24}$ means contraction on a timescale $t$ shorter than $t_{\rm KH}$. Such a contraction releases gravitational energy of order $GM^2/R$ in time $t$, a power $GM^2/(Rt)$ larger than $L$ by the factor $t_{\rm KH}/t$ from (1.2). By the virial argument of §8 , half of that energy must leave the star, and the observed luminosity does not carry it. Setting $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}$ in (2.1) discards a term of that relative size.
182: A sound wave carries the information that restores balance. The isothermal sound speed at the Sun's centre, $\sqrt{P_c/\rho_c}$, is $394.1\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, and the sound crossing time $R/c_s$ is $1765.1$ s, or $29.4$ minutes. It differs from $t_{\rm ff}$ by $0.3$ per cent. Both are order-of-magnitude estimates, so the closeness to $0.3$ per cent is partly accidental. That the two are of the same order is not: the virial theorem, Proposition 12 of §8 , gives $3\int P\,dV = -\Omega$, and with $\Omega\sim -GM^2/R$ and $\int P\,dV\sim (P/\rho)M$ this makes $P/\rho\sim GM/R$, so $R/c_s\sim\sqrt{R^3/GM}\sim t_{\rm ff}$. Any departure from balance is corrected in about half an hour. Module 4 studies those sound waves directly.
184: 3. The plane-parallel atmosphere and the scale height
186: Before the whole star, take a thin layer at its surface, or the atmosphere of a planet. If the layer is thin compared with the radius, the curvature of the surface and the change of $g$ with height can both be ignored.
191: Equation (3.1) needs a second relation between $P$ and $\rho$. The ideal gas law supplies one when the temperature is known:
195: in which $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ the temperature, $m_u$ the atomic mass unit, and $\mu$ the mean molecular weight, the mean mass per free particle in units of $m_u$. For dry air $\mu = 28.9647$; for a fully ionised gas of hydrogen mass fraction $X$, helium mass fraction $Y$ and heavy-element fraction $Z$, $\mu = 1/(2X + \tfrac{3}{4}Y + \tfrac{1}{2}Z)$.
207: The scale height is the height over which the pressure falls by a factor $e$. It is the height through which an energy $k_BT$, the thermal energy per particle to within a factor of order one, would lift one particle against its weight $\mu m_u g$. A hot gas or a light gas or a weak gravity gives a thick atmosphere.

For a layer on a body of radius $r$ and mass $M$, $g = GM/r^2$, and it changes by a fractional amount of about $2H/r$ across one scale height. The layer's area also changes by the same fraction. 227: 4. The polytropic atmosphere: the terrestrial half
229: The Earth's lower atmosphere is not isothermal. Its temperature falls with height at a nearly constant rate, the lapse rate . This section shows that a constant lapse rate is exactly what a power-law relation between pressure and density produces, and so it introduces the closure that the rest of the module applies to stars.
235: Definition 3 is a statement about the structure of a body, a relation between $P$ and $\rho$ at different places. It need not be a statement about how one parcel responds when it is compressed, although in §5 it will be one.
250: Inverting (4.2) turns a measured lapse rate $\Gamma \equiv |dT/dz|$ into a polytropic index, $n = \mu m_u g/(k_B\Gamma) - 1$. Two lapse rates of the Earth's atmosphere are worth converting.
252: The first is the lapse rate of the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA), $6.5\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$ up to the tropopause at $11$ km. It gives $n = 4.2558$.
254: The second is the dry adiabatic lapse rate, the rate at which a parcel of dry air cools when it rises without exchanging heat. A parcel of ideal gas compressed or expanded adiabatically obeys $P\propto\rho^{\gamma}$, with $\gamma$ the ratio of specific heats. If the atmosphere is well mixed, so that every level lies on the same adiabat, the structure obeys $P=K\rho^{\gamma}$ with one $K$, and comparison with (4.1) gives $1+1/n = \gamma$, or
258: For the diatomic molecules of dry air, $\gamma = 7/5$ and $n = 2.5$. Substituting into (4.2) gives $\Gamma = g(\gamma-1)\mu m_u/(\gamma k_B) = g/c_p$, where $c_p = \gamma k_B/((\gamma-1)\mu m_u) = 1004.7\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ is the specific heat at constant pressure. Numerically $g/c_p = 9.761\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$, and inverting (4.2) returns $n = 2.5000$.
337: Three hydrostatic atmospheres with the same sea-level state, $T(0)=288.15$ K and $P(0)=1013.25$ hPa. Left: temperature against altitude. Right: pressure, from (3.3) for the isothermal case and (4.3) for the two polytropes. The standard atmosphere, $n=4.26$, lies between the isothermal limit and the dry adiabat, $n=2.50$; at $11$ km the dry adiabat is $36$ K colder. The circle marks the tabulated tropopause pressure of $226$ hPa, which the $n=4.26$ curve reaches by construction.
340: Fig. 1 compares the three. The real troposphere cools more slowly than the dry adiabat, by $35.9$ K over $11$ km. The difference is the latent heat released when water vapour condenses in rising air, which warms the parcel as it rises. The index $4.26$ is therefore not an adiabatic index of any gas. It is an effective index that summarises the combined effect of adiabatic cooling and condensation heating on the structure.
342: Equation (4.3) also gives the pressure at the tropopause. With $n = 4.2558$, $T(0) = 288.15$ K and a tropopause temperature of $288.15 - 6.5\times11 = 216.65$ K, it gives $226.32$ hPa, in ratio $1.00001$ to the tabulated $226.32$ hPa.
348: 5. Why a polytrope: two indices fixed in advance
350: The spherical equations (2.4) need a closure, and Definition 3 is the simplest one. A closure is only useful if its index can be fixed before the star is examined. If $n$ were chosen to fit a model, agreement with that model would prove nothing. This section derives two indices from physics alone.
358: The physical input is the hypothesis of uniform entropy. Convection supplies it: rising and sinking parcels mix the gas, and where convection is efficient it carries the stratification to the adiabatic state. That is asserted here and derived in Module 6 through the Schwarzschild criterion. The index follows from the hypothesis with no free parameter. The monatomic assumption requires the gas to be fully ionised or neutral atomic; in a zone where hydrogen or helium is partly ionised, ionisation absorbs energy and $\gamma$ falls below $5/3$.
360: The second index comes from radiation pressure. A hot star contains a photon gas as well as a particle gas, and both push.
380: Note what the proof needs. It needs $\beta$ to be constant, and it needs $1-\beta$ to be non-zero, since otherwise $K$ vanishes and the relation says nothing. It does not need $1-\beta$ to be large. The hypothesis of constant $\beta$ has a direct test. Since $1-\beta = a_{\rm rad}T^4/(3P)$, constant $\beta$ means $T^4\propto P$, that is $d\ln T/d\ln P = 1/4$ at every depth. §10 measures that slope through the Sun, and measures the size of $1-\beta$ at its centre.
382: Both indices are now fixed. The number $3/2$ follows from uniform entropy in a monatomic gas, and the number $3$ follows from a uniform radiation share. Neither was read from a solar model, and so each can be tested against one.
384: 6. The Lane–Emden equation
386: Substituting the polytropic closure (4.1) into the spherical equations (2.4) produces one second-order equation for the density. Scaling out the dimensional constants leaves an equation that depends on $n$ alone.
408: The scaling has removed $K$, $\rho_c$ and $G$. Every polytrope of index $n$, whatever its mass and radius, is the same function $\theta_n(\xi)$, stretched in radius by $a$ and in density by $\rho_c$. So one integration for each $n$ describes all stars of that index.
410: The point $\xi=0$ is a singular point of (6.2), because the term $2\theta'/\xi$ has a zero denominator. A numerical integration cannot start there, and it starts instead from a short series.
421: The case $n=0$ needs one remark before it can be used. With $n=0$, the substitution $\rho=\rho_c\theta^n$ makes $\rho$ uniform and says nothing about $\theta$, and $\rho_c^{1/n}$ in (6.1) is undefined. Since $P_c = K\rho_c^{1+1/n}$, the length in (6.1) can be written $a^2 = (n+1)P_c/(4\pi G\rho_c^2)$, and the closure gives $P = P_c\,\theta^{n+1}$. Both forms remain meaningful at $n=0$, and we take them as the definitions of $a$ and $\theta$ there. For uniform density, (2.4) gives $dP/dr = -\tfrac{4}{3}\pi G\rho_c^2 r$, so $P = P_c - \tfrac{2}{3}\pi G\rho_c^2 r^2$; with $\theta = P/P_c$ and $r = a\xi$, $a^2 = P_c/(4\pi G\rho_c^2)$, this is $\theta = 1-\xi^2/6$, which is the solution of (6.2) with $\theta^0 = 1$. So (6.2) and (6.3) hold at $n=0$ with this reading, and (7.2) below applies to it.
421: Three values of $n$ have solutions in closed form. They are the test cases for the numerical integrator, and each shows a different kind of star.
437: The integrator in figs/m03_numbers.py is a fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme started from (6.4) at $\xi=10^{-5}$. On the three exact cases it returns $\xi_1 = 2.449490$ for $n=0$ and $3.141593$ for $n=1$, both in ratio $1.000000$ to the exact values. It returns $\theta_1'(\xi_1) = -0.318310$ for $n=1$, in ratio $1.000000$. For $n=5$ it returns $\theta(200) = 8.659914\times10^{-3}$, in ratio $1.000000$ to (6.5), and $-\xi^2\theta' = 1.731856$ at $\xi=200$, in ratio $0.999888$ to $\sqrt3$. The largest departure is on $\theta_0'(\xi_1) = -0.816392$, in ratio $0.999871$ to the exact $-\sqrt6/3$: the $n=0$ solution meets the surface at a finite slope, and the last step lands just past it. The integrator is therefore trusted to about $1.3\times10^{-4}$ on the cases $n=3/2$ and $n=3$, which have no closed form.

585: 8. What balance gives with no closure
587: Everything in §6 and §7 depends on the closure (4.1). Before testing a closure, it is worth asking what hydrostatic equilibrium implies with no closure at all. The answer is two exact results. One is an inequality, and one is an identity.
598: For the Sun, with the tabulated radius of §9 , the bound is $4.4750\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$. The tabulated central pressure is $2.3380\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$, which exceeds the bound by a factor $522.5$. The bound is true and it excludes almost nothing. The factor of $522$ is the information that a closure must supply. In the notation of (7.2), the bound is $W\ge1/(8\pi) = 0.03979$; the tabulated Sun has $W = 20.7881$, and $n=3$ gives $W_3 = 11.0507$.
612: For an ideal monatomic gas, $\tfrac{3}{2}P$ is the internal energy per unit volume (Module 2, Proposition 1), so (8.2) says that the thermal energy $U = \int\tfrac{3}{2}P\,dV$ is $-\Omega/2$. The total energy is then $E = U + \Omega = \Omega/2$. A star that radiates loses energy, so $E$ and $\Omega$ decrease; a more negative $\Omega$ means a more compact star, and $U = -\Omega/2$ increases. The star contracts and its thermal energy rises, and half the released gravitational energy is radiated. That is why $t_{\rm KH}$ in (1.2), the binding energy divided by the luminosity, is the timescale of contraction.
614: Integrating the tabulated solar model of §9 gives $3\int P\,dV = 6.1594\times10^{48}$ erg and $-\Omega = 6.1564\times10^{48}$ erg, in ratio $1.00049$.

634: 10. The checks
636: The circularity noted in §8 decides what can be tested. The solar model satisfies (2.4) by construction, so the table cannot test hydrostatic equilibrium. It can test a closure: given (2.4), is the relation between $P$ and $\rho$ in the model the one that §5 derived? Each of the two indices of §5 was fixed before the table was read. This section tests each, then takes the boundary between them to a helioseismic measurement.

690: 10.3 Check 3: the base of the convection zone, against a measurement
692: Checks 1 and 2 compare a closure with a model. Neither compares anything with the Sun. The profile of Fig. 3 supplies a quantity that can be measured: the radius at which the stratification changes from radiative to adiabatic, the base of the convection zone.
694: The script locates it as the innermost radius, between $0.60$ and $0.95\,R$, at which $\nabla$ reaches $0.99$ of its plateau value $0.3957$. Applied to BS2005-AGS,OP, this gives $r_b = 0.7269\,R$. Table 1 of Bahcall, Serenelli & Basu (2005) gives $0.7280\,R$ for the same model, a ratio of $0.99851$. The locator therefore reproduces the model's published boundary to $0.15$ per cent.
696: Helioseismology measures the same boundary in the real Sun. The frequencies of the Sun's acoustic oscillations depend on the sound-speed profile, and the sound speed changes slope where $\nabla$ changes from its radiative to its adiabatic value. Basu & Antia (2004) obtain $r_b = (0.7133\pm0.0005)\,R$ from GONG p-mode frequencies, and $(0.7132\pm0.0005)\,R$ from MDI frequencies.
772: Upper panel: the effective polytropic index $n_{\rm eff} = 1/\nabla - 1$ of the tabulated solar model, against the two indices derived in §5 . The shaded region is the convection zone, where $n_{\rm eff}$ lies on the $n=3/2$ line to $1.8$ per cent (Check 2). The radiative interior crosses $n=3$ twice and remains on it nowhere (Check 1). Lower panel: $\nabla$ near the base of the convection zone. The orange line is the model's published base, $0.7280\,R$; the violet line and band are the helioseismic measurement, $(0.7133\pm0.0005)\,R$. The gap is $0.0147\,R$, $29$ standard deviations of the measurement (Check 3).
777: The number of standard deviations would mean little if the locator could move the base by a comparable amount. Across a grid of fitting windows of $\pm10$, $\pm25$ and $\pm50$ rows and thresholds of $0.98$, $0.99$ and $0.995$ of the plateau, the recovered base ranges from $0.7255$ to $0.7308\,R$. The spread, $0.0054\,R$, is $0.37$ of the model–measurement gap. The grid value nearest the measurement, $0.7255\,R$, is still $24.3\sigma$ from it.
779: State exactly what this check refutes. It does not refute hydrostatic equilibrium, and it does not refute the $n=3/2$ closure of the convection zone. Both are the instrument: they define the plateau and locate the boundary, and Check 2 confirms the closure in the region where it is claimed. What fails is an input of this particular model, the physics that sets where $\nabla$ first reaches $\nabla_{\rm ad}$. In a radiative region, that radius is set by the opacity and by the heavy-element abundance on which the opacity depends. BS2005-AGS,OP was built with the lower heavy-element abundances denoted "AGS" in its name, and Table 1 of Bahcall, Serenelli & Basu (2005) lists its base, $0.7280$, beside the measured $0.713\pm0.001$ that the paper quotes in its equation (1). This disagreement between low-abundance solar models and helioseismology is known as the solar abundance problem .

The agreement of Check 2 is a statement about BS2005-AGS,OP; it becomes a statement about the Sun only to the extent that the model's convection zone matches the Sun's, which helioseismology tests separately.
