1. The problem: a cloud that two criteria judge differently

Module 3 treated self-gravity in hydrostatic equilibrium. Module 4 studied acoustic perturbations while keeping the gravitational field fixed. We now allow the density perturbation to generate a gravitational perturbation through Poisson's equation and ask when that feedback overcomes pressure support. The distinction is between an equilibrium gravitational field and the evolving field of the disturbance.

Barnard 68 provides a concrete test case. It is a small, isolated molecular cloud with a fitted mass of $2.10\,M_\odot$, radius $1.25\times10^4$ au and temperature $16$ K at the adopted distance of $125$ pc. These values imply a mean density of $1.5245\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.

Two commonly used criteria give different answers for Barnard 68. The Jeans criterion treats the cloud as part of a uniform medium and predicts a critical mass of $2.97\,M_\odot$, classifying the cloud as stable. The Bonnor–Ebert criterion treats it as a pressure-confined isothermal sphere. Its fitted dimensionless radius, $\xi_{\max}=6.9\pm0.2$, exceeds the critical value $6.4508$ and therefore indicates instability.

The disagreement arises from the background states used in the two derivations. The standard Jeans calculation perturbs a uniform static medium that is not a self-consistent finite equilibrium. The Bonnor–Ebert calculation perturbs a pressure-confined equilibrium sphere. Sections 3–5 derive both approaches, while Sections 6 and 7 compare them with Barnard 68 and other Bok globules.

2. Self-gravity and the isothermal closure

The gas is described by the continuity equation and the Euler equation of Module 2, (5.1) and (7.3), with Module 2's $p$ written $P$. The acceleration $\mathbf{g}$ is now written as the gradient of a gravitational potential $\Phi$, and $\Phi$ is produced by the gas itself through Poisson's equation:

$$ \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u}) = 0, \tag{2.1}$$ $$ \rho\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\right] = -\nabla P - \rho\nabla\Phi, \tag{2.2}$$ $$ \nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho. \tag{2.3}$$

Here $\rho$ is the mass density, $\mathbf{u}$ the bulk velocity, $P$ the pressure and $G$ the gravitational constant. Equation (2.3) couples the gas back to its own field. It is what Modules 2 to 4 left out.

The perturbation equations require a pressure closure. For the cold clouds considered here, we assume isothermal gas, $P=c_s^2\rho$. This approximation requires radiative heating and cooling to restore the ambient temperature faster than the compression changes it.

Definition 1 (isothermal sound speed). For an ideal gas at a fixed temperature $T$ with mean molecular weight $\mu$ per free particle, the isothermal closure is $$ P = c_s^2\,\rho, \qquad c_s \equiv \sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{\mu\,m_u}}, \tag{2.4}$$ with $k_B$ Boltzmann's constant and $m_u$ the atomic mass unit. The constant $c_s$ is the isothermal sound speed, the speed $c_{\rm N}$ of Module 4, (1.1).

In cold molecular gas the hydrogen is molecular and the helium atomic. With one helium atom for every ten hydrogen nuclei, each $\mathrm{H_2}$ molecule comes with $0.2$ helium atoms. The mass per $\mathrm{H_2}$ molecule is $(2.016 + 0.2\times4.003)\,m_u = 2.8\,m_u$ and the number of particles is $1.2$, so $\mu = 2.8/1.2 = 7/3$. (Module 2 writes the particle mass as $\mu_{\rm m}m_p$. This module uses $\mu\,m_u$, as Modules 3 and 4 do.) Kandori et al. (2005) round this to $2.33$, and this module uses their value so that its numbers can be compared with their tables. The mass density is $\rho = 2.8\,m_u\,n(\mathrm{H_2})$. At $10$ K, (2.4) gives $c_s = 0.1889\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$.

The helium abundance of one atom per ten hydrogen nuclei and the rounded $\mu = 2.33$ are inputs, not checked against a source here. The isothermal closure is an assumption. §7.3 tests it against a measured temperature profile and a measured density profile of Barnard 68, and it fails there.

3. The Jeans instability

Module 4 perturbed a static equilibrium and found sound waves. The same method is applied here to a gas that makes its own gravity. The equilibrium Jeans chose is the simplest possible one: a medium of uniform density $\rho_0$ and uniform pressure $P_0$, at rest, filling all space. §4 shows that this state is not a solution of (2.1)–(2.3). This section follows Jeans and makes the assumption explicitly, so that its consequences can be traced.

Proposition 1 (the Jeans dispersion relation). Let the equilibrium be $\rho = \rho_0$, $P = c_s^2\rho_0$, $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$, all uniform, and assume that the equilibrium potential $\Phi_0$ has zero gradient. Let the perturbations of (2.1)–(2.4) be proportional to $\exp[i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x} - \omega t)]$, in the convention of Module 4. Then the linearised system has solutions with $\rho_1\neq0$ if and only if $$ \omega^2 = c_s^2k^2 - 4\pi G\rho_0, \tag{3.1}$$ with $k = |\mathbf{k}|$.

Write $\rho = \rho_0 + \rho_1$, $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}_1$, $\Phi = \Phi_0 + \Phi_1$ and keep terms of first order, as in Module 4, Definition 2. With $\rho_0$ uniform and $\nabla\Phi_0 = \mathbf{0}$ by assumption, (2.1), (2.2) with (2.4), and (2.3) become

$$ \frac{\partial\rho_1}{\partial t} + \rho_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}_1 = 0, \qquad \rho_0\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_1}{\partial t} = -c_s^2\nabla\rho_1 - \rho_0\nabla\Phi_1, \qquad \nabla^2\Phi_1 = 4\pi G\rho_1. $$

The term $\rho_1\nabla\Phi_0$ that would appear in the Euler equation vanishes only because of the assumption. Take the time derivative of the first equation and the divergence of the second, and eliminate $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}_1$:

$$ \frac{\partial^2\rho_1}{\partial t^2} = c_s^2\nabla^2\rho_1 + \rho_0\nabla^2\Phi_1 = c_s^2\nabla^2\rho_1 + 4\pi G\rho_0\,\rho_1. $$

Substituting $\rho_1\propto\exp[i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\omega t)]$ replaces $\partial^2/\partial t^2$ by $-\omega^2$ and $\nabla^2$ by $-k^2$, which gives $-\omega^2 = -c_s^2k^2 + 4\pi G\rho_0$ for any non-zero amplitude (with $k\neq0$, which the expression for $\Phi_1$ below requires). Conversely, a $\rho_1$ of this form with $\omega$ satisfying (3.1) determines $\Phi_1 = -4\pi G\rho_1/k^2$ and $\mathbf{u}_1 = (\omega/\rho_0k^2)\,\rho_1\mathbf{k}$ from the first and third equations, and these satisfy the second. ∎

With $G = 0$, (3.1) is the isothermal sound wave of Module 4. Gravity subtracts a constant from $\omega^2$. For short wavelengths the pressure term wins and the perturbation oscillates. For long wavelengths the right-hand side is negative, $\omega$ is imaginary, and one of the two solutions grows exponentially. Fig. 1 shows the relation.

Definition 2 (Jeans wavenumber, length and mass). The Jeans wavenumber $k_J$ is the wavenumber at which the right-hand side of (3.1) vanishes, and the Jeans length is the corresponding wavelength: $$ k_J^2 \equiv \frac{4\pi G\rho_0}{c_s^2}, \qquad \lambda_J \equiv \frac{2\pi}{k_J} = \left(\frac{\pi c_s^2}{G\rho_0}\right)^{1/2}. \tag{3.2}$$ The Jeans mass is the mass of a sphere of diameter $\lambda_J$: $$ M_J \equiv \frac{4\pi}{3}\rho_0\left(\frac{\lambda_J}{2}\right)^3 = \frac{\pi^{5/2}}{6}\,\frac{c_s^3}{G^{3/2}\rho_0^{1/2}}. \tag{3.3}$$

Perturbations with $\lambda\gt\lambda_J$ grow, and those with $\lambda\lt\lambda_J$ oscillate. The Jeans mass is a convention, not a derived threshold: (3.1) is a statement about plane waves, and the choice of a sphere of diameter $\lambda_J$ is one of several in use. A cube of side $\lambda_J$ gives a mass larger by $6/\pi = 1.9099$. The factor matters in §7.2, where the Jeans mass is compared with a measured mass, and it is stated there.

For a molecular cloud core at $10$ K and $n(\mathrm{H_2}) = 10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, so that $\rho_0 = 4.6495\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, (3.2) and (3.3) give $\lambda_J = 40177$ AU, or $0.19479$ pc, and $M_J = 2.6584\,M_\odot$.

3.1 Two timescales that differ by a fixed factor

The growth rate of an unstable mode is $s = (4\pi G\rho_0 - c_s^2k^2)^{1/2}$, and it is largest for $k\to0$, where $s\to(4\pi G\rho_0)^{1/2}$. The inverse of that limit is the growth time $t_{\rm grow} \equiv (4\pi G\rho_0)^{-1/2}$. A second timescale is used for the same purpose in the literature: the time a pressureless uniform sphere takes to collapse to a point.

Proposition 2 (free-fall time). A uniform sphere of density $\rho_0$, initially at rest and with no pressure, collapses to its centre in $$ t_{\rm ff} = \left(\frac{3\pi}{32\,G\rho_0}\right)^{1/2}, \qquad \frac{t_{\rm ff}}{t_{\rm grow}} = \pi\sqrt{\frac{3}{8}} = 1.9238, \tag{3.4}$$ independently of $\rho_0$, of temperature and of composition.

Take a shell initially at radius $R_0$ enclosing mass $M = (4\pi/3)\rho_0R_0^3$. Suppose shells do not cross, so the enclosed mass is constant and $\ddot r = -GM/r^2$. Energy conservation from rest gives $\dot r^2 = 2GM(1/r - 1/R_0)$. Substitute $r = R_0\cos^2\theta$, so that $dr = -2R_0\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta$ and $1/r - 1/R_0 = \tan^2\theta/R_0$. Then

$$ t = \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{2R_0\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta}{(2GM/R_0)^{1/2}\tan\theta} = \left(\frac{2R_0^3}{GM}\right)^{1/2}\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}\left(\frac{R_0^3}{2GM}\right)^{1/2}. $$

Using $R_0^3/M=3/(4\pi\rho_0)$ gives $t_{\rm ff}=(3\pi/32G\rho_0)^{1/2}$. Every shell has the same value of $R_0^3/M$, so $r/R_0$ follows the same time dependence for all shells. Their ordering is preserved, and they reach the origin simultaneously. The dimensionless time is $t_{\rm ff}(4\pi G\rho_0)^{1/2}=\pi\sqrt{3/8}$. ∎

The two timescales are the same order and are not equal. The free-fall time is $1.92$ growth times. For the $10$ K core, $t_{\rm grow} = 1.6047\times10^{5}$ yr and $t_{\rm ff} = 3.0871\times10^{5}$ yr. This module quotes whichever one a source uses and names it.

4. The Jeans swindle, and a background that has no swindle

Proposition 1 assumed that $\nabla\Phi_0 = \mathbf{0}$. The next proposition shows that this assumption contradicts the equations the proposition was derived from.

Proposition 3 (no uniform static self-gravitating equilibrium). There is no solution of (2.1)–(2.4) with $\rho = \rho_0\gt0$, $P = P_0$ and $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$ all uniform and constant in time.

With $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$ and $P_0$ uniform, (2.2) reduces to $\rho_0\nabla\Phi_0 = \mathbf{0}$, so $\nabla\Phi_0 = \mathbf{0}$ everywhere and $\nabla^2\Phi_0 = 0$. Poisson's equation (2.3) then requires $4\pi G\rho_0 = 0$, which contradicts $\rho_0\gt0$. ∎

Jeans's derivation keeps the perturbed Poisson equation $\nabla^2\Phi_1 = 4\pi G\rho_1$ and drops the unperturbed one. This step is known as the Jeans swindle. The name is not a judgement that (3.1) is useless. It is a statement that its derivation is inconsistent.

The size of what is dropped can be computed. Two parcels a distance $L$ apart in a uniform medium accelerate towards each other at $g(L) = (4\pi/3)G\rho_0L$, by Gauss's theorem applied about either one. The time over which that acceleration changes a distance $L$ appreciably is $(L/g)^{1/2}$. Its ratio to the growth time is

$$ \frac{(L/g)^{1/2}}{t_{\rm grow}} = \left(\frac{3}{4\pi G\rho_0}\cdot4\pi G\rho_0\right)^{1/2} = \sqrt3 = 1.7321, \tag{4.1}$$

for every $L$, density and temperature. For the $10$ K core at $L = \lambda_J$, $g = 7.8128\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}$ and $(L/g)^{1/2} = 2.7794\times10^{5}$ yr against $t_{\rm grow} = 1.6047\times10^{5}$ yr. The discarded background acceleration acts on the same timescale as the instability it is used to derive. It is not a small correction that can be neglected in a first approximation. (The time to fall a distance $L$ from rest at constant $g$ is $(2L/g)^{1/2}$, larger by $\sqrt2$. The conclusion is the same.)

There are two ways to remove the inconsistency. The first keeps the medium uniform and lets it move: an expanding or contracting uniform medium is an exact solution. The second keeps it static and lets the density vary: a stratified cloud is an exact solution. This section takes the first; §5 takes the second.

4.1 The expanding background

Proposition 4 (a uniform self-gravitating medium in homologous motion). Let $a(t)\gt0$ satisfy $$ \ddot a = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\rho_0(t)\,a, \qquad \rho_0(t)\,a(t)^3 = \text{constant}, \tag{4.2}$$ and let $H(t)\equiv\dot a/a$. Then $\rho = \rho_0(t)$, $\mathbf{u}_0 = H\mathbf{x}$, $\Phi_0 = (2\pi/3)G\rho_0|\mathbf{x}|^2$ and any spatially uniform $P_0(t)$ solve (2.1)–(2.3) exactly.

Continuity (2.1): $\nabla\cdot(\rho_0H\mathbf{x}) = 3H\rho_0$, and $\dot\rho_0 = -3H\rho_0$ follows from $\rho_0a^3$ constant, so the sum vanishes. Poisson (2.3): $\nabla^2[(2\pi/3)G\rho_0|\mathbf{x}|^2] = (2\pi/3)G\rho_0\cdot6 = 4\pi G\rho_0$. Euler (2.2): $\partial_t(H\mathbf{x}) + (H\mathbf{x}\cdot\nabla)(H\mathbf{x}) = (\dot H + H^2)\mathbf{x} = (\ddot a/a)\mathbf{x} = -(4\pi G/3)\rho_0\mathbf{x}$, and $-\nabla\Phi_0 = -(4\pi G/3)\rho_0\mathbf{x}$, while $\nabla P_0 = \mathbf{0}$. ∎

The background potential is now kept, and it is balanced by the deceleration of the expansion rather than by a pressure gradient. The choice of origin in $\Phi_0$ is arbitrary: moving the origin adds a uniform velocity and a uniform acceleration, which changes no relative motion. The perturbation problem about this background has no swindle in it.

Proposition 5 (the Jeans equation in an expanding medium). About the background of Proposition 4, write $\mathbf{x} = a(t)\mathbf{q}$, $\rho = \rho_0(t)[1 + \delta]$, $\mathbf{u} = H\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{v}$ and $\Phi = \Phi_0 + \phi$, and let $P - P_0 = c_s^2(\rho - \rho_0)$. For a perturbation $\delta\propto\exp(i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{q})$ with fixed comoving wavevector $\mathbf{k}$, to first order, $$ \ddot\delta + 2H\dot\delta = \left(4\pi G\rho_0 - \frac{c_s^2k^2}{a^2}\right)\delta, \tag{4.3}$$ where the dot is a time derivative at fixed $\mathbf{q}$.

At fixed $\mathbf{q}$, $\partial_t|_{\mathbf{q}} = \partial_t|_{\mathbf{x}} + H\mathbf{x}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}$, and $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} = a^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}$. Substitute into (2.1). The background terms cancel by Proposition 4, and the first-order remainder is $\rho_0[\partial_t\delta|_{\mathbf{x}} + H\mathbf{x}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\delta] + \rho_0\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$, that is

$$ \dot\delta = -\frac{1}{a}\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{v}. $$

In (2.2), expand $\partial_t|_{\mathbf{x}}(H\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{v}) + [(H\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{v})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}](H\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{v})$. The zeroth-order part $(\dot H + H^2)\mathbf{x}$ cancels against $-\nabla\Phi_0$. The first-order part is $\partial_t\mathbf{v}|_{\mathbf{x}} + H\mathbf{x}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{v} + H\mathbf{v} = \dot{\mathbf{v}} + H\mathbf{v}$. Because $\rho_0$ is uniform, $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}P/\rho = c_s^2\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\delta$ to first order, so

$$ \dot{\mathbf{v}} + H\mathbf{v} = -\frac{c_s^2}{a}\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\delta - \frac{1}{a}\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\phi, \qquad \frac{1}{a^2}\nabla^2_{\mathbf{q}}\phi = 4\pi G\rho_0\,\delta, $$

the second from (2.3). Apply $a^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\cdot$ to the Euler equation. On the left, $a^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{d}{dt}(a^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{v}) + H\,a^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{v} = -\ddot\delta - H\dot\delta$, and the $H\mathbf{v}$ term gives $-H\dot\delta$. On the right, the two terms give $-c_s^2a^{-2}\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}^2\delta - 4\pi G\rho_0\delta$. So $\ddot\delta + 2H\dot\delta = c_s^2a^{-2}\nabla^2_{\mathbf{q}}\delta + 4\pi G\rho_0\delta$, and $\nabla^2_{\mathbf{q}}\to-k^2$ gives (4.3). ∎

Equation (4.3) has the sign structure of (3.1): the physical wavenumber is $k/a$, and the right-hand side is positive, driving growth, exactly when $k/a\lt k_J$. The Jeans length reappears without any discarded term. The new term $2H\dot\delta$ is a drag produced by the expansion. It changes the growth from exponential to a power law.

To see this, take a background with zero pressure in which the expansion is set by the gas itself, $a\propto t^{2/3}$. Then $H = 2/(3t)$, and (4.2) gives $4\pi G\rho_0 = 2/(3t^2)$. In the limit $k\to0$, (4.3) becomes $\ddot\delta + (4/3t)\dot\delta - (2/3t^2)\delta = 0$. Trying $\delta\propto t^p$ gives $p^2 + p/3 - 2/3 = 0$, so $p = 2/3$ or $p = -1$. The growing mode is $\delta\propto t^{2/3}\propto a$.

For the growing mode, a perturbation beginning at $z_*=1089.92$ grows in proportion to the scale factor. The available growth factor is therefore $1+z_*=1090.92$, rather than an exponential of elapsed time divided by $t_{\rm grow}$. Initial data with $\dot\delta=0$ place $3/5$ of the amplitude in this growing mode.

The background $a\propto t^{2/3}$ assumes that pressureless matter alone sets the expansion. The real universe differs at both ends of the interval: radiation contributes to the expansion near $z_*$, and dark energy at late times. The factor $1090.92$ is therefore the value for this model background only. The correction for the measured cosmology is not computed here. The Newtonian treatment also assumes wavelengths far inside the Hubble radius $c/H$ and a pressure far below $\rho c^2$, with $c$ the speed of light.

5. A static equilibrium that exists: the isothermal sphere

The second repair keeps the gas at rest and gives up uniformity. A static isothermal gas that makes its own gravity must be stratified, as the atmospheres of Module 3 were. Module 3 solved the polytropic case, $P\propto\rho^{1+1/n}$, through the Lane–Emden equation. The isothermal closure (2.4) is the limit $n\to\infty$, and it needs its own variables.

Definition 3 (the isothermal Lane–Emden equation). Let a static, spherically symmetric gas obey (2.4), and let $\rho_c$ be its central density. Define $$ \rho = \rho_c\,e^{-\psi}, \qquad r = \alpha\,\xi, \qquad \alpha\equiv\frac{c_s}{(4\pi G\rho_c)^{1/2}}. \tag{5.1}$$
Proposition 6 (the isothermal sphere). Under Definition 3, hydrostatic equilibrium and Poisson's equation reduce to $$ \frac{1}{\xi^2}\frac{d}{d\xi}\left(\xi^2\frac{d\psi}{d\xi}\right) = e^{-\psi}, \qquad \psi(0) = \psi'(0) = 0. \tag{5.2}$$ Near the centre, $\psi=\xi^2/6-\xi^4/120+O(\xi^6)$. The mass inside $\xi$ is $M(\xi)=4\pi\rho_c\alpha^3\xi^2\psi'(\xi)$, equivalently $M=(c_s^2r/G)\xi\psi'(\xi)$. The equation also has the singular solution $\psi=\ln(\xi^2/2)$, or $\rho=c_s^2/(2\pi Gr^2)$. Its regular solution has $\rho>0$ at every finite $\xi$, so an untruncated isothermal sphere has no surface.

With $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$, (2.2) and (2.4) give $c_s^2\nabla\rho = -\rho\nabla\Phi$, so $c_s^2\ln\rho + \Phi$ is constant and $\psi = (\Phi - \Phi_c)/c_s^2$, with $\Phi_c$ the central potential. Poisson's equation in spherical symmetry is $r^{-2}(r^2\Phi')' = 4\pi G\rho_c e^{-\psi}$. Dividing by $c_s^2$ and using $r = \alpha\xi$ with $\alpha^2 = c_s^2/(4\pi G\rho_c)$ gives (5.2). Regularity at the centre requires $\Phi'(0) = 0$, so $\psi'(0) = 0$, and $\psi(0) = 0$ by the definition of $\rho_c$.

(a) Insert $\psi = A\xi^2 + B\xi^4$ and $e^{-\psi} = 1 - A\xi^2 + O(\xi^4)$ into (5.2). The left side is $6A + 20B\xi^2$. Matching powers gives $A = 1/6$ and $B = -A/20 = -1/120$.

(b) $M(\xi) = \int_0^{\alpha\xi}4\pi r^2\rho\,dr = 4\pi\rho_c\alpha^3\int_0^{\xi}\xi'^2e^{-\psi}d\xi'$. By (5.2) the integrand is $(\xi'^2\psi')'$, so $M = 4\pi\rho_c\alpha^3\xi^2\psi'$. Since $4\pi\rho_c\alpha^2 = c_s^2/G$ and $\alpha\xi = r$, this is $(c_s^2r/G)\,\xi\psi'$.

(c) With $\psi = \ln(\xi^2/2)$, $\xi^2\psi' = 2\xi$, the left side of (5.2) is $2/\xi^2$, and $e^{-\psi} = 2/\xi^2$. In dimensional form $\rho = \rho_c\cdot2\alpha^2/r^2 = c_s^2/(2\pi Gr^2)$. It does not satisfy the central conditions.

(d) $\rho = \rho_ce^{-\psi}$ and $\psi$ is finite wherever the solution exists. Since $\xi^2\psi' = \int_0^\xi\xi'^2e^{-\psi}d\xi'\gt0$, $\psi$ increases, and $\psi'\le\xi/3$ because $e^{-\psi}\le1$; so $\psi\le\xi^2/6$ stays finite for every finite $\xi$. ∎

Property (d) is the whole difference from Module 3. A polytrope with $n\lt5$ reaches zero density at a finite radius. The isothermal sphere never does; its mass grows without bound as $\xi\to\infty$, since by (b) and (c) the mass approaches $2c_s^2r/G$. A real isothermal cloud must therefore be cut off by something outside it.

A fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme integrates equation (5.2) from the central series. The two agree to $4\times10^{-6}$ at $\xi=0.05$ and $2\times10^{-7}$ at $\xi=0.2$. At large radius, the regular solution oscillates around the singular isothermal sphere with decreasing amplitude. Its logarithmic slope reaches a universal maximum $\xi\psi'=2.5176$ at $\xi=8.993$ before approaching 2 through damped oscillations. Section 7.3 uses this upper bound.

Definition 4 (Bonnor–Ebert sphere). A Bonnor–Ebert sphere is the solution of (5.2) truncated at a dimensionless radius $\xi_{\max}$ and held there by an external pressure equal to the gas pressure at the boundary: $$ R = \alpha\,\xi_{\max}, \qquad P_{\rm ext} = c_s^2\rho_c\,e^{-\psi(\xi_{\max})}. \tag{5.3}$$ Its centre-to-edge density contrast is $\rho_c/\rho(R) = e^{\psi(\xi_{\max})}$, a function of $\xi_{\max}$ alone.

For a fixed temperature, a Bonnor–Ebert sphere has two parameters, $\rho_c$ and $\xi_{\max}$. Its shape, the profile $\rho/\rho_c$ against $r/R$, depends only on $\xi_{\max}$. This is what makes the model testable against a single measured profile: one number fixes the shape.

Proposition 7 (dimensionless mass and radius). At fixed $c_s$ and $P_{\rm ext}$, the mass and radius of a Bonnor–Ebert sphere are $$ M = \frac{c_s^4}{G^{3/2}P_{\rm ext}^{1/2}}\,m(\xi_{\max}), \qquad m(\xi)\equiv\frac{\xi^2\psi'(\xi)\,e^{-\psi(\xi)/2}}{(4\pi)^{1/2}}, \tag{5.4}$$ $$ R = \frac{c_s^2}{G^{1/2}P_{\rm ext}^{1/2}}\,\tilde r(\xi_{\max}), \qquad \tilde r(\xi)\equiv\frac{\xi\,e^{-\psi(\xi)/2}}{(4\pi)^{1/2}}. \tag{5.5}$$ At fixed $c_s$ and fixed mass $M$, the radius is $R = GM/[c_s^2\,\xi_{\max}\psi'(\xi_{\max})]$.

From (5.3), $\rho_c = (P_{\rm ext}/c_s^2)e^{\psi}$, so $\alpha = c_s^2/(4\pi GP_{\rm ext})^{1/2}\cdot e^{-\psi/2}$. Then $R = \alpha\xi$ is (5.5), and Proposition 6(b) gives $M = 4\pi\rho_c\alpha^3\xi^2\psi' = 4\pi(P_{\rm ext}/c_s^2)e^{\psi}\cdot c_s^6(4\pi GP_{\rm ext})^{-3/2}e^{-3\psi/2}\,\xi^2\psi'$, which simplifies to (5.4). The last statement is Proposition 6(b) evaluated at $r = R$. ∎

The function $m(\xi)$ rises from zero, reaches a maximum and falls. The maximum, located on the numerical solution and refined by a parabola through the three bracketing samples, is

$$ \xi_{\rm crit} = 6.4508, \qquad m_{\rm crit} = 1.1822, \qquad \frac{\rho_c}{\rho(R)}\bigg|_{\rm crit} = 14.042. \tag{5.6}$$

Halving the integration step moves $\xi_{\rm crit}$ by $8.7\times10^{-8}$ and $m_{\rm crit}$ by $2.9\times10^{-9}$. The coefficient $1.18$ in the Bonnor–Ebert mass $M_{\rm BE} = 1.18\,c_s^4/(G^{3/2}P_{\rm ext}^{1/2})$ is $m_{\rm crit}$. Kandori et al. (2005) give the critical state as $\xi_{\max} = 6.5$ with a contrast of $14$, which (5.6) reproduces to rounding.

The maximum has a direct consequence that needs no stability theory. For a given external pressure and temperature, no isothermal sphere in hydrostatic equilibrium has a mass above $M_{\rm BE}$. A cloud of larger mass in that environment has no static state to be in. The stability statement is the next proposition, and it needs an assumption.

Proposition 8 (the Bonnor–Ebert criterion). Consider the sequence of Bonnor–Ebert spheres of fixed mass $M$ and fixed $c_s$, parametrised by $\xi_{\max}$. Assume the static stability criterion on this range: for $0\lt\xi_{\max}\lt8.9937$, an equilibrium is stable against radial compression if and only if $d\ln P_{\rm ext}/d\ln R\lt0$ along the sequence, so that squeezing the sphere requires a larger external pressure. Then, on $0\lt\xi_{\max}\lt8.9937$, the sphere is stable for $\xi_{\max}\lt\xi_{\rm crit} = 6.4508$ and unstable for $\xi_{\rm crit}\lt\xi_{\max}\lt8.9937$.

At fixed $M$ and $c_s$, (5.4) gives $P_{\rm ext}\propto m(\xi)^2$, and Proposition 7 gives $R\propto1/(\xi\psi')$. Along the sequence,

$$ \frac{d\ln P_{\rm ext}}{d\ln R} = \frac{2\,m'/m}{-(\xi\psi')'/(\xi\psi')}. $$

The denominator is negative wherever $\xi\psi'$ increases, which the numerical solution shows for $0\lt\xi\lt8.9937$, the location of the slope maximum above. So the sign of $d\ln P_{\rm ext}/d\ln R$ is the opposite of the sign of $m'$. The numerical solution has $m'\gt0$ below $\xi_{\rm crit}$ and $m'\lt0$ above it, and a scan of $d\ln P_{\rm ext}/d\ln R$ on $0.05\lt\xi\lt8.99$ changes sign only at $\xi = 6.451$. As $\xi\to0$ the ratio tends to $-3$: a nearly uniform sphere obeys Boyle's law $P\propto R^{-3}$. ∎

Two limits of the proposition must be stated. First, it rests on the static criterion, which is an assumption here; a linear analysis of radial pulsations of the sphere is not carried out in this module. Second, it says nothing beyond $\xi_{\max} = 8.9937$, where the radius at fixed mass reaches its minimum, $R_{\min} = GM/(2.5176\,c_s^2)$, and the sign test is no longer used. The test must not be used there: at $\xi_{\max} = 10$ it returns $d\ln P_{\rm ext}/d\ln R = -12.09$, which would read as stable, on a sphere that lies further along the unstable branch. The general tool is the turning-point method, in which stability changes only where the equilibrium sequence has an extremum. It is not developed here. The criterion is applied below only to values of $\xi_{\max}$ on the range where it holds, or to state that a value lies above $\xi_{\rm crit}$.

The only rate that an isothermal sphere contains is $(G\rho_c)^{1/2}$ multiplied by a function of $\xi_{\max}$. The timescale on which an unstable sphere departs from equilibrium is therefore $(G\rho_c)^{-1/2}$ times a factor that depends on $\xi_{\max}$. The factor becomes infinite at $\xi_{\rm crit}$, where the growth rate passes through zero, and it is not computed here.

A short program reproduces (5.6). It integrates (5.2) and locates the maximum of $m(\xi)$ on the grid.

import numpy as np


def rhs(x, y):
    psi, dpsi = y
    return np.array([dpsi, np.exp(-psi) - 2*dpsi/x])


h, x = 1e-3, 1e-3
y = np.array([x*x/6, x/3])
best = (0.0, 0.0)
while x < 12:
    k1 = rhs(x, y)
    k2 = rhs(x + h/2, y + h*k1/2)
    k3 = rhs(x + h/2, y + h*k2/2)
    k4 = rhs(x + h, y + h*k3)
    y, x = y + h*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)/6, x + h
    m = x*x*y[1]*np.exp(-y[0]/2)/np.sqrt(4*np.pi)
    best = max(best, (m, x))
m, xc = best
print(f"xi_crit = {xc:.3f}, m_crit = {m:.4f}")

Running it prints $\xi_{\rm crit} = 6.451$ and $m_{\rm crit} = 1.1822$. The grid step of $10^{-3}$ limits $\xi_{\rm crit}$ to three decimals; figs/m05_numbers.py refines it.

6. Barnard 68 and the Bok globules

A Bok globule is a small, isolated dark cloud, seen against the stars behind it. The dust in the cloud dims and reddens those stars, and the reddening of each star measures the column of material in front of it. Mapping many stars gives the column density across the cloud, and averaging it in rings gives a radial profile. A Bonnor–Ebert sphere projected onto the sky predicts that profile with one shape parameter.

Kandori et al. (2005) fitted Bonnor–Ebert spheres to near-infrared extinction profiles of ten globules and tabulated, with them, fits from the literature to four more, including Alves, Lada & Lada (2001) for Barnard 68. Their Table 4 has fifteen rows, because the Coalsack appears twice, from two different papers. Each row gives $\xi_{\max}$ with its fitted error and a centre-to-edge contrast. Their Table 5 gives the temperature, distance, radius, mass and external pressure.

For Barnard 68 the fit is $\xi_{\max} = 6.9\pm0.2$, a precision of $2.9$ per cent. The first Barnard 68 line of Table 5 gives $T = 16$ K, a distance of $125$ pc, $R = 1.25\times10^{4}$ AU, $M = 2.10\,M_\odot$ and $P_{\rm ext}/k_B = 1.8\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$. With $\mu = 2.33$, $c_s = 0.2389\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. The mean density from $M$ and $R$ is $1.5245\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, against $1.5\times10^{-19}$ quoted by Burkert & Alves (2009), a ratio of $1.0163$. The free-fall time (3.4) at that density is $1.7049\times10^{5}$ yr, against their $0.17\times10^{6}$ yr, a ratio of $1.0029$.

What the fit determines. The profile fixes the shape, $\xi_{\max}$, but not the scale. Kandori et al. (2005, section 4.2.2) show, crediting Lai et al. (2003), that substituting $R\propto d$ and $\rho_c\propto d^{-1}$ for a cloud at distance $d$ into the definition of $\xi_{\max}$ gives $T/d = \text{constant}$. The same fitted shape is therefore consistent with a line of temperature-distance pairs. The second Barnard 68 line of Table 5, from Hotzel, Harju & Juvela (2002), takes the measured temperature $10\pm1.2$ K and reads $d = 85$ pc, $R = 0.85\times10^{4}$ AU and $M = 0.90\,M_\odot$. At fixed $\xi_{\max}$ the mass scales as $c_s^2R\propto Td$, which predicts a mass ratio of $2.3529$ between the two lines. The table gives $2.3333$, a ratio of $0.9917$.

Is the support thermal? Lada et al. (2003) measure a gas temperature of $10.5$ K and line widths of $0.18\pm0.01\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ in $\mathrm{C^{18}O}$ and $0.15\pm0.01\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ in $\mathrm{C^{34}S}$. The thermal width (full width at half maximum) of a $\mathrm{C^{18}O}$ molecule, mass $30\,m_u$, at $10.5$ K is $0.1270\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Subtracting it in quadrature leaves a non-thermal width of $0.1275\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. The thermal width of the mean particle, mass $2.33\,m_u$, is $0.4558\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, $3.57$ times larger. Lada et al. state from their own analysis that thermal pressure exceeds non-thermal pressure by a factor of $4$ to $5$ in the central regions. The premise that pressure is mainly thermal is supported. The premise that it is isothermal is tested in §7.3.

Numerical consistency check. Kandori et al. derived the contrast column of their Table 4 from $\xi_{\max}$. Integrating equation (5.2) reproduces all fifteen entries from $6.63$ to $364$. The largest residual is $2.2$ per cent for CB 110, while Barnard 68 gives $16.56$ against the tabulated $16.6$. This agreement verifies the integration and parameter convention but supplies no independent evidence about the clouds.

Source note. The Bok-globule values come from Tables 4 and 5 of Kandori et al. (2005). Results attributed there to Alves et al. (2001), Hotzel et al. (2002), and Lai et al. (2003) are used only through Kandori's compilation. The Gaussian line widths from Lada et al. (2003) are treated as full widths at half maximum, consistent with their equation 2.

7. The checks

Three statements are tested against measurements. The first is that the fitted globules are stable static equilibria. The second compares the two stability criteria on one cloud. The third is that the gas of Barnard 68 is isothermal.

7.1 Check 1: the fitted cores are not stable equilibria

Kandori et al. place Lynds 694-2 in their star-forming category because of evidence for inward gas motion. That classification should not be restated as a detection of an embedded star: their discussion distinguishes it from Barnard 335, which has an identified Class 0 source. The quoted Bonnor–Ebert fit can still be compared with the critical dimensionless radius, subject to the equilibrium model's assumptions.

The instability rate has the form $(G\rho_c)^{1/2}f(\xi_{\max})$. The function $f$ vanishes at $\xi_{\rm crit}$, where the equilibrium sequence changes stability. Barnard 68 lies only $6.96$ per cent above this threshold, so its departure from equilibrium can be much slower than the free-fall time of $1.70\times10^5$ yr computed from its mean density.

The tension is sharpest for Barnard 68 because it has been read as long-lived. Lada et al. (2003) interpret its line profiles as oscillations of its outer layers. Burkert & Alves (2009) note that on this reading the cloud should be older than one oscillation time, $2R/V = 3\times10^{6}$ yr for velocities $V\approx0.04\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. That is $17.6$ free-fall times at the mean density. They state this in order to argue against it, and propose that a future collision with a small core will trigger its collapse. Kandori et al. offer a different resolution: a sphere that begins to collapse from a nearly critical state "should mimic marginally unstable Bonnor-Ebert spheres for a long time". Neither resolution is tested here.

Check 1: most fitted globules are unstable under the static isothermal criterion. Proposition 8 gives $\xi_{\rm crit}=6.4508$. Eight of eleven starless globules exceed this value, and Barnard 68 has $\xi_{\max}=6.9\pm0.2$, or $2.25\sigma$ above the threshold. The conclusion applies only if the fitted isothermal sphere is interpreted as a static equilibrium.

7.2 The anchor: why the Jeans criterion cannot see the instability

The Jeans criterion can be applied to Barnard 68 at its own mean density and at the temperature its own fit requires. With $\rho_0 = 1.5245\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $c_s = 0.2389\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, (3.2) gives $\lambda_J = 28066$ AU, or $0.1361$ pc, which is $1.1226$ times the cloud's diameter of $25000$ AU. Equation (3.3) gives $M_J = 2.9713\,M_\odot$, against the published $2.10\,M_\odot$, a ratio $M_J/M = 1.4149$. The Jeans criterion calls the cloud stable. The Bonnor–Ebert criterion calls it unstable at $2.25\sigma$. The next proposition shows that the first conclusion is not specific to Barnard 68.

Proposition 9 (the Jeans conclusion on any Bonnor–Ebert sphere). Let a Bonnor–Ebert sphere have truncation radius $\xi_{\max}$, and mass $M$ and radius $R$ related by Proposition 7. Let $M_J$ be the Jeans mass (3.3) at its mean density $\bar\rho = 3M/(4\pi R^3)$ and its own $c_s$. Then $$ \frac{M_J}{M} = \frac{\pi^{5/2}}{6}\left(\frac{4\pi}{3}\right)^{1/2}\big[\xi_{\max}\psi'(\xi_{\max})\big]^{-3/2} \ge 1.4938, \tag{7.1}$$ with the minimum at $\xi_{\max} = 8.993$. The ratio depends on $\xi_{\max}$ alone, not on $T$, the distance or $P_{\rm ext}$.

Define $S=\xi_{\max}\psi'(\xi_{\max})$. Proposition 7 gives $M=c_s^2RS/G$. Substituting this relation and $\bar\rho=3M/(4\pi R^3)$ into equation (3.3) cancels every dimensional factor. The result depends only on $S^{-3/2}$. Its minimum follows from the numerical maximum $S=2.5176$ at $\xi=8.993$. ∎

At $\xi_{\max} = 6.9$, (7.1) gives $1.5409$; at $\xi_{\rm crit}$ it gives $1.5709$. The published value $1.4149$ differs from $1.5409$ by the factor $(2.10/1.9839)^{3/2}$, the internal inconsistency of the published row found below. The Jeans criterion at the mean density therefore calls every isothermal equilibrium sphere stable, including every sphere on the unstable branch. This is not an observation about Barnard 68. It is what the uniform-background assumption of Proposition 1 costs.

The two consistent entries in Table 5 have the same $\xi_{\max}$ and nearly equal $M_J/M$: $1.4149$ and $1.3972$. Their $1.25$ per cent difference reflects rounding in the tabulated masses and radii. The alternative value $0.7522$ combines a temperature and mass that violate the Bonnor–Ebert scaling $M\propto Td$ at fixed $\xi_{\max}$, so it does not represent the fitted sphere.

The two criteria disagree because they ask different questions. The Jeans criterion asks whether a uniform medium of the cloud's mean density is unstable. The cloud is not uniform: its centre is $16.6$ times denser than its edge. The Bonnor–Ebert criterion is stated in exactly that contrast. The uniform medium that the Jeans criterion assumes is the state that Proposition 3 showed cannot exist.

Anchor. Barnard 68 (Kandori et al. 2005, Tables 4 and 5): Jeans $M_J/M = 1.4149$ (stable) against Bonnor–Ebert $\xi_{\max}/\xi_{\rm crit} = 1.0696\pm0.0310$ (unstable). By Proposition 9, $M_J/M\ge1.4938$ for every self-consistent Bonnor–Ebert sphere, so the Jeans criterion at mean density cannot return "unstable" for any of them; $1.4149$ lies below that bound only because the published $M$ and $R$ disagree by $13.4$ per cent.

The two reconstructions differ by $13.4$ per cent. This is a consistency discrepancy between the adopted inputs and model relations, not a calibrated uncertainty on every mass estimate for the cloud. A mass uncertainty would require an error budget for the measurements, their covariance and the modelling assumptions. We retain both results to show the sensitivity of this reconstruction.

The mass and radius of Barnard 68 are those of the Bonnor–Ebert fit as tabulated by Kandori et al. (2005), not independent measurements. The $13.4$ per cent inconsistency is printed rather than resolved; neither route is preferred.

7.3 Check 2: the outer profile of Barnard 68 is not a static isothermal sphere

Nielbock et al. (2012) modelled Herschel far-infrared maps of Barnard 68 with radiative transfer and derived the radial profiles of dust temperature and gas density. Two of their results bear on the closure.

The first is the temperature. The dust temperature falls from $16.7^{+1.3}_{-1.0}$ K at the edge to $8.2^{+2.1}_{-0.7}$ K at the centre, a factor of $2.04$. An isothermal sphere assumes a factor of $1$. Dust temperature is not gas temperature, so this is suggestive rather than decisive.

The second is the density slope, which Nielbock et al. derive from the same radiative-transfer model. Their abstract gives $n_{\rm H}\propto r^{-3.5}$ outside the inner plateau. §5 showed that the local slope $-d\ln\rho/d\ln r$ of an isothermal sphere does not exceed $2.5176$ on the integrated range, for any $\xi_{\max}$. The measured slope is $1.390$ times that ceiling.

The observed profile is $n_{\rm H}(r)=\Delta n/[1+(r/r_0)^2]^{\eta/2}+n_{\rm out}$, with $r_0=35''$, $\eta=4.0$, $n_0=3.4\times10^5\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, and $n_{\rm out}=4\times10^2\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. The fitted Bonnor–Ebert model has $\xi_{\max}=6.9$ at $100''$. Comparing logarithmic slopes at equal angular radius eliminates the uncertain distance. Beyond $46.2''$, the observed slope exceeds the isothermal-sphere maximum. At $100''$, it is $3.2428$, compared with $2.4660$ for the fitted sphere.

Check 2: the outer half of Barnard 68 is inconsistent with a static isothermal sphere. At $100^{\prime\prime}$, the measured local density slope is $3.2428$, whereas every isothermal sphere has a ceiling of $2.5176$. The observed slope exceeds the ceiling for radii beyond $46.2^{\prime\prime}$. Across the same cloud, the dust temperature varies by a factor of $2.04$.

The slope comparison excludes the combined assumptions of a static, spherical, isothermal, self-gravitating outer envelope. It does not identify which assumption fails. An outward temperature rise can support a steeper density profile, while asymmetry or motion offer other explanations. Because $\xi_{\max}=6.9$ comes from the same isothermal fit, the $2.25\sigma$ result of Check 1 is conditional on a model that fails over the outer $53.8$ per cent of the fitted radius.

Model dependence of the density profile. Nielbock et al. infer the profile from a spherical radiative-transfer model with assumed opacity and gas-to-dust ratio. Their alternative fits give outer slopes from $3.4$ to $3.7$, while the adopted fit gives $\eta=4.0$. All exceed the isothermal ceiling, so the conclusion is insensitive to the chosen fit. The assumed distance of $150$ pc cancels from the angular comparison.

Conceptual ↑ contents

C1. Recompute the Jeans mass of the warm neutral medium of Table 1 with the adiabatic sound speed, $\gamma = 5/3$, in place of the isothermal one. Give the factor, and state what physical condition decides which of the two is correct for a given cloud.
Solution
By (3.3), $M_J\propto c^3$. The adiabatic sound speed is $\gamma^{1/2}$ times the isothermal one, so $M_J$ rises by $\gamma^{3/2} = 2.1517$, from $2.980\times10^{7}$ to $6.412\times10^{7}\,M_\odot$. For the $10$ K core the same factor takes $2.6584$ to $5.7200\,M_\odot$. The closure describes how the pressure of a compressed parcel responds. If the gas radiates the heat of compression away in a time short compared with the growth time, the parcel stays at the ambient temperature and the isothermal closure holds. If the cooling time is long compared with the growth time, the heat stays and the adiabatic closure holds. Proposition 1 needs the closure to hold on the timescale of the instability, not in general. The cooling times themselves are not computed in this module.
C2. Two Bonnor–Ebert spheres at the same temperature and external pressure have $\xi_{\max} = 6$ and $\xi_{\max} = 7$. Compare their masses, say which is stable, and name a quantity an observer could measure to tell them apart.
Solution
By (5.4), $m(6) = 1.17882$ and $m(7) = 1.17825$. The masses differ by $0.049$ per cent, which no mass measurement resolves. By Proposition 8 the sphere at $\xi_{\max} = 6$ is stable and the one at $7$ is unstable, since $\xi_{\rm crit} = 6.4508$. Their contrasts differ by a factor of $1.46$: $e^{\psi(6)} = 11.790$ against $e^{\psi(7)} = 17.160$, either side of the critical $14.042$. The contrast, or equivalently the shape of the column-density profile, separates them. This is why Check 1 uses $\xi_{\max}$ and not the mass.
C3. Equation (4.1) gives the ratio $\sqrt3$ for the $10$ K core. Compute the same ratio for the warm neutral medium of Table 1 at $L = \lambda_J$, explain why the two agree, and explain why the expanding background of Proposition 4 does not need the step that (4.1) measures.
Solution
For the warm neutral medium, $g = 1.4950\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}$ at $L = \lambda_J = 1490.9$ pc, $(L/g)^{1/2} = 5.5587\times10^{7}$ yr and $t_{\rm grow} = 3.2093\times10^{7}$ yr: the ratio is $1.7321$. The two agree because $L/g = 3/(4\pi G\rho_0)$ does not contain $L$, and $t_{\rm grow}^2 = 1/(4\pi G\rho_0)$. The density cancels and temperature never entered. The discarded acceleration is always as fast as the instability. In Proposition 4 the acceleration $-\nabla\Phi_0$ is not discarded: it equals $(\ddot a/a)\mathbf{x}$, the deceleration of the expansion. The background is an exact solution, so there is nothing to discard.

Derivational ↑ contents

D1. Show that in the limit $k\to0$ the growth time of (3.1) and the free-fall time of (3.4) differ by a factor independent of every property of the gas, and evaluate both for the $10$ K core of Table 1.
Solution
As $k\to0$, $s\to(4\pi G\rho_0)^{1/2}$, so $t_{\rm grow} = (4\pi G\rho_0)^{-1/2}$. Proposition 2 gives $t_{\rm ff} = (3\pi/32G\rho_0)^{1/2}$. Their ratio is $(3\pi\cdot4\pi/32)^{1/2} = \pi(3/8)^{1/2} = 1.923825$. Both scale as $\rho_0^{-1/2}$ and neither contains $c_s$, $T$ or $\mu$, so the ratio is a pure number. For $\rho_0 = 4.6495\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $t_{\rm grow} = 1.6047\times10^{5}$ yr and $t_{\rm ff} = 3.0871\times10^{5}$ yr. The growth time describes the exponential phase of a small perturbation. The free-fall time describes the collapse of a whole uniform sphere from rest to a point. They answer different questions and agree only in order of magnitude.
D2. (a) Show that $\rho(z) = \rho_0\,\mathrm{sech}^2(z/2H)$ with $H = c_s/(8\pi G\rho_0)^{1/2}$ is a static isothermal self-gravitating equilibrium, and that its surface density is $\Sigma = 4\rho_0H$. (b) Treat the layer as infinitely thin. Given that a surface density perturbation $\Sigma_1e^{ikx}$ produces the potential $\Phi_1 = -(2\pi G\Sigma_1/|k|)\,e^{ikx - |k||z|}$, derive $\omega^2 = c_s^2k^2 - 2\pi G\Sigma|k|$ and find the critical and fastest-growing wavelengths. (c) Evaluate for the $10$ K core density, and check whether the thin-layer assumption holds at those wavelengths.
Solution
(a) Hydrostatic balance with (2.4) gives $c_s^2\,d\ln\rho/dz = -d\Phi/dz$, and Poisson gives $d^2\Phi/dz^2 = 4\pi G\rho$, so $c_s^2\,d^2\ln\rho/dz^2 = -4\pi G\rho$. With $\ln\rho = \ln\rho_0 - 2\ln\cosh(z/z_0)$, $d^2\ln\rho/dz^2 = -(2/z_0^2)\,\mathrm{sech}^2(z/z_0)$, so the equation holds when $2c_s^2/z_0^2 = 4\pi G\rho_0$, that is $z_0 = c_s/(2\pi G\rho_0)^{1/2} = 2H$. Then $\Sigma = \int\rho_0\,\mathrm{sech}^2(z/2H)\,dz = 2H\rho_0[\tanh]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = 4\rho_0H$. The layer is an exact equilibrium, so its instability involves no swindle.
(b) Integrating (2.1) and (2.2) over $z$ for a thin layer gives $\partial_t\Sigma_1 + \Sigma\,\partial_xu_1 = 0$ and $\Sigma\,\partial_tu_1 = -c_s^2\partial_x\Sigma_1 - \Sigma\,\partial_x\Phi_1|_{z=0}$. With $e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$: $-i\omega\Sigma_1 + ik\Sigma u_1 = 0$ and $-i\omega\Sigma u_1 = -ikc_s^2\Sigma_1 + ik\Sigma(2\pi G\Sigma_1/|k|)$. Eliminating $u_1$ gives $\omega^2 = c_s^2k^2 - 2\pi G\Sigma|k|$. It vanishes at $k_{\rm crit} = 2\pi G\Sigma/c_s^2$, so $\lambda_{\rm crit} = c_s^2/(G\Sigma)$. The growth rate squared, $2\pi G\Sigma k - c_s^2k^2$, is largest at $k = \pi G\Sigma/c_s^2 = k_{\rm crit}/2$, a wavelength $2\lambda_{\rm crit}$, with $s_{\max} = \pi G\Sigma/c_s$.
(c) With $\rho_0 = 4.6495\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $c_s = 0.1889\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$: $H = 4521.6$ AU, $\Sigma = 1.2580\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-2}} = 60.2\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{pc^{-2}}$, $\lambda_{\rm crit} = 0.13773$ pc $= 28410$ AU, fastest wavelength $0.27547$ pc, and $1/s_{\max} = 2.2693\times10^{5}$ yr, which is $\sqrt2$ times the spherical $t_{\rm grow}$ at the same density. The thin-layer assumption needs $kH\ll1$. But $G\Sigma/c_s^2 = 1/(2\pi H)$, so $k_{\rm crit}H = 1$ exactly and the fastest mode has $kH = 1/2$: the assumption fails at precisely the wavelengths it predicts. The finite thickness of the layer changes these numbers. The correction is not computed here.
D3. Using Proposition 7, show that at fixed mass and temperature a Bonnor–Ebert sphere cannot be smaller than $R_{\min} = GM/(2.5176\,c_s^2)$, and that the smallest sphere has the steepest edge slope of any isothermal sphere. Evaluate $R_{\min}$ for $2.10\,M_\odot$ at $16$ K and compare with the published radius of Barnard 68.
Solution
Proposition 7 gives $R = GM/[c_s^2\,\xi_{\max}\psi'(\xi_{\max})]$ at fixed $M$ and $c_s$. The radius is smallest where $\xi\psi'$ is largest, and §5 found the maximum of $\xi\psi'$ to be $2.5176$ at $\xi = 8.993$. So $R_{\min} = GM/(2.5176\,c_s^2)$. By Proposition 6, $\xi\psi' = -d\ln\rho/d\ln r$, so the same function is both the inverse radius and the local slope: the smallest sphere of a given mass is the one whose edge is steepest. For $M = 2.10\,M_\odot$ and $c_s = 0.2389\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, $R_{\min} = 12961$ AU. At $\xi_{\max} = 6.9$ the same formula gives $13231$ AU, and at $\xi_{\rm crit}$ it gives $13403$ AU. The published radius, $12500$ AU, is $3.6$ per cent below $R_{\min}$: no isothermal sphere of $2.10\,M_\odot$ at $16$ K is that small. This is the same internal inconsistency of the published row as in §7.2, where the mass from the radius came out $0.9447$ of the published mass, and it lies within the $13.4$ per cent spread found there.
