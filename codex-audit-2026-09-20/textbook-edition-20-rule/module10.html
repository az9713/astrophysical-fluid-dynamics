1. The nonlinear term, and the number that decides

Module 2 derived the Navier–Stokes equation and showed that viscosity is small in many astrophysical flows. A small viscous term does not mean that viscosity is irrelevant. In a turbulent flow, nonlinear advection transfers kinetic energy to small scales, where even a small viscosity can dissipate it.

For smooth incompressible Euler flow in a closed or periodic domain, kinetic energy is conserved. At small nonzero viscosity, the flow can transfer kinetic energy to increasingly small scales, where viscosity converts it into heat. The kinetic-energy dissipation rate may remain finite as viscosity tends to zero. This limiting behaviour is called the dissipation anomaly. It does not violate conservation of total energy.

Definition 1 (Reynolds number, and the turbulent regime). For a flow of characteristic speed $U$ over a characteristic scale $L$ in a fluid of kinematic viscosity $\nu = \mu/\rho$, $$ \mathrm{Re} \;\equiv\; \frac{UL}{\nu}. \tag{1.1}$$ It is the ratio of the inertial term $(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u} \sim U^2/L$ of Module 2's equation (7.5) to the viscous term $\nu\nabla^2\mathbf{u} \sim \nu U/L^2$. The flow is called turbulent when $\mathrm{Re}$ is large enough for the nonlinearity to dominate at every scale between $L$ and the scale at which viscosity finally acts.

No universal Reynolds number marks the onset of turbulence. Pipe flow becomes turbulent near $\mathrm{Re}\approx2300$, whereas a flat-plate boundary layer may remain laminar to about $5\times10^5$. Geometry and disturbances affect the transition. Astrophysical Reynolds numbers often exceed either value by many orders of magnitude, so the precise threshold is not important here.

Proposition 1 (relation among Reynolds, Mach, and Knudsen numbers). With $\mu\simeq\tfrac13\rho\bar v\lambda$, $$ \mathrm{Re} \;=\; \frac{\rho UL}{\tfrac13\rho\,\bar v\,\lambda} \;=\; 3\,\frac{U}{\bar v}\,\frac{L}{\lambda} \;=\; \frac{3\,\mathrm{Ma_{th}}}{\mathrm{Kn}}, \qquad \mathrm{Ma_{th}}\equiv\frac{U}{\bar v},\quad \mathrm{Kn}\equiv\frac{\lambda}{L}. \tag{1.2}$$ The relation holds for any thermal-speed convention if the same $\bar v$ appears in both viscosity and Mach number. Changing that convention changes $\mathrm{Ma_{th}}$ but leaves the resulting Reynolds number unchanged.
Substitute $\mu = \tfrac13\rho\bar v\lambda$ into (1.1) written as $\mathrm{Re}=\rho UL/\mu$. The density cancels, leaving $3(U/\bar v)(L/\lambda)$, which is the right-hand side. The clause about the speed is the part worth care. Nothing above fixes what $\bar v$ means. It enters the viscosity and the Mach number to the same power, so replacing it everywhere by any other thermal speed leaves (1.2) true and leaves $\mathrm{Re}$ where it was, while moving $\mathrm{Ma_{th}}$. It is therefore not enough to say which identity is being used; a module must say which speed. §6.4 prints every speed this module uses and how many components each one averages over. ∎

Throughout this module, $\bar v=\sqrt{8k_{\rm B}T/(\pi m)}$ denotes the mean thermal speed used in the kinetic-theory viscosity $\nu=\lambda\bar v/3$. Module 2 instead used $v_{\rm th}=\sqrt{2k_{\rm B}T/m}$. The two conventions differ by a factor $\sqrt{4/\pi}=1.1284$, which is included explicitly in the cross-module comparisons of §6.

Equation (1.2) is the reason this module can be written at all. It converts a Reynolds number, which needs a viscosity, into a Knudsen number, which needs only a mean free path — and Module 1 tabulated mean free paths for every gas in this book. Direct calculation of all dynamically active scales is therefore impractical. Statistical scaling arguments can nevertheless predict spectra and scale relations without resolving every eddy.

2. Kolmogorov 1941, by dimensional analysis alone

The argument in this section is due to A. N. Kolmogorov, in a three-page note of 1941, and it is the most economical piece of physics in this book. It solves no equation. It makes one assumption about what a turbulent flow forgets, and then counts dimensions.

Definition 2 (the dissipation rate and the inertial range). Let $\varepsilon$ be the energy dissipated per unit mass per unit time, with dimensions $\mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-3}}$. In a statistically steady flow stirred at the scale $L$, $\varepsilon$ is also the rate at which energy is supplied, since none accumulates. The inertial range is the band of scales $$ \eta \;\ll\; \ell \;\ll\; L \tag{2.1}$$ on which neither the stirring scale $L$ nor the viscosity $\nu$ appears in the statistics, so that $\varepsilon$ and the scale $\ell$ — equivalently the wavenumber $k = 1/\ell$ — are the only quantities available. Here $\eta$ is the scale at which viscosity acts, constructed in §3.
That is an assumption, and it is the only one. Equation (2.1) asserts that a scale in the middle of the range has forgotten both how the energy was put in and how it will be taken out, and remembers only how fast it is passing through. Nothing proves it. Everything in §5 and §7 is a test of it, and §11 names the respect in which it is known to be false.
Proposition 2 (the K41 spectrum). Let $E(k)$ be the energy spectrum per unit mass, defined so that $\int_0^\infty E(k)\,\mathrm{d}k$ is the kinetic energy per unit mass and $E$ therefore has dimensions $\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-2}}$. If in the inertial range $E$ depends only on $\varepsilon$ and $k$, then $$ E(k) \;=\; C\,\varepsilon^{2/3}k^{-5/3} \tag{2.2}$$ with $C$ a dimensionless constant, and the exponents are unique. The corresponding velocity increment across a separation $\ell$ is $$ \delta v(\ell) \;=\; (\varepsilon\ell)^{1/3}. \tag{2.3}$$
Write the most general such dependence, $E = C\,\varepsilon^{a}k^{b}$, and match dimensions. With $[\varepsilon] = \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-3}}$ and $[k] = \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, $$ \mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-2}} \;=\; (\mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-3}})^{a}\,(\mathrm{cm^{-1}})^{b}. $$ The two units give two equations in two unknowns: $$ \text{seconds:}\quad -2 = -3a \;\Rightarrow\; a = \tfrac23; \qquad \text{centimetres:}\quad 3 = 2a - b \;\Rightarrow\; b = 2a-3 = -\tfrac53. \tag{2.4}$$ The system is $2\times2$ with a non-singular matrix, so the solution is unique: no other pair of exponents is dimensionally possible. That is the force of the result — nothing was fitted, and the exponent $-5/3$ is not a best-fit slope but the only slope two units permit. For (2.3), the same two quantities give exactly one speed at the scale $\ell$: $[\varepsilon\ell] = \mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-3}}$, whose cube root is a speed. Consistency with (2.2) is a check rather than a second assumption — the energy in a band of wavenumbers of width $\sim k$ around $k$ is $kE(k) \sim C(\varepsilon/k)^{2/3}$, and its square root is $(\varepsilon\ell)^{1/3}$ up to the constant. ∎

The exponent depends on defining $E(k)$ as energy per unit wavenumber. Its units are $\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-2}}$, not those of energy itself. Dimensional analysis then gives $k^{-5/3}$. Changing the spectral definition changes the exponent and the physical quantity being described.

Dimensional analysis leaves the Kolmogorov constant $C$ undetermined. If local isotropic cascade physics is universal, different flows should yield the same value. Section 5 tests this claim with laboratory measurements before the constant is used astrophysically.

3. The dissipation scale, and what a simulation would cost

The same scaling that determines the inertial-range spectrum also fixes the dissipation scale. Resolving that scale throughout a three-dimensional domain sets the minimum cost of direct numerical simulation. For astrophysical Reynolds numbers, that cost is prohibitive.

Proposition 3 (Kolmogorov microscales). The viscosity $\nu$ and energy flux $\varepsilon$ define one length, speed, and time: $$ \eta \;=\; \left(\frac{\nu^3}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/4},\qquad u_\eta \;=\; (\nu\varepsilon)^{1/4},\qquad \tau_\eta \;=\; \left(\frac{\nu}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/2}. \tag{3.1}$$ They satisfy $$ \frac{u_\eta\,\eta}{\nu} \;=\; 1 \tag{3.2}$$ Thus the Reynolds number of the smallest eddy is unity, marking the scale where viscosity becomes important.
With $[\nu] = \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $[\varepsilon] = \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-3}}$, seek $\nu^{a}\varepsilon^{b}$ with the dimensions of a length: $2a+2b = 1$ and $-a-3b = 0$, so $b = -a/3$ and $a = 3/4$, giving $\eta = (\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}$. The same two equations with the dimensions of a speed give $a = b = 1/4$, and with those of a time, $a = -b = 1/2$. Each system is $2\times2$ and non-singular, so each answer is the only one. For (3.2), substitute: $u_\eta\eta/\nu = (\nu\varepsilon)^{1/4}(\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}/\nu = (\nu^4)^{1/4}/\nu = 1$. The prep script evaluates this numerically and prints $1.000000$, which is the intended reading: it is an algebraic identity, and a numerical value differing from one would mean a typing error and nothing else. ∎

Put numbers in. For air near the ground, stirred at the rate $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{W\,kg^{-1}} = 10\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$ typical of the atmospheric boundary layer, with $\nu = 0.15\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$:

$$ \eta = 1.355\ \mathrm{mm},\qquad u_\eta = 1.11\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}},\qquad \tau_\eta = 122.47\ \mathrm{ms}. \tag{3.3}$$
Precision, stated once here and inherited everywhere below. The $0.15\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ is a two-significant-figure value for which no primary source was obtained: the standard reference, Kadoya, Matsunaga & Nagashima (1985), is listed in Sources and was not served by any archive tried. Every quantity in (3.3) therefore carries two significant figures whatever width it is printed at, and so does every number derived from it, including Problem C1. The extra digits are carried only so that the identity (3.2) closes to $1.000000$. This statement is not repeated at each later use.
Proposition 4 (the number of degrees of freedom, and the cost of resolving them). With $U$ the outer-scale speed and $\varepsilon \sim U^3/L$, $$ \frac{L}{\eta} \;=\; \mathrm{Re}^{3/4}, \qquad N \;=\; \left(\frac{L}{\eta}\right)^{3} \;=\; \mathrm{Re}^{9/4}. \tag{3.4}$$ A time-explicit code is limited by the Courant condition to a step $\delta t \sim \eta/U$ — the largest speed on the smallest cell — so one outer turnover $L/U$ costs $L/\eta = \mathrm{Re}^{3/4}$ steps and $$ \mathrm{Re}^{9/4}\times\mathrm{Re}^{3/4} \;=\; \mathrm{Re}^{3} \tag{3.5}$$ point-updates in total.
Take $\varepsilon = U^3/L$, which is (2.3) at $\ell = L$ rearranged, and substitute into $\eta = (\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}$: $$ \frac{L}{\eta} \;=\; L\left(\frac{U^3}{L\nu^3}\right)^{1/4} \;=\; \left(\frac{UL}{\nu}\right)^{3/4} \;=\; \mathrm{Re}^{3/4}. $$ A cube of side $L$ resolved to $\eta$ needs $(L/\eta)^3$ cells, which is (3.4). For the step count, $(L/U)/(\eta/U) = L/\eta$, and multiplying by the cell count gives $\mathrm{Re}^{9/4+3/4} = \mathrm{Re}^{3}$. ∎

Using the Kolmogorov turnover time $\tau_\eta$ gives $\mathrm{Re}^{1/2}$ small-eddy turnovers and $\mathrm{Re}^{11/4}$ updates. A numerical solver must instead use the advection time $\eta/U$, which is shorter by $$ \frac{U}{u_\eta} \;=\; \mathrm{Re}^{1/4}. \tag{3.6}$$ The resulting operation count scales as $\mathrm{Re}^3$. 4. The one exact result, and its minus sign

Everything in §2 and §3 is dimensional analysis: correct up to a constant that dimensions cannot fix. The four-fifths law is an exact relation derived from the Navier–Stokes equations under specified assumptions; its coefficient is not obtained from dimensional analysis.

Proposition 5 (Kolmogorov's four-fifths law). For homogeneous, isotropic, statistically stationary turbulence at high Reynolds number, the third-order longitudinal structure function $$ S_3(\ell) \;\equiv\; \big\langle\,\delta u_\parallel(\ell)^3\,\big\rangle, \qquad \delta u_\parallel(\ell) \;\equiv\; \big[\,\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\ell}) - \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\,\big]\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}, $$ satisfies, for $\eta \ll \ell \ll L$, $$ S_3(\ell) \;=\; -\tfrac45\,\varepsilon\,\ell. \tag{4.1}$$ Stated, not derived here. The derivation is von Kármán and Howarth's, from the two-point correlation equation. It is a page of tensor algebra that would displace two of this module's four checks, and it is quoted in the same way Module 9 quotes the Eddington luminosity. Its four hypotheses — homogeneous, isotropic, stationary, $\mathrm{Re}\to\infty$ — are named in this sentence because each of them fails somewhere in this module.
Not given; see the statement. What can be checked here without the derivation is consistency with (2.3): $S_3 \propto \varepsilon\ell$ has the dimensions of a speed cubed, and $(\varepsilon\ell)^{1/3}$ is that speed, so the exact law and the dimensional estimate agree on the scaling and differ only by the coefficient $4/5$. The ratio of the two is $(4/5)^{1/3} = 0.9283$, which is the number Problem C3 asks a reader to recognise. ∎

The minus sign is the physics. A symmetric distribution of velocity increments has zero third moment, so (4.1) says the distribution is skewed: at any separation in the inertial range, large negative longitudinal increments are more common than large positive ones. Fluid elements approach each other along the line joining them more violently than they separate. That asymmetry is the direction of the cascade written as a number — energy flows from large scales to small, and the sign of $S_3$ is what says so. Reverse it and the energy would flow the other way, which is what happens in two-dimensional turbulence and is one of the reasons that subject is different.

The second-order structure function $S_2(\ell) = \langle\delta u_\parallel^2\rangle$ has no such exact law. It is $C_2(\varepsilon\ell)^{2/3}$, with $C_2$ related to the spectral constant of (2.2) by an isotropy factor. §5 quotes both.

5. The terrestrial check: is there one Kolmogorov constant?

K41 predicts the spectral exponent but leaves the Kolmogorov constant to experiment. Laboratory measurements test whether that constant is universal. This calibration precedes its use for solar-wind and molecular-cloud spectra.

The convention matters, because three different constants are called "the Kolmogorov constant" in the literature and they differ by factors of two and more. Sreenivasan (1995) defines his through the one-dimensional longitudinal spectrum $E_{11}(k_1)$, the thing a single hot wire in a single mean flow actually measures:

$$ E_{11}(k_1) \;=\; C_K\,\varepsilon^{2/3}k_1^{-5/3}. \tag{5.1}$$

The three-dimensional constant $C$ of (2.2) is related to it by an isotropy factor, and the second-order structure function by another:

$$ C \;=\; \tfrac{55}{18}\,C_K, \qquad C_2 \;=\; 4.02\,C_K. \tag{5.2}$$

Both factors are Sreenivasan's, from his Section I, and both assume local isotropy — the same assumption Definition 2 makes. They are quoted, not derived.

A second Reynolds number, and it is not Definition 1's. Experimenters report the Taylor-microscale Reynolds number $R_\lambda \equiv u'\lambda_T/\nu$, where $u'$ is the root-mean-square velocity fluctuation of one component and $\lambda_T$ is the Taylor microscale $u'/\langle(\partial u/\partial x)^2\rangle^{1/2}$. They report it because $\lambda_T$ can be measured from a single probe where $L$ often cannot. For homogeneous turbulence $R_\lambda \propto \mathrm{Re}^{1/2}$, so a range in $R_\lambda$ is half as many decades as the same range in $\mathrm{Re}$. This relation is standard and is quoted here, not derived. $R_\lambda$ is new to this book, is not numbered as a definition because nothing later depends on it, and appears only in this section.

Sreenivasan compiled more than a hundred measurements of $C_K$ — grid turbulence, jets, wakes, boundary layers, a tidal channel — and found:

Check 2: the Kolmogorov constant is approximately universal across the compiled flows. Sreenivasan reports $C_K=0.53\pm0.055$ for more than 100 flows, a fractional scatter of $10.4$ per cent. The data span $R_\lambda\simeq50$ to $10^4$, corresponding to about $4.6$ decades in Reynolds number. K41 does not predict the numerical value $0.53$; it predicts that one dimensionless value should describe many geometries at high Reynolds number. The observed scatter quantifies that universality.
The $\pm0.055$ is the scatter of the experiments, not the error on their mean; with $N \gt 100$ the error on the mean would be smaller by $\sqrt{N} \gt 10$, and quoting it would turn a statement about universality into a statement about arithmetic. The span is stated in both variables deliberately: "three decades", the phrase one might reach for, is neither of them.

In the three-dimensional form (5.2) the compiled value is $C = 1.619 \pm 0.168$, and the second-order structure constant is $C_2 = 2.131 \pm 0.221$. Sreenivasan then revises his own number: allowing that the local-isotropy estimate of $\varepsilon$ undercounts by about ten per cent moves $C_K$ to about $0.50$ and the three-dimensional constant to $1.53$. This module therefore quotes the range $1.53$–$1.62$ wherever a three-dimensional constant is needed, including in Problem K2, rather than a single digit that would hide a correction the author himself applied.

Grant, Stewart, and Moilliet (1962) measured tidal-channel spectra at $R_\lambda=3000$–$18000$. Their three-dimensional constant is $1.436\pm0.061$, only $1.09$ population standard deviations below the compiled mean. It is therefore typical of the laboratory sample.

What the laboratory data contribute is the constant and its universality — a different claim, and a weaker one, than a measured $-5/3$. Proposition 6 (when there is no viscosity to put in). If $\mathrm{Kn} \gtrsim 1/3$, the Chapman–Enskog expansion of Module 1 is failing, the Navier–Stokes viscosity does not exist as a property of the gas, and $UL/\nu$ is a formula evaluated outside its own domain. By (1.2), such a gas has $$ \mathrm{Re} \;\lt\; 1 \quad\text{whenever}\quad \mathrm{Kn} \;\gt\; 3\,\mathrm{Ma_{th}}, \tag{6.1}$$ so "the Reynolds number is small" and "the fluid description has failed" are one statement in such a gas, not two. Where a magnetic field is present, the transport step across the field is not $\lambda$ but the gyroradius $r_g$, so the perpendicular viscosity is bounded above and the perpendicular Reynolds number below: $$ \nu_\perp \;\le\; \frac{r_g\,\bar v}{3}, \qquad \mathrm{Re}_\perp \;\ge\; \frac{UL}{r_g\bar v/3}. \tag{6.2}$$
The first part is (1.2) rearranged: $\mathrm{Re} = 3\,\mathrm{Ma_{th}}/\mathrm{Kn} \lt 1$ exactly when $\mathrm{Kn} \gt 3\,\mathrm{Ma_{th}}$. Nothing further is needed, and that is the point — one identity turns a statement about a transport coefficient into a statement about a mean free path, which is why Fig. 2 can draw both on one plane. For (6.2), Module 1 §8 showed that across $\mathbf{B}$ a charged particle's excursion between scatterings is bounded by $r_g$ however long it goes without colliding, whereas along $\mathbf{B}$ it is free to travel $\lambda$. A diffusivity is a step squared over a time, so replacing the step $\lambda$ by $r_g$ in $\nu = \lambda\bar v/3$ gives the perpendicular value, and it is an upper bound because scattering can only be more effective than the gyro-orbit alone, never less. Dividing $UL$ by an upper bound on $\nu$ gives a lower bound on $\mathrm{Re}_\perp$, which is (6.2). It is printed as a bound in both directions throughout this module and never as a value. ∎

6.1 The intracluster medium, and the field that rescues it

The Coulomb mean free path in a cluster core is $22.5$ kpc — a third of the region Hitomi observed. The ion mean thermal speed is $\bar v_i = \sqrt{8k_{\rm B}T/\pi m_{\rm p}} = 1450\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so $\nu = \lambda\bar v_i/3 = 3.3557\times10^{30}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ and

$$ \mathrm{Re} \;=\; \frac{(1.64\times10^{7})(60\ \mathrm{kpc})}{3.3557\times10^{30}} \;=\; 0.90, \qquad \mathrm{Kn} \;=\; \frac{22.5}{60} \;=\; 0.375. \tag{6.3}$$
Read those two together, not separately. $\mathrm{Re} \lt 1$ because $\mathrm{Kn} \gt 1/3$, and by (1.2) they are one statement. At $\mathrm{Kn} = 0.375$ the expansion of Module 1 is failing, so $0.90$ is a formula evaluated outside its own domain of validity. It does not say the intracluster medium is a barely turbulent fluid. It says the collisional fluid description does not apply to the intracluster medium — and the observations of §8 show gas that is moving, which no laminar reading of $\mathrm{Re} = 0.90$ could accommodate.
The speed in $\lambda\bar v/3$ here is the ion mean thermal speed at $m = m_{\rm p}$, not the mean speed of an average particle at $\mu_{\rm ICM} = 0.61$. A viscosity is carried by whatever carries the momentum, and in a hydrogen plasma that is the ions; $\mu_{\rm ICM} = 0.61$ is a mass per particle with the electrons counted in, and it belongs in the sound speed and in the pressure ratio of §8, not here. Using it here would inflate $\nu$ by $\sqrt{1.00728/0.61} = 1.285$.

The way out is Module 1's, and (6.2) is what it buys. At $B = 1\ \mu$G the proton gyroradius is $r_g = 1.341\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm} = 8.97\times10^{-4}$ au, so

$$ \frac{\lambda}{r_g} \;=\; 5.177\times10^{12}, \qquad \nu_\perp \;\le\; \frac{r_g\bar v_i}{3} \;=\; 6.4824\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}, \qquad \mathrm{Re}_\perp \;\ge\; 4.684\times10^{12}. \tag{6.4}$$

That is the cascade §8 measures. It exists because the field, not the collisions, sets the transport step across $\mathbf{B}$ — the same one-dimensional rescue Module 1 §8 described, here turned into a number.

The opposite bound is worth printing beside it. Braginskii's classical perpendicular viscosity, $0.3125\,\nu_\parallel (r_g/\lambda)^2$ — note the square — is $4.0775\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$, which would give $\mathrm{Re}_\perp = 7.446\times10^{25}$.

The local viscosity bounds span $1.59\times10^{13}$. This factor is $3.07$ times $\lambda/r_g$ because Braginskii's parallel coefficient uses a different speed convention and carries the numerical factor $0.96$. Neither endpoint is a measured effective viscosity for the intracluster medium. Pressure-anisotropy microinstabilities can increase the scattering rate, so the calculation bounds $\mathrm{Re}_\perp$ without assigning it a unique value.

Zhuravleva et al. (2019) compare deep Chandra observations of the Coma cluster with viscous simulations. They find no Coulomb-viscous cutoff and infer that effective viscosity is suppressed by at least a factor 10–1000 for $\mathrm{Pr}\le1$. The classical value $\mathrm{Re}=0.90$ is therefore only a lower bound.

Two caveats, and both are theirs. The suppression factor depends on the Prandtl number, which they leave as a free parameter — which is why the bound is a range of two orders of magnitude and not a number. And: "it is not at present possible to prove that the observed fluctuations are associated with the turbulence in clusters as we do not have direct velocity measurements." $\mathrm{Pr}$ is quoted here inside their sentence and is computed nowhere in this module.

What the two intracluster rows do and do not license

In the intracluster-outskirts entry of Module 1, $n=10^{-4}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $T=10^8$ K and $L=2$ Mpc. The resulting $\lambda=222$ kpc and $\mathrm{Kn}=0.11$ are ten times the core mean free path, partly offset by a system size twice as large.

One paper ruling the collisional description out in a second cluster: Zhuravleva's, whose offset Coma region has $\lambda \approx 30$ kpc. However, this book has no scale $L$ for her fluctuation spectrum, so no Knudsen number may be computed from it, only her own conclusion quoted. That is the whole of the evidence, and the prose may not widen it into a claim about every scale at which the intracluster medium has been measured.

6.2 Braginskii's coefficient, and a convention that is not a coefficient

The estimate $\nu=\lambda\bar v/3$ approximates angular transport by a factor of one third. Braginskii (1965) derives the plasma coefficients kinetically. Comparing the two requires separating the numerical transport coefficient from the different definitions of collision time and thermal speed.

Braginskii defines the ion collision time by $$ \tau_i \;=\; \frac{3\sqrt{m_i}\,T_i^{3/2}}{4\sqrt{\pi}\,\ln\Lambda\,e^4Z^4 n_i} \;=\; 4.4126\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{s}, \tag{6.5}$$ With the Spitzer mean free path, this equals $\lambda/v_{\rm rms}$ algebraically. The numerical equality holds to four figures after retaining the unrounded $22.5$ kpc mean free path. This identity fixes the convention; it is not an independent validation of the mean-free-path coefficient.

Second: the coefficient check. His ion viscosity is $\eta_0^{\rm B} = 0.96\,n_iT_i\tau_i$, so $\nu_{\rm B} = 0.96\,k_{\rm B}T\tau_i/m_i = 3.4966\times10^{30}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$, and since $k_{\rm B}T/m = v_{\rm rms}^2/3$,

$$ \frac{\nu_{\rm B}}{\lambda v_{\rm rms}/3} \;=\; 0.9600 \qquad\text{exactly.} \tag{6.6}$$
Check on the coefficient, and on nothing else. Braginskii's proper kinetic calculation gives $0.96$ where the elementary estimate of Module 1 gives $1$, at the same speed. Four per cent. That is what (6.6) says, and it is the only coefficient statement in this module.

Third: a number that looks like a second coefficient and is not one. Against this module's own $\lambda\bar v/3$, which uses the mean speed, the same viscosity gives

$$ \frac{\nu_{\rm B}}{\lambda\bar v/3} \;=\; 0.96\sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{8}} \;=\; 1.0420, \tag{6.7}$$

whatever the temperature, the density or the mean free path. It is printed only in that algebraic form, because writing it as a decimal invites a reader to treat $1.042$ as a rival to $0.96$. The extra $8.5$ per cent is the mean speed against the root-mean-square speed:

$$ \frac{\bar v}{v_{\rm rms}} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{8}{3\pi}} \;=\; 0.9213, \qquad \frac{0.96}{0.9213} \;=\; 1.0420. \tag{6.8}$$

Equation (6.8) reconciles two viscosity normalizations through velocity averaging. Both speeds are three-dimensional; one is $\langle v\rangle$ and the other $\sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle}$. The coefficients $0.96$ and $1.042$ therefore do not represent competing physical viscosities.

Notation. Braginskii's dynamic viscosities are written $\eta_0^{\rm B}$ and $\eta_1^{\rm B}$ in this module, always with both marks, and appear only in this subsection; bare $\eta$ is the Kolmogorov dissipation scale of Proposition 3 throughout. He gives $\eta_1^{\rm B} = (3/10)n_iT_i/(\omega_i^2\tau_i)$ with $\eta_2^{\rm B} = 4\eta_1^{\rm B}$, and it is $\eta_1^{\rm B}/\eta_0^{\rm B} = 0.3125\,(r_g/\lambda)^2$ that produced the classical perpendicular viscosity of §6.1.

7. The anchor: the solar wind at 1 au

The solar-wind spectrum provides two tests from one instrument and one fitting interval. The velocity and magnetic fields yield different exponents, so the comparison can distinguish a successful scaling from an unsuccessful one under the same observational conditions.

Podesta, Roberts and Goldstein (2007) fitted magnetic, velocity, kinetic-energy and total-energy spectra from Wind measurements at 1 au. Their four intervals sample solar minimum, maximum, and the ascending and descending phases of the cycle. Each interval spans 54–81 days.

Taylor's hypothesis is doing work here, and it is an assumption. A spacecraft measures a time series at a fixed point, so what is fitted is a frequency spectrum $P(f)$. Converting it to a wavenumber spectrum assumes the structures are swept past the spacecraft faster than they evolve or propagate — that the wind speed greatly exceeds the Alfvén and sound speeds. On Module 1's census values the Alfvén speed is $v_A = B/\sqrt{4\pi\rho} = 48.8\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ with $\rho = n m_{\rm p}$, so $U/v_A = 8.2$: the wind sweeps a structure past the spacecraft about eight times faster than the structure can propagate. That is a ratio of order ten and not of order a hundred, so the assumption is good rather than exact. It is not addressed anywhere in this book. Frequencies in this section are written $f$, in hertz, and never $\nu$, which is the kinematic viscosity throughout this module.

The fit window is $10^{-3} \le f \le 10^{-2}$ Hz. Their Sect. 3 bounds the window's own effect: moving the endpoints over $7\times10^{-4}$ to $1.5\times10^{-3}$ Hz and $7\times10^{-3}$ to $1.5\times10^{-2}$ Hz changes an exponent "typically by 1%", always less than the tabulated error. 8. The cluster check: what "static" costs, measured

Module 3 built its hydrostatic equilibria on the idealisation it lists in its own §11 as “Static: $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}$”, applied it to an intracluster medium, and addressed that idealisation through Module 4. This section prices it: in one cluster core, with one measurement, how much of the mass does a hydrostatic analysis miss because the gas is moving?

One thing Module 3 did not do, stated because it would be easy to assume otherwise. Module 3 did not name turbulence as the limitation behind that row. The word does not occur in that module at all. What it did was list “Static: $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}$” among its idealisations, put an intracluster medium at $r = 1$ Mpc in its §3 scale-height table, and use that row in its Problem C3. This section supplies a price for the idealisation, measured, and claims nothing about what Module 3 anticipated.

In 2016 the Hitomi satellite's calorimeter resolved the iron K line in the core of the Perseus cluster, for a few weeks before the spacecraft was lost. In the region $30$–$60$ kpc from the nucleus it measured a line-of-sight velocity dispersion

$$ \sigma_v \;=\; 164 \pm 10\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}} \quad (90\%\ \text{confidence}), \tag{8.1}$$

which as a one-standard-deviation error is $\pm 6.1\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, together with $k_{\rm B}T = 4.1 \pm 0.1$ keV for the same region. An independent determination from the hydrogen-like Lyman-$\alpha$ line gives $160 \pm 16\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, consistent with (8.1).

At $\mu_{\rm ICM} = 0.61$ and $\gamma = 5/3$ the sound speed of that gas is $1040\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so

$$ \mathcal{M}_{\rm 1D} \;=\; \frac{\sigma_v}{c_s} \;=\; 0.158, \qquad \mathcal{M}_{\rm 3D} \;=\; \frac{\sqrt3\,\sigma_v}{c_s} \;=\; 0.273. \tag{8.2}$$

Both are printed because both are used, and the factor $\sqrt3$ between them is the component count of §6.4: a Doppler width is one component and a Mach number of a flow is three. The gas is subsonic on either reading, which is what makes the incompressible theory of §2 the right one to apply here and the wrong one to apply in §10.

8.1 The turbulent pressure, and what the density does not do

For isotropic turbulence the turbulent pressure is $\tfrac13\rho\langle v^2\rangle = \tfrac13\rho(3\sigma_v^2) = \rho\sigma_v^2$, and the thermal pressure of an ideal gas is $\rho k_{\rm B}T/(\mu m_{\rm u})$. The ratio is

$$ \alpha \;\equiv\; \frac{P_{\rm turb}}{P_{\rm th}} \;=\; \frac{\mu m_{\rm u}\sigma_v^2}{k_{\rm B}T} \;=\; 4.15\ \text{per cent}. \tag{8.3}$$
The density cancels. The mean mass per particle does not. That is why a line width and a temperature suffice, with no density measurement anywhere. It is why $\mu = 0.61$ must be named every time (8.3) is used, including in every problem statement that uses it, because the ratio is linear in it.
What (8.3) is, and three things it is not — kept apart deliberately.
  1. It is a validation of the arithmetic, not a check against nature. The paper's own headline is "turbulent pressure support in the gas is 4% or less of the thermodynamic pressure". The $4.15$ per cent above reproduces that from the paper's own dispersion and the paper's own temperature. Nothing independent is being tested — the same treatment Module 5 gave Kandori's contrast column, where the label reads in full “A validation, not a check.”
  2. The "or less" is inherited, not established here. Both numbers come from the same $164\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, which the paper says its pixel self-calibration and its angular resolution each push up. So $4.15$ per cent is an upper-ish value for exactly the reason $4$ per cent is, and this module does not treat "or less" as a bound it has independently obtained.
  3. No ratio may be printed to three decimals. "4%" carries one significant figure and spans $3.5$ to $4.5$ per cent, against which $4.15$ gives a ratio anywhere from $0.92$ to $1.18$. A ratio quoted to three decimals would claim that a discrepancy had been measured when none has been.

8.2 The mass a hydrostatic analysis misses

Proposition 7 (the hydrostatic mass bias). Suppose the gas is supported against gravity by $P_{\rm tot} = P_{\rm th} + P_{\rm turb}$, and suppose $\alpha = P_{\rm turb}/P_{\rm th}$ is constant with radius. Then an observer who infers a mass from the thermal pressure gradient alone obtains $M_{\rm th}$, whereas the true mass is $M = (1+\alpha)M_{\rm th}$, and the fraction of the mass missed is $$ \frac{M - M_{\rm th}}{M} \;=\; \frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}. \tag{8.4}$$ The constant-$\alpha$ assumption is stated before the number, not after it.
Hydrostatic balance for the total pressure is $\mathrm{d}P_{\rm tot}/\mathrm{d}r = -GM\rho/r^2$, which is Module 3's equation with the turbulent term added. If $\alpha$ does not depend on $r$ then $P_{\rm tot} = (1+\alpha)P_{\rm th}$ and $\mathrm{d}P_{\rm tot}/\mathrm{d}r = (1+\alpha)\,\mathrm{d}P_{\rm th}/\mathrm{d}r$, so $$ -\frac{GM\rho}{r^2} \;=\; (1+\alpha)\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\rm th}}{\mathrm{d}r} \;=\; -(1+\alpha)\frac{GM_{\rm th}\rho}{r^2}, $$ giving $M = (1+\alpha)M_{\rm th}$ and hence (8.4). Drop the constant-$\alpha$ assumption and the result changes in kind, not in size: $\mathrm{d}P_{\rm tot}/\mathrm{d}r = (1+\alpha)\mathrm{d}P_{\rm th}/\mathrm{d}r + P_{\rm th}\,\mathrm{d}\alpha/\mathrm{d}r$, so the bias acquires a term in the difference between the two pressures' logarithmic scale lengths, and a single dispersion measured in a single annulus cannot supply it. ∎
Conditional estimate of hydrostatic mass bias. If the turbulent-to-thermal pressure ratio is constant with radius, the adopted ratio of $4.15$ per cent implies a mass underestimate of $3.98$ per cent. The measurement in one annulus does not establish that radial constancy. In general, $M=(1+\alpha)M_{\rm th}-[r^2P_{\rm th}/(G\rho)]\,d\alpha/dr$, so the bias also depends on the pressure-ratio gradient. The quoted percentage is therefore a conditional calculation, not an independently measured mass bias.
Scope, stated: one pointing, one annulus, one cluster. Hitomi observed $30$–$60$ kpc in one cluster core, and the paper itself restricts the claim to "the central regions". Cluster masses used for cosmology are measured near $r_{500}$, far outside this radius, where no comparable measurement exists. Nothing in this section licenses a four-per-cent correction to a cluster mass function.

Three further numbers from the same observation, each of which cuts against reading (8.3) too confidently. The central region — which contains the active galactic nucleus — gives $187 \pm 13\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ at $3.8$ keV, so $\alpha = 5.82$ per cent there: higher, and not cleanly attributable to a cascade. A velocity gradient of $150 \pm 70\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ runs across the $60$ kpc image, which is bulk motion rather than turbulence and is the shear the "at most doubling" refers to. And the systematic budget on the $164$ is energy resolution $\pm6$, temperature $\pm2$ and calibration scatter $-3\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ — all smaller than the $\pm10$ statistical limit, and all quoted by the collaboration.

9. Supersonic turbulence, and the density it makes

K41 and the exact $4/5$ law assume incompressibility. This is reasonable for the intracluster example, where $\mathcal M_{3D}=0.273$. Molecular-cloud turbulence has Mach number near ten, so density fluctuations and shocks change the cascade phenomenology.

Definition 3 (sonic Mach number, and the log density). $\mathcal{M} \equiv U/c_s$, with $c_s$ the adiabatic sound speed of Module 4 and $U$ the root-mean-square turbulent speed — not a bulk speed and not a one-component dispersion. And $$ s \;\equiv\; \ln\!\left(\frac{\rho}{\langle\rho\rangle}\right), \tag{9.1}$$ the logarithm of the density in units of its mean, which is the variable in which the statistics below are simple.

For the cloud row of §6 — $10$ K, $\mu = 2.33$, $\gamma = 5/3$ — the sound speed is $c_s = 0.2439\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, against Larson's three-dimensional $\sigma_L = 2.639\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ at $10$ pc. So $\mathcal{M} = 10.82$: ten times the sound speed, where the intracluster medium was a quarter of it. The flow is a field of shocks, and the first thing to ask is what a field of shocks does to a spectrum.

Proposition 8 (a field of shocks gives $k^{-2}$, and $h = 1/2$). A velocity field consisting of isolated discontinuities has $$ E(k) \;\propto\; k^{-2}, \qquad \delta v(\ell) \;\propto\; \ell^{1/2}. \tag{9.2}$$
Take a single step of height $\Delta u$ at $x_0$: $u(x) = \Delta u\,H(x-x_0)$. Its Fourier transform is $$ \hat u(k) \;=\; \Delta u\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kx_0}}{\mathrm{i}k}, \qquad |\hat u(k)|^2 \;=\; \frac{(\Delta u)^2}{k^2}, $$ so the energy per unit wavenumber falls as $k^{-2}$. That $|\hat u(k)|^2$ is a one-dimensional spectral density, and the $E(k)$ of (2.2) is the three-dimensional one; for a power law the two carry the same exponent and differ only by a constant factor — the isotropy factor $55/18$ of (5.2) is that constant for the Kolmogorov case — so the exponent $-2$ transfers and the prefactor does not. A superposition of randomly placed steps adds these spectra incoherently and leaves the exponent unchanged; only the prefactor counts the shocks. For the increment, consider two points a distance $\ell$ apart in a field with $n_s$ shocks per unit length. The probability that a shock lies between them is $n_s\ell$ for $n_s\ell \ll 1$, and when one does, the increment is $\Delta u$; otherwise it is the small smooth variation, which may be neglected. Then $$ \big\langle\delta v(\ell)^2\big\rangle \;\simeq\; n_s\ell\,(\Delta u)^2 \;\propto\; \ell, $$ so the root-mean-square increment scales as $\ell^{1/2}$ and $h = 1/2$, against Kolmogorov's $h = 1/3$ from (2.3). The two statements in (9.2) are consistent with each other by Proposition 10 below, which is the general bridge between an increment exponent and a spectral one. ∎

Two candidate exponents are relevant: $5/3$ for a Kolmogorov-like cascade and $2$ for a shock-dominated field. They differ by a factor $2.15$ in power per decade of wavenumber. Section 10 tests which scaling better describes a molecular cloud.

9.1 What supersonic turbulence does to the density

Incompressible turbulence has no density statistics to speak of. Supersonic turbulence also develops large density fluctuations, whose distribution is important for star formation.

Definition 4 (the forcing parameter). $b$ is the ratio of the fractional density dispersion to the Mach number, $\sigma_\rho/\langle\rho\rangle = b\mathcal{M}$. It runs from about $1/3$ for purely solenoidal (divergence-free) forcing in three dimensions to about $1$ for purely compressive (curl-free) forcing. Both endpoints are stated, from Federrath et al. (2010), their Sect. 5 and Fig. 8. This module quotes them and computes nothing about the forcing itself. The value $1/3$ is a three-dimensional statement: solenoidal forcing drives one of the three components compressively by a cascade, and the fraction is $1/3$ for that reason.
Proposition 9 (the log-normal density distribution). In isothermal supersonic turbulence $s$ of (9.1) is normally distributed with variance $$ \sigma_s^2 \;=\; \ln\!\left(1 + b^2\mathcal{M}^2\right) \tag{9.3}$$ — stated, Federrath et al. (2010) equations (18) and (19) — and with mean $$ s_0 \;=\; -\tfrac12\sigma_s^2, \tag{9.4}$$ which is forced, not fitted. The fraction of the volume with $\rho/\langle\rho\rangle$ above a threshold $x$ is then $$ f(\gt x) \;=\; \tfrac12\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left[\frac{\ln x - s_0}{\sigma_s\sqrt2}\right]. \tag{9.5}$$
Equation (9.3) is quoted. It is a fit to simulations, not a theorem, and the module marks it as such. Equations (9.4) and (9.5) follow from it. For (9.4): if $s$ is normal with mean $s_0$ and variance $\sigma_s^2$, then $\langle\mathrm{e}^{s}\rangle = \exp(s_0 + \tfrac12\sigma_s^2)$, the standard Gaussian moment-generating result. But $\mathrm{e}^{s} = \rho/\langle\rho\rangle$ by (9.1), whose volume average is $1$ by construction. Setting $\exp(s_0+\tfrac12\sigma_s^2) = 1$ gives $s_0 = -\tfrac12\sigma_s^2$. The mean of $s$ is therefore not an independent parameter: $e^{s_0}$ is the median density ratio and is below the mean. The mode of the density PDF is $e^{s_0-\sigma_s^2}$. For (9.5): $f(\gt x) = \int_{\ln x}^{\infty} (2\pi\sigma_s^2)^{-1/2}\exp[-(s-s_0)^2/2\sigma_s^2]\,\mathrm{d}s$. Substituting $t = (s-s_0)/(\sigma_s\sqrt2)$ turns this into $\pi^{-1/2}\int_{t_0}^\infty \mathrm{e}^{-t^2}\mathrm{d}t = \tfrac12\mathrm{erfc}(t_0)$ with $t_0 = (\ln x - s_0)/(\sigma_s\sqrt2)$. ∎

Because $s=\ln x$ is Gaussian with mean $s_0=-\sigma_s^2/2$, the median density ratio is $x_{\rm med}=e^{s_0}$. The density probability distribution is $p_x(x)=p_s(\ln x)/x$, whose maximum occurs at $x_{\rm mode}=e^{s_0-\sigma_s^2}$. 10. The interstellar check: the size–linewidth exponent

The final comparison asks whether molecular-cloud linewidths follow the Kolmogorov exponent $h=1/3$. They do not. Connecting observed linewidths to a spectral index requires the statistical relation derived in the following proposition.

Proposition 10 (from an increment exponent to a spectral exponent). Suppose $E(k)$ is a power law $Ak^{-p}$ with $p \gt 1$ over a range of wavenumbers, and let $\delta v(\ell)$ be the root-mean-square increment across the corresponding separations. Then $\delta v(\ell) \propto \ell^{h}$ with $h = (p-1)/2$, equivalently $$ E(k) \;\propto\; k^{-(1+2h)}. \tag{10.1}$$ In particular $h = 1/3$ gives $5/3$ and $h = 1/2$ gives $2$. The power-law hypothesis is the one that cannot be dropped: a measured increment exponent fixes a spectral exponent only if there is a spectral power law to fix, and §10 measures the increment exponent, not the spectrum.
The velocity increment across a separation $\ell$ samples all wavenumbers above $k = 1/\ell$, so $$ \delta v(\ell)^2 \;\simeq\; \int_{1/\ell}^{\infty} E(k)\,\mathrm{d}k. $$ Put $E(k) = Ak^{-p}$ with $p \gt 1$, which is required for the integral to converge at the upper limit. Then $$ \int_{1/\ell}^{\infty}Ak^{-p}\,\mathrm{d}k \;=\; \frac{A}{p-1}\,\ell^{\,p-1}, $$ so $\delta v \propto \ell^{(p-1)/2}$. Matching to $\ell^{h}$ gives $h = (p-1)/2$, that is $p = 1+2h$, which is (10.1). Substituting $h = 1/3$ gives $p = 5/3$, recovering Proposition 2, and $h = 1/2$ gives $p = 2$, recovering Proposition 8. ∎
This module measures an exponent, and infers a spectrum. What §10 compares against the sky is $h$, a size–linewidth exponent. The corresponding $E(k) \propto k^{-2}$ follows through (10.1) and through the assumption stated below about two-point versus ensemble statistics. No interstellar power spectrum is measured anywhere in this module, and the only measured spectral exponents in it are Podesta's eight, in §7.

10.1 Two linewidth–size fits published six years apart

Larson (1981) collected linewidths and sizes from the literature and drew a line through them:

$$ \sigma_L \;=\; 1.10\,L^{0.38}\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}, \qquad 0.1 \lesssim L \lesssim 100\ \mathrm{pc}, \tag{10.2}$$

with $\sigma_L$ three-dimensional and no error bar of any kind: the paper calls it an eye-fitted line, and its only stated scatter is $\mathrm{rms}(\log\sigma) = 0.14$, a factor of $1.38$ in $\sigma$. The exponent $0.38$ lies $+0.0467$ from Kolmogorov's $1/3$ and $-0.1200$ from Burgers' $1/2$. Larson drew the obvious conclusion himself, on his p. 816: "it is perhaps remarkable that the observed relation $\sigma \propto L^{0.38}$ is so close to the Kolmogoroff law $\sigma \propto L^{0.33}$."

Solomon and colleagues used one survey, one distance scale and one cloud-identification method for their sample of 273 Galactic molecular clouds:

$$ \sigma_v \;=\; (1.0 \pm 0.1)\,S^{\,0.5\,\pm\,0.05}\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}, \tag{10.3}$$

a least-squares fit, with $\sigma_v$ one-dimensional, $S$ the cloud size in parsecs, and a dispersion in $\log\sigma_v$ of $\pm0.11$ dex.

What the $\pm0.05$ is, established before it is used for anything. Their p. 737: "The quoted error in the exponent is larger than the formal fit error and represents an estimate of the systematic error based on several fits to the data with varying definitions of cloud boundaries." It is a spread over where a cloud ends. It is not a standard deviation, and no $\sigma$ is quoted from it anywhere in this module. What is printed instead is the gap measured in whole envelopes.

10.2 The comparison, and what it does and does not decide

Interpretive assumption. A structure function measures velocity differences within one flow as a function of separation. The Solomon et al. exponent instead comes from one linewidth and one size for each of 273 clouds. Comparing it with $\delta v(\ell)\propto\ell^{1/3}$ assumes that the cloud ensemble samples one statistical cascade. Larson made this identification explicitly, but the following inference remains conditional on it.
Check 4: the molecular-cloud exponent disagrees with incompressible K41 scaling. The measured exponent is $0.50$, while K41 predicts $1/3$. Their difference, $0.1667$, is $3.33$ times the stated systematic envelope of $\pm0.05$. Burgers scaling predicts $1/2$ and matches the exponent, although the exponent alone does not select its physical mechanism.

Solomon et al. report that formal fit error is smaller than their systematic envelope, so the envelope provides the conservative uncertainty. They explicitly conclude that their data rule out the earlier Kolmogorov interpretation.

Larson’s exponent $0.38$ came from an eye fit to heterogeneous literature data and has no parameter uncertainty. Solomon et al. fit 273 clouds from one survey and report an uncertainty envelope, obtaining $0.50$. Larson motivated the question; the homogeneous Solomon sample provides the stronger test.

The $1/2$ exponent is not unique to Burgers turbulence. Virial equilibrium at constant surface density also gives $\sigma_v\propto S^{1/2}$ because $\sigma^2\sim GM/S$ and $M\sim\Sigma S^2$. Solomon et al. favour this interpretation using their mean surface density $170\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{pc^{-2}}$. The linewidth–size exponent therefore excludes the K41 value under the ensemble assumption but does not distinguish a compressive cascade from virial balance.

Using $\sigma_v(1\,\mathrm{pc})=1.0\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ in the uniform-sphere virial estimate gives $\Sigma=222M_\odot\,\mathrm{pc^{-2}}$, compared with the reported 170. The ratio 1.31 is acceptable only as an order-of-magnitude check because the adopted virial coefficient differs from Solomon et al.’s.

Take air near the ground with kinematic viscosity $\nu = 0.15\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ — a two-significant-figure value for which this book obtained no primary source — stirred at $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{W\,kg^{-1}}$. First convert $\varepsilon$ to CGS, then find $\eta$, $u_\eta$ and $\tau_\eta$, and verify (3.2). To how many figures may the three be quoted?
Solution

$1\ \mathrm{W\,kg^{-1}} = 10^{7}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}/10^{3}\ \mathrm{g} = 10^{4}\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$, so $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{W\,kg^{-1}} = 10\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}\,s^{-1}} = 10\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-3}}$. The conversion is worth doing explicitly: a factor $10^4$ mislaid here moves $\eta$ by $10$.

Then $\eta = (\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4} = (0.15^3/10)^{1/4} = 0.1355\ \mathrm{cm} = 1.355$ mm; $u_\eta = (\nu\varepsilon)^{1/4} = (1.5)^{1/4} = 1.11\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$; $\tau_\eta = (\nu/\varepsilon)^{1/2} = (0.015)^{1/2} = 0.12247\ \mathrm{s} = 122.47$ ms. Check: $u_\eta\eta/\nu = 1.11\times0.1355/0.15 = 1.000000$, as (3.2) requires identically.

Two figures. $\nu$ carries two and every one of the three is a power of it, so $\eta \approx 1.4$ mm, $u_\eta \approx 1.1\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$, $\tau_\eta \approx 0.12$ s is the appropriate statement. The extra digits above are carried only so the identity closes to $1.000000$. They are not accuracy.

C2. A molecular cloud $10$ pc across has Larson's three-dimensional velocity dispersion $\sigma_L = 2.64\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and, at $T = 10$ K with $\mu = 2.33$ and $\gamma = 5/3$, a sound speed $c_s = 0.244\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Find the eddy turnover time at the outer scale, and the time sound would take to cross the cloud. Which is the relevant dynamical time, and why?
Solution

$10\ \mathrm{pc} = 3.0857\times10^{19}$ cm. Turnover: $L/\sigma_L = 3.0857\times10^{19}/2.64\times10^{5} = 1.169\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{s} = 3.70$ Myr. Sound crossing: $L/c_s = 3.0857\times10^{19}/2.439\times10^{4} = 1.265\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s} = 40.1$ Myr.

The ratio of the sound-crossing time to the turnover time is the Mach number, $10.82$. The turnover time governs the cascade and rearrangement of the cloud structure. Sound crosses the region more than ten times too slowly to maintain pressure communication during one turnover.

The three-dimensionality of $\sigma_L$ matters here. Larson adds his dispersion into a three-dimensional $\sigma^2$, so $2.64\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ is the full speed and not one component. Using a one-dimensional linewidth in its place would give a turnover time $\sqrt3$ too long.

C3. For the same cloud, take $\varepsilon = U^3/L$ with $U = \sigma_L$ — note that this is a K41 relation and not a measurement — and evaluate the four-fifths law (4.1) at $\ell = 1$ pc. Then compare $|S_3|^{1/3}$ with $(\varepsilon\ell)^{1/3}$, and say what the ratio is without computing it a second time.
Solution

$\varepsilon = (2.64\times10^{5})^3/3.0857\times10^{19} = 5.963\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$. At $\ell = 1\ \mathrm{pc} = 3.0857\times10^{18}$ cm,

$S_3 = -\tfrac45\varepsilon\ell = -0.8\times5.963\times10^{-4}\times3.0857\times10^{18} = -1.472\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-3}}$, so $|S_3|^{1/3} = 1.138\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, against $(\varepsilon\ell)^{1/3} = 1.225\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$.

The exact-to-dimensional velocity ratio is $(4/5)^{1/3}=0.9283$. Both expressions contain the same product $\varepsilon\ell$ and differ only by the exact coefficient. The dimensional estimate is therefore accurate to about eight per cent for this quantity.

The negative third-order moment means longitudinal increments are skewed toward approaching motions, consistent with a forward cascade. Evaluating it with $\varepsilon=U^3/L$ still imports the outer-scale K41 estimate, even though the $4/5$ coefficient itself is exact.

D1. An intracluster medium has $n = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $T = 10^{8}$ K and $\ln\Lambda = 37.8$, giving a Coulomb mean free path of $22.5$ kpc and an ion mean thermal speed of $1450\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Turbulence of $U = 164\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ fills a region $L = 60$ kpc across. Compute $\varepsilon$ and the dissipation scale $\eta$ using the unmagnetised viscosity $\nu = \lambda\bar v_i/3$. Then repeat with the magnetised bound $\nu_\perp \le r_g\bar v_i/3$ at $B = 1\ \mu$G. Before quoting either answer, check whether the first one is admissible.
Solution

$\nu = \lambda\bar v_i/3 = (6.943\times10^{22})(1.450\times10^{8})/3 = 3.3557\times10^{30}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $\varepsilon = U^3/L = (1.64\times10^{7})^3/(1.8514\times10^{23}) = 2.382\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$. D2. In isothermal supersonic turbulence at root-mean-square sonic Mach number $\mathcal{M} = 10$, compute $\sigma_s$ from (9.3) and the fraction of the volume with $\rho \gt 100\langle\rho\rangle$ from (9.5), for the two forcing extremes: $b = 1/3$ (purely solenoidal in three dimensions) and $b = 1$ (purely compressive), both from Federrath et al. (2010). Comment on the ratio of the widths against the ratio of the tails.
Solution

$\sigma_s = \sqrt{\ln(1+b^2\mathcal{M}^2)}$. At $b = 1/3$: $b\mathcal{M} = 3.333$, $1+b^2\mathcal{M}^2 = 12.11$, $\sigma_s = 1.5793$. At $b = 1$: $b\mathcal{M} = 10$, $1+100 = 101$, $\sigma_s = 2.1483$. The widths differ by a factor $1.36$.

$s_0 = -\sigma_s^2/2$ is $-1.247$ and $-2.308$. With $\ln 100 = 4.605$, equation (9.5) gives $t_0 = (4.605+1.247)/(1.5793\sqrt2) = 2.620$ and $(4.605+2.308)/(2.1483\sqrt2) = 2.275$, so $f(\gt100) = \tfrac12\mathrm{erfc}(2.620) = 1.054\times10^{-4}$ and $\tfrac12\mathrm{erfc}(2.275) = 6.459\times10^{-4}$.

A factor $1.36$ in the width becomes a factor $6.1$ in the tail — and at $\rho \gt 1000\langle\rho\rangle$, a factor $73.9$. The reason is that the tail of a Gaussian is controlled by the exponent $-t_0^2$, and $t_0$ is inversely proportional to $\sigma_s$; a small change in the denominator is a large change after exponentiation. Since star formation is a threshold process in the far tail, the forcing that a cloud experiences matters far more for its star-formation rate than for any bulk property of its density field.

Note that $\sigma_s$ is dimensionless and is not a speed. It is the width of $\ln(\rho/\langle\rho\rangle)$, and comparing it with any of the speeds of §6.4 would be meaningless.

By (3.4), $L/\eta = \mathrm{Re}^{3/4} = (8.107\times10^{7})^{3/4} = 8.54\times10^{5}$. Directly: $\varepsilon = 5.963\times10^{-4}$ from C3 gives $\eta = (\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4} = 3.6\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm} = 2.4$ au, and $3.0857\times10^{19}/3.6\times10^{13} = 8.5\times10^{5}$. The two agree, as they must: (3.4) was derived by putting $\varepsilon = U^3/L$ into the definition of $\eta$.

Here $\mathrm{Kn}=3.2\times10^{-7}$, safely inside the Chapman–Enskog regime. The Reynolds-number estimate is therefore meaningful, unlike the collisionless example in D1. Because the adopted cross-section is uncertain by about a factor of two, one significant figure is sufficient.

K1. Price a direct numerical simulation at $\mathrm{Re} = 10^{6}$. Give the grid side, the total number of points, and the size of one velocity snapshot storing three components at eight bytes each, so 24 bytes per point, and nothing else. Then give the number of point-updates in one outer turnover on the CFL count $\mathrm{Re}^{3}$, and say how much the softer $\mathrm{Re}^{11/4}$ count would understate it.
Solution

Side: $\mathrm{Re}^{3/4} = (10^{6})^{3/4} = 31623$ points. Total: $\mathrm{Re}^{9/4} = 3.162\times10^{13}$ points. Snapshot: $24\times3.162\times10^{13} = 7.589\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{B} = 759$ TB.

Work on the CFL count: $\mathrm{Re}^{3} = 10^{18}$ point-updates per outer turnover. On the Kolmogorov-time count: $\mathrm{Re}^{11/4} = 3.16\times10^{16}$, smaller by $\mathrm{Re}^{1/4} = 31.6$ — which is (3.6), $U/u_\eta$. The softer count is the number of turnovers of the smallest eddy. It is not the number of steps a time-explicit code takes, because the code is limited by the largest speed on the smallest cell and not by the local eddy time.

And $759$ TB is one snapshot of one field at a Reynolds number three orders of magnitude below the solar photosphere of §1. A time integrator needs at least two copies, and a magnetohydrodynamic code needs three more fields.

K2. (a) How large a line-of-sight velocity dispersion would the Perseus core need for a hydrostatic mass analysis to miss 20 per cent of the mass, at $k_{\rm B}T = 4.1$ keV and $\mu = 0.61$? Compare with Hitomi's measured $164\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. (b) Evaluate $E(k)$ at $k = 1/(1\ \mathrm{pc})$ in the $10$ pc cloud of D3, taking the three-dimensional Kolmogorov constant $C = 1.62$ — and state why the answer should be read as a range.
Solution

(a) Invert (8.4): a bias of $0.20 = \alpha/(1+\alpha)$ needs $\alpha = 0.25$. Then (8.3) rearranges to $\sigma_v = \sqrt{\alpha\,k_{\rm B}T/(\mu m_{\rm u})}$. With $k_{\rm B}T = 4.1\ \mathrm{keV} = 6.569\times10^{-9}$ erg and $\mu m_{\rm u} = 0.61\times1.6605\times10^{-24} = 1.013\times10^{-24}$ g, $k_{\rm B}T/(\mu m_{\rm u}) = 6.485\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-2}}$, so $\sigma_v = \sqrt{1.621\times10^{15}} = 4.03\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}} = 403\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$.

The required dispersion is $2.46$ times the measured $164\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Because $\alpha\propto\sigma_v^2$, this corresponds to a factor of $6.0$ in the pressure ratio. A twenty-per-cent hydrostatic mass bias is therefore excluded by a wide margin for this core. The result scales as $\mu^{-1/2}$ and is independent of density.

(b) $E(k) = C\varepsilon^{2/3}k^{-5/3}$ with $\varepsilon = 5.963\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-3}}$ and $k = 1/(3.0857\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{cm})$: $\varepsilon^{2/3} = 7.08\times10^{-3}$, $k^{-5/3} = 6.55\times10^{30}$, so $E = 1.62\times7.08\times10^{-3}\times6.55\times10^{30} = 7.503\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-2}}$.

The measured three-dimensional Kolmogorov constant spans approximately $1.53$–$1.62$. This gives $E(k)=7.1$–$7.5\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-2}}$ for the stated inputs. Reporting the range reflects the uncertainty in the constant and preserves the spectral-density units.

K3. (a) Give the Reynolds number of the intracluster medium of D1 without a magnetic field, printing the Knudsen number beside it, and then with one. (b) At what Reynolds number does a direct numerical simulation need $N = 10^{9}$ grid points, and what does that say about the largest flow anyone has simulated?
Solution

The unmagnetised formula gives $\mathrm{Re}=0.90$ at $\mathrm{Kn}=0.375$. Since this Knudsen number lies outside the Chapman–Enskog regime, the result does not establish laminar flow. It only shows that the collisional viscosity formula is being extrapolated beyond its domain.

Magnetised: $\nu_\perp \le r_g\bar v_i/3 = 6.4824\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$, so $\mathrm{Re}_\perp \ge 4.68\times10^{12}$. It is a lower bound and must never be written as a value, because (6.2) bounds $\nu_\perp$ from above: scattering can only be more effective than the bare gyro-orbit. The true effective viscosity is set by microinstabilities, and Zhuravleva et al. (2019) bound its suppression below the Coulomb value at a factor of $10$ to $1000$.

(b) $N = \mathrm{Re}^{9/4} = 10^{9}$ gives $\mathrm{Re} = (10^{9})^{4/9} = 1.000\times10^{4}$. A $1000^3$ grid — a large calculation — resolves a Reynolds number of ten thousand. The photosphere of §1 is at $1.4\times10^{9}$, five orders of magnitude higher, and by (3.4) it would need $\mathrm{Re}^{9/4} = 3.8\times10^{20}$ points. No increase in computer power closes a gap of eleven and a half orders of magnitude in $N$, which is why astrophysical codes model the small scales instead of resolving them.
