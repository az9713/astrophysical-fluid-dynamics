12. Magnetohydrodynamics

Magnetohydrodynamics extends ordinary fluid dynamics by adding the Lorentz force and an equation for magnetic-field evolution. The resulting theory describes Alfvén waves, magnetic support, anisotropic transport, the Parker spiral, and the magnetorotational instability.

Magnetic fields have already entered several physical arguments in this book. They confine weakly collisional plasmas, modify the stability of interfaces and clouds, and make transport direction-dependent. We now treat the field as a dynamical variable. Magnetohydrodynamics, or MHD, couples the fluid equations of Module 2 to Maxwell’s equations.

This coupling introduces two equations. The momentum equation acquires the Lorentz force, while Faraday’s law and Ohm’s law determine the evolution of $\mathbf B$. The kinetic derivation in Module 2 §4 remains applicable because $\nabla_{\mathbf v}\!\cdot\mathbf a=0$ for the Lorentz acceleration $\mathbf a=(q/mc)\,\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$. We can therefore retain the same moment hierarchy and add the electromagnetic terms explicitly.

Scope of the empirical comparisons. The four tests in this module use astronomical data. Section 4 instead reproduces a calculation from Module 7 to verify cross-module consistency. That reproduction checks the algebra and conventions; it is not an independent measurement.

1. When is a fluid magnetised?

The word magnetised refers to two different physical comparisons. The plasma beta $\beta$ compares gas pressure with magnetic pressure, so it measures the field’s dynamical importance. The product $\omega_{\rm c}\tau$ compares the gyrofrequency with the collision rate. It measures whether a particle executes appreciable gyromotion between collisions. Either condition may hold without the other.

Definition 1 (magnetic pressure, the plasma beta, and the Alfvén speed). For a field of strength $B$ in a gas of number density $n$ at temperature $T$ and mass density $\rho$, define $$ p_{\rm mag} \equiv \frac{B^2}{8\pi}, \qquad \beta \equiv \frac{p_{\rm gas}}{p_{\rm mag}} = \frac{8\pi nk_{\rm B}T}{B^2}, \qquad v_{\rm A} \equiv \frac{B}{\sqrt{4\pi\rho}}. \tag{1.1}$$ $\beta$ asks whether the field can push the gas. $v_{\rm A}$ is a combination of $B$ and $\rho$ with the dimensions of a speed; that it is the speed of anything is not assumed here and is proved in Proposition 6. Both $p_{\rm mag}$ and the tension that accompanies it are derived, not posited, in §3.
Definition 2 (magnetisation). The dimensionless product $\omega_{\rm c}\tau$ measures the gyro-phase accumulated between collisions. Here $\omega_{\rm c}=ZeB/mc$, and $\tau$ is Braginskii's collision time for electrons or ions. For the intracluster medium in §8, $\omega_i\tau_i=4.3\times10^{12}$. This large value leads to the strong suppression of perpendicular classical transport. Section 8.1 compares Braginskii's convention with the alternative mean-free-path convention used elsewhere in the book.
Proposition 1 (the two speeds are one ratio). For an ideal gas with ratio of specific heats $\gamma$, $$ \frac{v_{\rm A}}{c_s} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma\beta}} \tag{1.2}$$ identically, where $c_s = \sqrt{\gamma p_{\rm gas}/\rho}$. The mass per particle cancels.
$v_{\rm A}^2/c_s^2 = (B^2/4\pi\rho)\big/(\gamma p_{\rm gas}/\rho) = B^2/(4\pi\gamma p_{\rm gas})$. The density has cancelled, and $B^2/4\pi = 2p_{\rm mag}$, so the ratio is $2p_{\rm mag}/(\gamma p_{\rm gas}) = 2/(\gamma\beta)$. ∎

Plasma beta provides a convenient environmental census because it does not require an assumed mean particle mass. Computing the Alfvén speed does require that assumption. The table lists both quantities and verifies $v_{\rm A}^2/c_s^2=2/(\gamma\beta)$. Agreement with this identity checks that the pressure, density, composition, and sound-speed conventions are mutually consistent.

2. The induction equation and magnetic flux freezing

Maxwell’s equations determine the magnetic field, while the fluid equations determine the motion of the gas. Ohm’s law couples these systems by relating the electric field to the flow and current. Substitution into Faraday’s law produces an induction equation with two competing processes: advection of magnetic flux and resistive diffusion.

Proposition 2 (the induction equation). For a fluid of uniform electrical conductivity $\sigma_{\rm e}$ moving non-relativistically, $$ \frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} \;=\; \nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B) \;+\; \eta_{\rm m}\nabla^2\mathbf B, \qquad \eta_{\rm m}\equiv\frac{c^2}{4\pi\sigma_{\rm e}}, \tag{2.1}$$ with $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ preserved for all time if it holds once.
Ohm's law in the frame of the moving fluid is $\mathbf j = \sigma_{\rm e}(\mathbf E + \mathbf u\times\mathbf B/c)$, so $\mathbf E = \mathbf j/\sigma_{\rm e} - \mathbf u\times\mathbf B/c$. Ampère's law without the displacement current gives $\mathbf j = (c/4\pi)\nabla\times\mathbf B$. Substituting both into Faraday's law $\partial\mathbf B/\partial t = -c\,\nabla\times\mathbf E$, $$ \frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} = \nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B) - \frac{c^2}{4\pi\sigma_{\rm e}}\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B), $$ and $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B) - \nabla^2\mathbf B = -\nabla^2\mathbf B$. Taking the divergence of (2.1) gives $\partial(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)/\partial t = \eta_{\rm m}\nabla^2(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)$, a diffusion equation for $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B$; if it starts at zero it stays there. ∎
Non-relativistic ordering. With $E\sim UB/c$, the displacement current is smaller than $\nabla\times\mathbf B$ by a factor of order $(U/c)^2$. The fastest flow considered here is the solar wind at $435.6\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, for which this factor is $2.1\times10^{-6}$. The approximation therefore applies to the present examples. It fails for relativistic jets and pulsar magnetospheres.
Proposition 3 (the magnetic Reynolds number, and why size is the variable). The ratio of the two terms of (2.1) on a flow of speed $U$ over a scale $L$ is $$ \mathrm{R_m} \;\equiv\; \frac{UL}{\eta_{\rm m}}. \tag{2.2}$$ With Braginskii's $\sigma_\parallel = 1.96\,n_{\rm e}e^2\tau_{\rm e}/m_{\rm e}$ and his $\tau_{\rm e}\propto T^{3/2}/(n\ln\Lambda)$, the density cancels and $$ \eta_{\rm m} \;\propto\; \frac{\ln\Lambda}{T^{3/2}} \tag{2.3}$$ depends on the temperature and the Coulomb logarithm alone. Moreover $$ \frac{L^2/\eta_{\rm m}}{L/U} \;=\; \frac{UL}{\eta_{\rm m}} \;=\; \mathrm{R_m} \tag{2.4}$$ identically: the ohmic decay time measured in the system's own dynamical times is the magnetic Reynolds number.
The inductive term is of order $UB/L$ and the resistive term of order $\eta_{\rm m}B/L^2$; their ratio is $UL/\eta_{\rm m}$. For (2.3), $\sigma_\parallel \propto n\tau_{\rm e}$ and $\tau_{\rm e}\propto T^{3/2}/(n\ln\Lambda)$, so $\sigma_\parallel\propto T^{3/2}/\ln\Lambda$ and $\eta_{\rm m} = c^2/4\pi\sigma_\parallel \propto \ln\Lambda/T^{3/2}$. Equation (2.4) is arithmetic: $(L^2/\eta_{\rm m})(U/L) = UL/\eta_{\rm m}$. ∎

Their relative importance is measured by the magnetic Reynolds number $\mathrm{R_m}=UL/\eta_{\rm m}$, where $U$ and $L$ are characteristic flow scales and $\eta_{\rm m}$ is the magnetic diffusivity. A large system can have $\mathrm{R_m}\gg1$ even when its conductivity is finite. Increasing $L$ slows diffusion relative to advection; it does not change the material conductivity. In a classical, fully ionised plasma, the leading density dependence of the resistivity cancels, leaving only the weaker density dependence contained in the Coulomb logarithm.

Proposition 4 (Alfvén's theorem). If $\eta_{\rm m} = 0$, the magnetic flux $$ \Phi(t) \;=\; \int_{S(t)}\mathbf B\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf S \tag{2.5}$$ through any surface $S(t)$ carried with the fluid is constant in time.
A surface moving with the fluid changes the flux through it in two ways: the field changes in place, and the boundary sweeps through the field. The transport theorem for a surface integral gives $$ \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t} = \int_{S}\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf S \;-\; \oint_{\partial S}(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol\ell, $$ where the second term is the sweeping, written with the sign that follows from $\mathrm{d}\mathbf S = \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf u\,\mathrm{d}t$ on the swept strip. Stokes's theorem turns the line integral into $\int_S\nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf S$, so $$ \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t} = \int_{S}\Big[\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} - \nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)\Big]\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf S = \int_{S}\eta_{\rm m}\nabla^2\mathbf B\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf S $$ by (2.1), which vanishes when $\eta_{\rm m} = 0$. The surface was arbitrary. ∎
Ohmic diffusion is extremely slow in the fully ionised examples. The smallest valid magnetic Reynolds number in the table is $1.024\times10^{12}$ for the solar corona. Equation (2.4) then gives an ohmic-decay time of approximately $10^{12}$ dynamical times. Flux freezing is therefore an excellent approximation on the dynamical scales considered here, although it does not preclude reconnection in thin current sheets.

Proposition 4 states the flux-freezing theorem associated with Alfvén (1942). The derivation given here is self-contained: when resistive diffusion is negligible, a material surface conserves its magnetic flux. The original paper is cited for historical attribution rather than as a missing step in the argument.

3. Magnetic pressure and tension

The plasma current couples the magnetic field to the fluid through the Lorentz force. Rewriting that force separates two effects. Spatial variation in $B^2/8\pi$ acts as a magnetic-pressure gradient, while curvature of the field lines produces magnetic tension. Pressure pushes across the field; tension acts along the local direction of a curved field line.

Proposition 5 (the Lorentz force splits into a pressure and a tension). The force per unit volume on a conducting fluid is $$ \mathbf f = \frac{1}{c}\,\mathbf j\times\mathbf B = \frac{1}{4\pi}(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf B = -\nabla\!\left(\frac{B^2}{8\pi}\right) + \frac{(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B}{4\pi}. \tag{3.1}$$ The first term is an isotropic pressure $p_{\rm mag} = B^2/8\pi$. The second is a tension $B^2/4\pi$ along the field; where a field line has radius of curvature $R_c$ it contributes a force per volume $B^2/(4\pi R_c)$ directed toward the centre of curvature.
Use the identity $\nabla(\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf B) = 2(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B + 2\mathbf B\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B)$, so that $$ (\nabla\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf B = -\mathbf B\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B) = (\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B - \tfrac12\nabla B^2. $$ Dividing by $4\pi$ gives (3.1). For the curvature, write $\mathbf B = B\hat{\mathbf b}$ with $\hat{\mathbf b}$ a unit vector. Then $$ \frac{(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B}{4\pi} = \frac{B}{4\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\ell}\big(B\hat{\mathbf b}\big) = \hat{\mathbf b}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\ell}\!\left(\frac{B^2}{8\pi}\right) + \frac{B^2}{4\pi}\frac{\partial\hat{\mathbf b}}{\partial\ell}, $$ where $\partial/\partial\ell$ is the derivative along the field line. The first piece is a pressure gradient along $\hat{\mathbf b}$, which exactly cancels the field-parallel part of $-\nabla(B^2/8\pi)$ — so the magnetic pressure acts only across the field, never along it. The second piece uses the Frenet relation $\partial\hat{\mathbf b}/\partial\ell = -\hat{\mathbf n}/R_c$ with $\hat{\mathbf n}$ pointing away from the centre of curvature, giving a force $B^2/(4\pi R_c)$ toward it. ∎
The cancellation in that proof is the whole physical content, and it is easy to read past. A magnetic field is not a gas with pressure $B^2/8\pi$. Along its own direction the pressure term is cancelled exactly, and a straight uniform field exerts no force at all. What a field resists is being squeezed (the pressure, acting across the lines) and being bent (the tension, acting along them). Every result in §4 to §9 is one of these two, or both.

4. Magnetohydrodynamic wave speeds

Module 4 derived sound waves for a gas whose only restoring force was pressure. A magnetic field adds both magnetic pressure and field-line tension. These additional restoring forces split the compressive response into fast and slow magnetosonic modes and introduce the transverse Alfvén mode.

Proposition 6 (the three MHD wave speeds). Linearising the ideal MHD equations about a uniform state with field $\mathbf B_0$, for a plane wave with wavevector at angle $\theta$ to $\mathbf B_0$, the phase speeds are $$ v_{\rm Alfv\acute en} = v_{\rm A}\cos\theta, \qquad v_{\pm}^2 = \tfrac12\left(v_{\rm A}^2 + c_s^2\right) \pm \tfrac12\sqrt{\left(v_{\rm A}^2+c_s^2\right)^2 - 4v_{\rm A}^2c_s^2\cos^2\theta}, \tag{4.1}$$ with $v_+$ the fast and $v_-$ the slow magnetosonic speed. At $\theta = 0$, $v_+ = \max(v_{\rm A},c_s)$ and $v_- = \min(v_{\rm A},c_s)$. At $\theta = 90^\circ$ the slow and Alfvén speeds both vanish and only the fast wave survives, at $\sqrt{v_{\rm A}^2+c_s^2}$.
Write $\rho = \rho_0+\rho_1$, $\mathbf u = \mathbf u_1$, $\mathbf B = \mathbf B_0 + \mathbf B_1$ and keep first order. Continuity and the adiabatic closure give $p_1 = c_s^2\rho_1$. The induction equation (2.1) at $\eta_{\rm m}=0$ gives $\partial_t\mathbf B_1 = \nabla\times(\mathbf u_1\times\mathbf B_0)$; and the momentum equation with (3.1) gives $$ \rho_0\,\partial_t\mathbf u_1 = -\nabla p_1 - \nabla\!\left(\frac{\mathbf B_0\cdot\mathbf B_1}{4\pi}\right) + \frac{(\mathbf B_0\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B_1}{4\pi}. $$ Substituting $\propto\exp[i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x-\omega t)]$ turns continuity into $\rho_1 = \rho_0(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1)/\omega$ and the induction equation into $$ \mathbf B_1 = \frac{\mathbf B_0(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1) - \mathbf u_1(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf B_0)}{\omega}. $$ Putting both into the momentum equation and writing $\mathbf v_{\rm A} \equiv \mathbf B_0/\sqrt{4\pi\rho_0}$, so that $|\mathbf v_{\rm A}| = v_{\rm A}$, gives one vector equation in $\mathbf u_1$ alone: $$ \omega^2\mathbf u_1 = \left(c_s^2+v_{\rm A}^2\right)(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1)\,\mathbf k \;+\; (\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v_{\rm A})\Big[(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v_{\rm A})\mathbf u_1 - (\mathbf v_{\rm A}\cdot\mathbf u_1)\mathbf k - (\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1)\mathbf v_{\rm A}\Big]. \tag{4.1a}$$ Now choose axes: $\mathbf B_0 = B_0\hat{\mathbf z}$ and $\mathbf k = k(\sin\theta,\,0,\,\cos\theta)$, so $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v_{\rm A} = kv_{\rm A}\cos\theta$.

The $y$ component decouples. $u_y$ appears in neither $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1$ nor $\mathbf v_{\rm A}\cdot\mathbf u_1$, and $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf v_{\rm A}$ have no $y$ component, so every term on the right of (4.1a) except the first inside the bracket vanishes for it: $\omega^2u_y = k^2v_{\rm A}^2\cos^2\theta\,u_y$. This motion is perpendicular to both $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf B_0$, so it compresses nothing — $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1 = 0$ means $\rho_1 = 0$ and $p_1 = 0$ — and the only restoring force left is the tension. That is the Alfvén wave, and the proof shows what it is: a transverse wave on a string under tension $B^2/4\pi$ with mass per unit length $\rho$, whose speed $\sqrt{\rm tension/\rho}$ is exactly $v_{\rm A}$ — which is the promised justification for calling $v_{\rm A}$ a speed.

The $x$ and $z$ components remain, and they lie in the plane of $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf B_0$. Write $v \equiv \omega/k$ and $\Delta \equiv \sin\theta\,u_x + \cos\theta\,u_z$, so $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1 = k\Delta$. The $z$ component of (4.1a) is $$ v^2u_z = (c_s^2+v_{\rm A}^2)\Delta\cos\theta + v_{\rm A}^2\cos^2\theta\,u_z - v_{\rm A}^2\cos^2\theta\,u_z - v_{\rm A}^2\cos\theta\,\Delta = c_s^2\cos\theta\,\Delta, $$ the two tension terms in $u_z$ cancelling exactly — a field does nothing to motion along itself, which is §3's cancellation seen again. The $x$ component is $$ v^2u_x = (c_s^2+v_{\rm A}^2)\Delta\sin\theta + v_{\rm A}^2\cos^2\theta\,u_x - v_{\rm A}^2\cos\theta\sin\theta\,u_z. $$ Collecting, and using $(c_s^2+v_{\rm A}^2)\sin^2\theta + v_{\rm A}^2\cos^2\theta = c_s^2\sin^2\theta + v_{\rm A}^2$, $$ \begin{pmatrix} v^2 - c_s^2\sin^2\theta - v_{\rm A}^2 & -c_s^2\sin\theta\cos\theta \\ -c_s^2\sin\theta\cos\theta & v^2 - c_s^2\cos^2\theta \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u_x\\ u_z\end{pmatrix} = 0, $$ and a non-trivial solution needs the determinant to vanish: $$ \big(v^2 - c_s^2\sin^2\theta - v_{\rm A}^2\big)\big(v^2 - c_s^2\cos^2\theta\big) - c_s^4\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta = 0. $$ The two $c_s^4\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta$ terms cancel, leaving $$ v^4 - v^2\left(v_{\rm A}^2+c_s^2\right) + v_{\rm A}^2c_s^2\cos^2\theta = 0, $$ a quadratic in $v^2$ whose two roots are (4.1). At $\cos\theta = 1$ it factorises as $(v^2-v_{\rm A}^2)(v^2-c_s^2)=0$; at $\cos\theta = 0$ the constant term vanishes, so one root is $0$ and the other $v_{\rm A}^2+c_s^2$. ∎

5. The Parker spiral

The Parker spiral follows from solar rotation and radial wind expansion. Under ideal MHD, a field line remains tied to its rotating photospheric footpoint while the wind carries the outer portion away from the Sun. The radial and azimuthal motions therefore wind the field into a spiral. Once the solar rotation rate and wind speed are specified, the idealised geometry has no additional fitting parameter.

Proposition 7 (the Parker spiral). For a steady, spherically symmetric, radial wind of speed $v(r)$ from a source surface at $r_0$, on a star rotating at angular rate $\Omega$, the frozen-in field has $$ B_r(r) = B_r(r_0)\left(\frac{r_0}{r}\right)^{2}, \qquad \frac{B_\phi}{B_r} = -\,\frac{\Omega\,(r-r_0)\sin\theta}{v}, \tag{5.1}$$ with $\theta$ the colatitude. The first follows from $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ alone and is exact. The second is the kinematics of a line whose foot rotates.
The radial part. $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ integrated over a sphere of radius $r$ gives $\oint B_r\,\mathrm{d}S = 4\pi r^2\langle B_r\rangle = $ constant, since no flux is created between two spheres. Hence $B_r r^2$ is constant. No dynamics entered, which is why this half is exact and is the half the control in Check 1 tests.

The azimuthal part. Work in the frame rotating with the star, in which the flow is steady. There the velocity is $\mathbf v' = v\hat{\mathbf r} - \Omega(r-r_0)\sin\theta\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$ — Parker's equation (24), with $r_0$ his $b$ — and in a steady state the field must be parallel to the flow, for otherwise the flow would sweep the field into a configuration that changes with time. Hence $\mathbf B \parallel \mathbf v'$, so $$ \frac{B_\phi}{B_r} = \frac{v'_\phi}{v'_r} = -\frac{\Omega(r-r_0)\sin\theta}{v}, $$ which is Parker's equation (26). ∎
Finite source-surface radius. Parker's equation (26) contains $(r-r_0)$, whereas the familiar $\Omega r\sin\theta/v$ form assumes $r\gg r_0$. At 1 au with $r_0=2.5\,R_\odot$, retaining $r_0$ lowers $-B_\phi/B_r$ from $0.9840$ to $0.9726$, or by $1.16$ per cent. The symbol $r_0$ denotes the source-surface radius throughout this module.

The spiral changes the radial dependence of the total field. Define $x\equiv\Omega(r-r_0)\sin\theta/v$. Then $$ |\mathbf B| = B_r\sqrt{1+x^2}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln|\mathbf B|}{\mathrm{d}\ln r} = -2 + \frac{x^2}{1+x^2}\left(\frac{r}{r-r_0} - \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln v}{\mathrm{d}\ln r}\right). \tag{5.2}$$ Close to the Sun, $x\ll1$, the field is nearly radial, and $|\mathbf B|\propto r^{-2}$. Farther out, $x\gg1$, the azimuthal component dominates and the index approaches $-1$. A spacecraft sampling the transition measures an intermediate exponent, as shown in Fig. 2.

6. Locating the Alfvén surface

Close to the Sun, the wind is sub-Alfvénic: $v\lt v_{\rm A}$. Alfvénic disturbances can then propagate upstream against the outflow. Farther out, $v\gt v_{\rm A}$, and the wind carries all such disturbances away from the Sun. The Alfvén surface is the locus $v=v_{\rm A}$ separating these causal regimes.

Proposition 8 (the wind crosses its own radial Alfvén speed exactly once). For the wind of Proposition 7, mass conservation gives $\rho \propto r^{-2}v^{-1}$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ gives $B_r\propto r^{-2}$, so the radial Alfvén speed obeys $$ v_{\rm A,r} \;=\; \frac{B_r}{\sqrt{4\pi\rho}} \;\propto\; \frac{r^{-2}}{\sqrt{r^{-2}v^{-1}}} \;=\; r^{-1}v^{1/2}. \tag{6.1}$$ If $v$ is non-decreasing outward, $v/v_{\rm A,r}\propto r\,v^{1/2}$ is strictly increasing, so the equation $v = v_{\rm A,r}$ has exactly one root $r_{\rm A}$.
$\dot M = 4\pi r^2\rho v$ constant gives $\rho\propto r^{-2}v^{-1}$; substituting into $B_r/\sqrt{4\pi\rho}$ with $B_r\propto r^{-2}$ gives (6.1). Then $v/v_{\rm A,r}\propto v\cdot r\,v^{-1/2} = r\,v^{1/2}$, a product of two non-decreasing positive factors one of which is strictly increasing. A strictly increasing continuous function crosses the value $1$ at most once, and does so at least once given that it is $\ll1$ at the base and $\gg1$ at 1 au. ∎

7. Magnetic support against gravitational collapse

A magnetic field can oppose gravitational contraction across its field lines. The relevant measure is the mass per unit magnetic flux, $M/\Phi$. A cloud with a ratio below the critical value is subcritical; magnetic stresses can support it in the idealised geometry. A supercritical cloud exceeds that value, so its field cannot prevent collapse by itself. Ideal flux freezing conserves $M/\Phi$ for a material flux tube. Non-ideal processes, including ambipolar diffusion, can change it. The numerical critical coefficient depends on the assumed cloud geometry.

Definition 3 (the mass-to-flux ratio). For a cloud of mass $M$ threaded by magnetic flux $\Phi$, $$ \lambda_\Phi \;\equiv\; \frac{M/\Phi}{(M/\Phi)_{\rm crit}}, \qquad \left(\frac{M}{\Phi}\right)_{\rm crit} = \frac{c_\Phi}{\sqrt G}. \tag{7.1}$$ $\lambda_\Phi \gt 1$ is supercritical: the field cannot hold the cloud. The symbol carries its subscript because bare $\lambda$ is Module 1's mean free path book-wide, and both appear in this module.
Proposition 9 (the critical ratio is a pure number). For a flux-frozen, self-gravitating cloud, the gravitational and magnetic energies both scale as $1/R$ at fixed $M$ and $\Phi$, so their ratio is independent of the radius and the criterion for collapse is a condition on $M/\Phi$ alone. The coefficient is $$ c_\Phi = \frac{c_1\sqrt5}{3\pi} = 0.125745 \quad\text{(uniform sphere)}, \qquad c_\Phi = \frac{1}{2\pi} = 0.159155 \quad\text{(flattened cloud)}. \tag{7.2}$$
The form is derived. For a uniform sphere of mass $M$ and radius $R$ the gravitational energy is $W = -3GM^2/5R$. Flux freezing fixes $\Phi = \pi R^2\langle B\rangle$, so $\langle B\rangle = \Phi/\pi R^2$ and the magnetic energy inside the cloud is $\mathcal M = (B^2/8\pi)(4\pi R^3/3) = \Phi^2/(6\pi^2R)$. Both go as $1/R$, so their sum is either positive or negative for all $R$ at once: compressing the cloud cannot change the sign. The virial condition $\mathcal M + W = 0$ therefore gives a critical ratio and not a critical radius, $$ \frac{\Phi^2}{6\pi^2R} = \frac{3GM^2}{5R} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{M}{\Phi} = \frac{1}{3\pi}\sqrt{\frac{5}{2G}} = \frac{0.16777}{\sqrt G}. $$ That is the estimate this argument produces, and it is not the normalisation (7.2) uses. Mouschovias & Spitzer's equation (2) is written against $(1/3\pi)\sqrt{5/G} = 0.23726/\sqrt G$, which is $\sqrt2$ larger — it counts a magnetic energy twice the energy of the field inside the cloud, and where that second factor of two comes from is not derived here and is not derived anywhere in this book. Their $c_1$ is measured against their normalisation and not against the estimate above, so $c_1 = 0.53$ is not the size of the correction to this line: the ratio of their coefficient to the estimate above is $0.125745/0.16777 = 0.7495$. Every number in this module uses their formula with their $c_1$, so none of them turns on this.

The coefficients are stated, not derived. Mouschovias & Spitzer (1976) show that the virial estimate is an approximation and fit a correction factor to exact equilibrium models: their equation (2) reads $M_{c\Phi} = (c_1/3\pi)(5/G)^{1/2}\Phi$ and their equation (4) gives $c_1 = 0.53$, whence $c_\Phi = 0.53\sqrt5/3\pi = 0.125745$. 8. Transport across a magnetic field

Transport in the intracluster medium is strongly direction-dependent. Classical isotropic viscosity would make the Reynolds number too small to explain the observed turbulent fluctuations. Unrestricted classical conduction would also redistribute heat rapidly. A magnetic field suppresses motion across its direction while permitting much faster transport along it. Braginskii’s coefficients describe this local anisotropy, whereas astronomical observations constrain an effective coefficient averaged over tangled fields and large spatial scales.

Proposition 10 (perpendicular transport is suppressed by $(\omega_{\rm c}\tau)^{-2}$). In an unmagnetised gas a random walk of step $\lambda$ per collision time $\tau$ gives a diffusivity $\sim\lambda^2/\tau$. Across a field, a particle cannot travel $\lambda$ between collisions: it returns to within a gyroradius of where it started. The step is therefore $r_g$ rather than $\lambda$, and $$ \frac{D_\perp}{D_\parallel} \;\sim\; \frac{r_g^2}{\lambda^2} \;=\; \frac{1}{(\omega_{\rm c}\tau)^2}, \tag{8.1}$$ since $\lambda = v\tau$ and $r_g = v/\omega_{\rm c}$. The same factor governs the conductivity and the viscosity, with different numerical coefficients.
Between collisions the guiding centre is displaced along $\mathbf B$ by $\lambda$ and across it by at most $2r_g$. The perpendicular displacement per collision is therefore $O(r_g)$ and uncorrelated from one collision to the next. A random walk of $N$ steps of size $s$ in time $N\tau$ has diffusivity $s^2/\tau$, so $D_\perp/D_\parallel = r_g^2/\lambda^2$. With $\lambda = v\tau$ and $r_g = v/\omega_{\rm c}$ the speed cancels and the ratio is $(\omega_{\rm c}\tau)^{-2}$. ∎

For a hydrogen plasma, Braginskii’s equations (2.12) and (2.13) give the electron thermal conductivities.

$$ \kappa_\parallel = 3.16\,\frac{n_{\rm e}T_{\rm e}\tau_{\rm e}}{m_{\rm e}}, \qquad \kappa_\perp = 4.66\,\frac{n_{\rm e}T_{\rm e}}{m_{\rm e}\omega_{\rm e}^2\tau_{\rm e}}, \qquad \frac{\kappa_\perp}{\kappa_\parallel} = \frac{4.66}{3.16}\frac{1}{(\omega_{\rm e}\tau_{\rm e})^2}, \tag{8.2}$$

The parallel coefficient grows with collision time because particles travel farther between collisions. The perpendicular coefficient decreases because well-resolved gyromotion confines particles more effectively.

Braginskii’s ion-viscosity coefficients have the same qualitative structure. Module 10 §6.2 uses $\eta_0^{\rm B}=0.96\,n_iT_i\tau_i$ and $\eta_1^{\rm B}=(3/10)n_iT_i/(\omega_i^2\tau_i)$.

His Table 1 gives $\kappa_\parallel^{\rm e}=3.16,4.9,6.1,6.9,12.5$ for $Z=1,2,3,4,\infty$. The corresponding perpendicular coefficients are $4.66,4.0,3.7,3.6,3.2$. For $Z=1$, the ion-density factors in his collision times equal the electron density by charge neutrality.

8.2 What strong magnetisation does and does not imply

Strong particle magnetisation does not determine the effective viscosity. The large value of $\omega_i\tau_i$ places the ions in the strongly magnetised regime and strongly suppresses Braginskii's local perpendicular viscosity. Zhuravleva et al. infer only an upper bound on an effective cluster viscosity. Comparing that bound with a local anisotropic coefficient requires a model of field geometry and velocity structure. Without such a model, the observation neither measures nor excludes the classical perpendicular coefficient.

The large value of $\omega_i\tau_i$ establishes that individual ions are strongly magnetised. It does not determine the effective transport coefficient of a tangled, weakly collisional cluster plasma. That coefficient also depends on field-line geometry, pressure-anisotropy instabilities, and the relation between local stresses and coarse-grained motion. These effects must be modelled before Braginskii’s local tensor can be compared with the observed fluctuation spectrum.

Effective transport remains uncertain. Zhuravleva et al. give a bound that depends on the unknown Prandtl number. They also note that present observations do not establish whether the measured density fluctuations arise from turbulence. The local Braginskii coefficients therefore cannot be converted into a measured cluster-scale transport coefficient without additional assumptions.

9. The magnetorotational instability

Differentially rotating discs require a mechanism that transports angular momentum outward. The magnetorotational instability, or MRI, provides such a mechanism when a weak magnetic field couples neighbouring fluid elements. We analyse the instability locally, taking the background shear as given. The global disc structure and the $\alpha$ prescription are developed in Module 11.

A Keplerian disc has $\Omega\propto R^{-3/2}$ and specific angular momentum $\ell=\Omega R^2\propto R^{1/2}$. Because $\ell$ increases outward, the Rayleigh criterion predicts hydrodynamic stability. An outwardly displaced ring carries too little angular momentum for its new orbit and returns inward. A weak magnetic field changes this response by coupling displaced rings through magnetic tension. The coupling destabilises the differential rotation and permits outward angular-momentum transport.

Proposition 11 (the local MRI dispersion relation). Let $q \equiv -\mathrm{d}\ln\Omega/\mathrm{d}\ln R$, so $q = 3/2$ for a Keplerian disc, and let the epicyclic frequency be $\kappa_{\rm ep}^2 = 2(2-q)\Omega^2$. For an axisymmetric, incompressible perturbation with wavevector $\mathbf k\parallel\hat{\mathbf z}$ along a weak vertical field, writing $\sigma \equiv \gamma/\Omega$ and $x \equiv kv_{\rm A}/\Omega$, $$ \sigma^4 + \sigma^2\!\left(\frac{\kappa_{\rm ep}^2}{\Omega^2} + 2x^2\right) + x^2\!\left(x^2 - 4 + \frac{\kappa_{\rm ep}^2}{\Omega^2}\right) = 0. \tag{9.1}$$ There is a growing root whenever $x^2 \lt 2q$. At $q = 3/2$ this gives instability for $kv_{\rm A} \lt \sqrt3\,\Omega$, a maximum growth rate $$ \gamma_{\max} = \frac{q}{2}\,\Omega = 0.75\,\Omega \quad\text{at}\quad kv_{\rm A} = \frac{\sqrt{15}}{4}\,\Omega = 0.96825\,\Omega, \tag{9.2}$$ and marginal stability at $kv_{\rm A} = \sqrt3\,\Omega = 1.73205\,\Omega$.
Work in a local frame corotating at $R_0$ with coordinates $(x_1,x_2,x_3)$ along $(\hat{\mathbf R},\hat{\boldsymbol\phi},\hat{\mathbf z})$, and linearise the ideal MHD equations for displacements $\boldsymbol\xi\propto\exp(\gamma t + ikx_3)$. In the shearing sheet the equations of motion for a displacement are $\ddot\xi_1 = 2\Omega\dot\xi_2 + 2q\Omega^2\xi_1 + f_1$ and $\ddot\xi_2 = -2\Omega\dot\xi_1 + f_2$: the Coriolis coupling is $2\Omega$ in both, and the background shear appears once, as the tidal term $2q\Omega^2\xi_1$. A vertical field contributes a tension $f_i = -(kv_{\rm A})^2\xi_i$ to each in-plane component — the field lines act as springs joining the fluid element to its unperturbed radius. So $$ (\gamma^2 + k^2v_{\rm A}^2 - 2q\Omega^2)\,\xi_1 - 2\Omega\gamma\,\xi_2 = 0, \qquad 2\Omega\gamma\,\xi_1 + (\gamma^2 + k^2v_{\rm A}^2)\,\xi_2 = 0, $$ and the determinant condition is $$ (\gamma^2 + k^2v_{\rm A}^2 - 2q\Omega^2)(\gamma^2 + k^2v_{\rm A}^2) + 4\Omega^2\gamma^2 = 0, $$ which is (9.1) after dividing by $\Omega^4$ and using $\kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2 = 2(2-q) = 4-2q$.

The field-free limit is the check that this pair is the right one. At $k = 0$ it reduces to $\sigma^2(\sigma^2 + 4 - 2q) = 0$, so $\sigma^2 = 2q-4 = -\kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2$, which is $-1$ for a Keplerian disc: an oscillation at the epicyclic frequency and no growth. A Keplerian disc with no field is stable, as the opening of this section says, and any linearisation that does not reproduce that is wrong.

The instability condition. Equation (9.1) is a quadratic in $\sigma^2$ with positive coefficient on $\sigma^2$, so it has a positive root for $\sigma^2$ if and only if its constant term is negative, that is if and only if $x^2 - 4 + \kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2 \lt 0$. Substituting $\kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2 = 2(2-q)$ gives $x^2 \lt 2q$, so $x \lt \sqrt3$ at $q = 3/2$.

The maximum. Differentiate (9.1) implicitly with respect to $x^2$ and set $\mathrm{d}\sigma^2/\mathrm{d}x^2 = 0$: the surviving condition is $2\sigma^2 + 2x^2 - 4 + \kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2 = 0$. With $q = 3/2$, $\kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2 = 1$, this is $\sigma^2 = (3 - 2x^2)/2$. Substituting into (9.1) and solving gives $x^2 = 15/16$ and $\sigma^2 = 9/16$, hence $\sigma = 3/4$ and $x = \sqrt{15}/4$. ∎
Two things about this result that the algebra hides.
(i) There is no threshold field. The unstable band is $kv_{\rm A} \lt \sqrt3\,\Omega$, so a weaker field moves the band to larger $k$ — shorter wavelengths — without closing it. The instability disappears only when the longest unstable wavelength no longer fits inside the disc. That is why the condition that matters in practice is $\lambda_{\rm marg} \lt H$ and not a condition on $B$ alone.
(ii) The growth rate does not depend on the field at all — Fig. 5 is flat-topped at $0.75\,\Omega$ whatever the disc. $\gamma_{\max} = q\Omega/2$ contains no $B$, no $n$ and no $T$. A weaker field changes which wavelength grows fastest, not how fast it grows. In e-folds per orbit that is $2\pi(q/2) = 3\pi/2 = 4.712$ for every Keplerian disc — an identity, not a property of any particular one.

10. Anisotropy and critical balance

Module 10 finds a solar-wind magnetic spectrum close to $k^{-5/3}$. A hydrodynamic Kolmogorov argument alone cannot explain this exponent in a magnetised plasma because the cascade is anisotropic. Critical-balance theory supplies a separate argument for a $-5/3$ spectrum perpendicular to the local magnetic field while predicting a different scaling parallel to it.

Proposition 12 (critical balance gives the same exponent, and an anisotropy K41 does not have). Suppose an Alfvénic cascade keeps the linear Alfvén crossing time and the nonlinear turnover time equal at every scale: $$ k_\parallel v_{\rm A} \;\sim\; k_\perp\,\delta v_\perp. \tag{10.1}$$ If the perpendicular cascade is Kolmogorov, $\delta v_\perp \sim (\varepsilon/k_\perp)^{1/3}$, then $$ k_\parallel \;\sim\; k_\perp^{2/3}L^{-1/3}, \qquad E(k_\perp) \;\propto\; k_\perp^{-5/3}. \tag{10.2}$$
Substituting $\delta v_\perp \sim \varepsilon^{1/3}k_\perp^{-1/3}$ into (10.1) gives $k_\parallel \sim k_\perp^{2/3}\varepsilon^{1/3}/v_{\rm A}$; writing $\varepsilon \sim v_{\rm A}^3/L$ at the outer scale, where the balance is imposed, gives $\varepsilon^{1/3}/v_{\rm A} = L^{-1/3}$ and hence the first of (10.2). For the second, the perpendicular energy spectrum follows from $\delta v_\perp^2 \sim k_\perp E(k_\perp)$ with $\delta v_\perp \propto k_\perp^{-1/3}$, so $E \propto k_\perp^{-5/3}$. ∎
The spectral exponent alone does not distinguish the two cascades. K41 and critical balance both predict a $-5/3$ spectrum, but they predict different geometry. K41 assumes isotropic eddies, whereas critical balance gives $k_\parallel\propto k_\perp^{2/3}$. Module 10 reports solar-wind indices with a mean of $1.6550$, close to $5/3$. An anisotropy measurement is required to decide which physical mechanism applies.
Domain of the critical-balance result. Goldreich and Sridhar restrict their derivation to oppositely directed Alfvén-wave fluxes of equal strength. The solar wind generally has a larger outward than inward flux, so their symmetric result cannot be applied to it without modification. Agreement with a $-5/3$ solar-wind spectrum is therefore suggestive but does not by itself test the published symmetric theory.

The anisotropy relation must include the outer scale: $k_\parallel\approx k_\perp^{2/3}L^{-1/3}$. The factor $L^{-1/3}$ restores dimensional consistency. Writing only $k_\parallel\sim k_\perp^{2/3}$ would equate quantities with different physical dimensions.

11.1 Pressure anisotropy in weakly collisional plasmas

A magnetic field constrains charged-particle motion across the field but not along it. A magnetised collisionless plasma therefore need not possess a single scalar pressure. Instead it can have distinct components $p_\parallel$ and $p_\perp$. Collisions tend to equalise them, but that relaxation is slow in a weakly collisional plasma. The Chew–Goldberger–Low, or double-adiabatic, theory retains these two pressures. Solar-wind observations routinely measure the resulting anisotropy. When the anisotropy becomes large, firehose and mirror instabilities scatter particles and limit further departure from isotropy.

The single-fluid equations derived in this module assume an isotropic scalar pressure. They consequently exclude the firehose and mirror instabilities and cannot predict their regulation of transport. Describing those effects requires either kinetic theory or a fluid closure that evolves the parallel and perpendicular pressures separately.

K3. For the intracluster plasma in Problem D3, calculate ion magnetisation in two ways. First evaluate $\lambda/r_g$ using Module 1’s Coulomb mean free path at $\ln\Lambda=37.8$ and $r_g=v_\perp/\omega_{\rm c}$ with $v_\perp=\sqrt{2k_{\rm B}T/m_{\rm p}}$. Then evaluate $\omega_i\tau_i$ using Braginskii’s $\tau_i$ at $\ln\Lambda=37.081$. Derive the ratio between the results and state which convention belongs in Braginskii’s factor $(\omega_{\rm c}\tau)^{-2}$.
Solution

$\lambda = \mathbf{22.504}$ kpc $= 6.9440\times10^{22}$ cm — the Spitzer mean free path at this $n$, $T$ and $\ln\Lambda = 37.8$, which Module 1 prints rounded to $22.5$ kpc. Carry the unrounded value: $22.5$ kpc exactly would give $5.1759\times10^{12}$ below, and this module prints five figures. $v_\perp = \sqrt{2k_{\rm B}T/m_{\rm p}} = \mathbf{1285\ km\,s^{-1}}$ and $\omega_{\rm c} = eB/m_{\rm p}c = 9.579\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, so $r_g = v_\perp/\omega_{\rm c} = 1.3414\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm} = \mathbf{1.3414\times10^{5}}$ km. Hence $\lambda/r_g = \mathbf{5.1767\times10^{12}}$.

Braginskii's $\tau_i$ at $\ln\Lambda = 37.081$ is $4.4981\times10^{14}$ s, so $\omega_i\tau_i = \mathbf{4.3087\times10^{12}}$.

The ratio is $5.1767/4.3087 = \mathbf{1.2015}$, and it is two factors and nothing else:

$\sqrt{3/2} = \mathbf{1.224745}$, times $\ln\Lambda_{\rm e}/\ln\Lambda = 37.081/37.8 = \mathbf{0.980985}$, giving $1.224745\times0.980985 = \mathbf{1.2015}$.

The exact factor $\sqrt{3/2}$ comes from the thermal-speed convention. The mean free path uses the three-dimensional rms speed $\sqrt{3k_{\rm B}T/m}$, whereas the gyroradius uses the perpendicular rms speed $\sqrt{2k_{\rm B}T/m}$. Their ratio is $\sqrt{3/2}$. The remaining $1.9$ per cent comes from the two Coulomb logarithms.

Braginskii’s $\omega_{\rm c}\tau$ must be used in his transport factor $(\omega_{\rm c}\tau)^{-2}$ because the numerical transport coefficients are defined with that collision time. Substituting the mean-free-path convention would introduce an artificial factor $1.2015^2=1.44$.
