1. A fluid with no length

The inviscid equations contain no microscopic length scale. Viscosity and thermal conduction, which would set the internal thickness of a shock, were neglected in Module 2. The resulting equations can therefore admit discontinuities whose thickness is unresolved but whose jumps are fixed by conservation laws.

The inviscid equations can admit discontinuities. A shock is a propagating discontinuity across which matter flows and, for an admissible compressive gas-dynamic shock, entropy increases. A contact discontinuity is different: the normal velocity and pressure are continuous in the ideal gas-dynamic case, while density and temperature may jump. The jump conditions follow from conservation; additional conditions determine which type of discontinuity they describe.

Integrating the conservation laws across a thin shock relates the upstream and downstream states without resolving the internal layer. These Rankine–Hugoniot conditions determine the density, pressure and temperature jumps from the upstream Mach number and the equation of state.

A point explosion in a uniform medium also begins without an intrinsic length scale. Dimensional analysis of the explosion energy $E$, ambient density $\rho_0$ and time $t$ gives $R\propto(Et^2/\rho_0)^{1/5}$. The exponent $2/5$ follows before the internal similarity solution is calculated.

The Trinity photographs provide a direct test of this scaling. Taylor tabulated 25 fireball radii and times and compared them with the similarity prediction published during the war. Section 5 repeats the fit. The exponent agrees closely with $2/5$, while structured residuals reveal limitations of the simplest model.

Section 6 applies the jump conditions to Voyager 2 measurements of the solar-wind termination shock, using Neptune's bow shock as a control. Section 7 follows the evolution of a supernova remnant into its radiative phase. Section 8 discusses the Rayleigh–Taylor structure of Tycho's remnant and explains why a constant-acceleration planar mixing law cannot be transferred directly to an expanding shell.

2. The jump conditions

Everything in this module is a statement about the configuration defined next, and every hypothesis it carries is listed once here so that the propositions can name it rather than restate it.

Definition 1 (the shock). A surface across which $\rho$, $v$ and $p$ are discontinuous, of thickness negligible against every other length in the problem, moving through the gas at speed $D$. All quantities below are measured in the rest frame of the surface, in which the flow is steady and, locally, one-dimensional along the normal $\hat n$. Subscript $1$ denotes the state ahead of the surface (upstream) and subscript $2$ the state behind it (downstream), with $v_1, v_2 \gt 0$ the normal flow speeds in that frame, so the gas passes from side $1$ to side $2$. Far enough on each side of the surface the gas is uniform: $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}n = 0$ and $\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}n = 0$ there. That, and not the thickness itself, is what Proposition 1 uses. The gas is an ideal polytropic gas of ratio of specific heats $\gamma$ on both sides, with specific enthalpy $w = \gamma p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$.

The jump conditions assume a steady discontinuity, one polytropic gas, and one temperature on each side. Shock acceleration is negligible for the cases studied here. Trinity violates the constant-$\gamma$ approximation through dissociation, vibration, and radiation. Voyager’s termination shock violates the single-temperature assumption because pickup ions receive most of the dissipated energy.

Definition 2 (the upstream Mach number). $$ M_1 \;\equiv\; \frac{v_1}{c_1}, \qquad c_1 \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\gamma p_1}{\rho_1}}, \tag{2.1}$$ with $v_1$ the normal flow speed in the frame of the surface, which is the speed at which the shock advances into gas that is at rest in the laboratory. $c_1$ is the adiabatic sound speed of Module 4 Proposition 1, evaluated in the upstream gas. A shock is called strong when $M_1 \gg 1$; Problem D1 puts a number on how large that has to be.

Fig. 1 is the whole proof in a picture, and the question mark in the middle of it is the content of the proposition.

Proposition 1 (the Rankine–Hugoniot conditions). Across the surface of Definition 1, $$ \rho_1 v_1 \;=\; \rho_2 v_2, \tag{2.2}$$ $$ \rho_1 v_1^2 + p_1 \;=\; \rho_2 v_2^2 + p_2, \tag{2.3}$$ $$ w_1 + \tfrac12 v_1^2 \;=\; w_2 + \tfrac12 v_2^2, \qquad w = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{p}{\rho}. \tag{2.4}$$ No constitutive property of the interior enters: no value of the viscosity, the conductivity or the layer thickness appears in (2.2)–(2.4), and no equation of motion inside the layer is used.
The equations to integrate are the conservation forms of Module 2 — Module 2 Proposition 1 for mass, Proposition 2 for momentum and Proposition 5 for total energy — with the dissipative fluxes put back. They must be put back, because the layer is exactly the region where Module 1's Knudsen argument for dropping them fails. That failure is what makes the layer a shock. In the frame of Definition 1, where $\partial_t = 0$ and only the normal derivative survives, each equation reads $\mathrm{d}F/\mathrm{d}n = S$ with $$ F_{\rm mass} = \rho v, \qquad F_{\rm mom} = \rho v^2 + p - \sigma_{nn}, \qquad F_{\rm en} = \rho v\left(w + \tfrac12 v^2\right) - \sigma_{nn}v - q_n, $$ where $\sigma_{nn}$ is the normal viscous stress, $q_n$ the conductive heat flux along the normal, and $S$ collects the bounded volume sources — gravity in the momentum equation, radiative gain and loss per unit volume in the energy equation. Integrate each from $n = -\epsilon$ to $n = +\epsilon$, with $\epsilon$ chosen outside the layer on both sides, so that both endpoints lie in the uniform gas of Definition 1. Two things then happen, and they happen only because of where the endpoints were put. First, $\sigma_{nn}$ and $q_n$ are built from $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}n$ and $\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}n$, and both gradients vanish at the endpoints, so the dissipative terms drop out of $F(+\epsilon)$ and $F(-\epsilon)$ however large they are in between. Second, $\int_{-\epsilon}^{+\epsilon}S\,\mathrm{d}n \to 0$ as the layer thickness goes to zero, because $S$ is bounded. What survives is $F(+\epsilon) = F(-\epsilon)$ for each of the three, with every $F$ evaluated where its dissipative part is zero, and that is (2.2)–(2.4). Dividing the third by the first, which is legitimate because $\rho v \neq 0$ for a shock, turns the energy flux equality into the enthalpy form (2.4). ∎

The derivation requires conservation laws in divergence form so they can be integrated across the layer. It also requires uniform endpoint states and excludes finite surface losses of energy. The internal stress, heat flux, thickness, and equation of state need not be specified. Consequently, the jump conditions determine the endpoint states but contain no information about the transition structure.

Domain of the jump conditions. Proposition 1 applies to a non-magnetic gas. A magnetised shock requires additional jump conditions for the normal and tangential magnetic-field components, and magnetic terms enter the momentum flux. Section 6 compares only quantities that remain meaningful under the gas-dynamic approximation and identifies where an oblique-MHD treatment is required.

The jump conditions do not require a viscous model, conductive model, or local thermodynamic equilibrium inside the layer. That independence makes them powerful. Their limitation is equally clear: they determine only the states outside the shock. At the collisionless termination shock, the microscopic layer is far thinner than a collisional mean free path, so a one-temperature closure fails even though conservation still applies.

3. What the jumps say: a ceiling, a direction, and an escape

Proposition 1 supplies three equations for the downstream density, velocity, and pressure. Solving this system gives the compression, temperature, and entropy changes in terms of the upstream Mach number.

Proposition 2 (the jump ratios, and the ceiling on compression). For the gas of Definition 1, the non-trivial solution of (2.2)–(2.4) is $$ \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} \;=\; \frac{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}{(\gamma-1)M_1^2+2}, \qquad \frac{p_2}{p_1} \;=\; \frac{2\gamma M_1^2-(\gamma-1)}{\gamma+1}, \tag{3.1}$$ $$ \frac{T_2}{T_1} \;=\; \frac{p_2/p_1}{\rho_2/\rho_1}, \qquad M_2^2 \;=\; \frac{(\gamma-1)M_1^2+2}{2\gamma M_1^2-(\gamma-1)}. \tag{3.2}$$ As $M_1\to\infty$ the density ratio saturates at $$ \left.\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}\right|_{\max} \;=\; \frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}, \tag{3.3}$$ which is $4$ for $\gamma = 5/3$ and $6$ for $\gamma = 7/5$, while $p_2/p_1$ and $T_2/T_1$ grow without bound, both as $M_1^2$.
Write $r = \rho_2/\rho_1$. Mass conservation (2.2) gives $v_2 = v_1/r$. Substituting into the momentum condition (2.3), $$ p_2 - p_1 \;=\; \rho_1v_1^2\left(1-\frac1r\right), $$ and dividing by $p_1 = \rho_1c_1^2/\gamma$ turns it into $p_2/p_1 = 1 + \gamma M_1^2(1-1/r)$. Put that and $v_2 = v_1/r$ into the enthalpy condition (2.4), multiply through by $(\gamma-1)/c_1^2$, and the result is a quadratic in $r$ whose two roots are $r = 1$ — the trivial solution, no shock — and the first of (3.1). The pressure ratio follows by back-substitution, the temperature ratio from the ideal gas law $T \propto p/\rho$, and $M_2$ from $M_2^2 = v_2^2\rho_2/(\gamma p_2) = (v_1^2/r^2)(r\rho_1)/(\gamma p_2)$. Taking $M_1\to\infty$ in the first of (3.1) leaves $(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$, which is (3.3). ∎

Compression and heating behave differently at large Mach number. Pressure behind the front limits the density jump to $(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$, so compression remains finite. The temperature has no corresponding ceiling because additional kinetic energy becomes internal energy. Strong shocks therefore produce extremely hot gas without requiring an equally large density increase, as observed in X-ray-emitting supernova remnants.

The ceiling is approached quickly. At $\gamma = 5/3$, a Mach 3 shock has reached 75.00 per cent of it, Mach 5 gives 89.29 per cent, Mach 10 gives 97.09 per cent and Mach 30 gives 99.67 per cent. Problem D1 asks for the Mach number at which 99 per cent is reached and gets $M_1 = 17.2$ for $\gamma = 5/3$ and $22.2$ for $\gamma = 7/5$. The Trinity shock at one millisecond was at $M_1 = 43$ and was therefore strong by any measure.

3.1 Why there is no such thing as a rarefaction shock

The conservation laws also permit algebraic rarefaction jumps. For $M_1=0.7$ and $\gamma=5/3$, equation (3.1) gives $\rho_2/\rho_1=0.5616$ and $p_2/p_1=0.3625$. Such jumps conserve mass, momentum, and energy but violate the entropy condition. Thermodynamics therefore selects the compressive branch.

Proposition 3 (the entropy jump fixes the direction). Across the surface of Definition 1, the entropy change per particle is $$ \frac{\Delta s}{k_B} \;=\; \frac{1}{\gamma-1}\,\ln\!\left[\frac{p_2}{p_1}\left(\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\right)^{\!\gamma}\right], \tag{3.4}$$ with the ratios of (3.1). It is zero at $M_1 = 1$, positive for $M_1 \gt 1$ and negative for $M_1 \lt 1$. The second law therefore admits only $M_1 \gt 1$: a shock is always compressive, and always advances into its gas supersonically.
For an ideal gas the entropy per particle is $s = c_v\ln(p\rho^{-\gamma}) + \text{const}$ with $c_v = k_B/(\gamma-1)$, which gives (3.4) directly. Set $M_1 = 1+\epsilon$ and expand both ratios of (3.1) to third order in $\epsilon$. The first-order and second-order terms cancel identically in the combination (3.4), and what survives is $$ \frac{\Delta s}{k_B} \;=\; \frac{16\gamma}{3(\gamma+1)^2}\,\epsilon^3 + O(\epsilon^4), $$ an odd leading power. An odd leading power is the whole argument: $\Delta s$ changes sign with $\epsilon$, so the two branches are not symmetric and exactly one of them is admissible. Direct evaluation verifies the sign away from $M_1 = 1$: at $\gamma = 5/3$, $\Delta s/k_B = +0.00101$ at $M_1 = 1.1$, $+0.85031$ at $M_1 = 3$, and $-0.00159$ at $M_1 = 0.9$, $-1.21225$ at $M_1 = 0.5$. ∎

A second obstruction sits below the first, and it is worth separating because it is not thermodynamic at all. The pressure ratio in (3.1) vanishes at $M_1 = \sqrt{(\gamma-1)/2\gamma}$, which is $0.4472$ for $\gamma = 5/3$, and goes negative below it — at $M_1 = 0.4$ it returns $p_2/p_1 = -0.0500$. Below that Mach number the "rarefaction shock" is not a solution that the second law forbids. It is not a solution at all, because it would require a negative pressure. So the two objections are of different kinds and cover different ranges, and Problem C3 asks the reader to state both.

An expansion forms a rarefaction fan rather than a discontinuity. Its characteristics move at different speeds, so the fan broadens continuously with time. Proposition 3 excludes the discontinuous alternative through the entropy condition; a full characteristic derivation lies outside this module.

3.2 The escape: a shock that radiates

The ceiling (3.3) is a consequence of (2.4), and (2.4) assumed that no energy leaves the layer. Molecular gas radiates efficiently, and a dense shocked molecular cloud can lose its thermal energy far faster than the gas crosses the layer. Then the correct closure is not "no losses" but "the temperature does not change", and the ceiling disappears.

Proposition 4 (the isothermal shock). Replace (2.4) by $T_2 = T_1$, keeping (2.2) and (2.3). Then $$ \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} \;=\; \mathcal{M}^2, \qquad \mathcal{M} \;\equiv\; \frac{v_1}{c_T}, \quad c_T \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{\mu m_u}}, \tag{3.5}$$ and there is no upper bound on the compression.
With $T$ fixed, $p = \rho c_T^2$ on both sides. Mass conservation gives $v_2 = v_1\rho_1/\rho_2$, and momentum conservation becomes $$ \rho_1v_1^2 + \rho_1c_T^2 \;=\; \rho_2 v_2^2 + \rho_2c_T^2 \;=\; \frac{\rho_1^2v_1^2}{\rho_2} + \rho_2c_T^2. $$ Writing $r = \rho_2/\rho_1$ and dividing by $\rho_1c_T^2$ gives $\mathcal{M}^2 + 1 = \mathcal{M}^2/r + r$, whose roots are $r = 1$ and $r = \mathcal{M}^2$. ∎

The distinction is whether the shocked layer retains its heat or radiates it away. At $10$ K the rotational states of molecular hydrogen are largely frozen out because their characteristic temperature is about $85$ K. The gas then has three active translational degrees of freedom, so $\gamma=5/3$ is the appropriate ideal-gas value.

$\mathcal{M}$, the isothermal Mach number of (3.5), is written in a different typeface from $M_1$ deliberately. They are ratios to different speeds — $c_T$ is the isothermal sound speed, smaller than the adiabatic $c$ of Definition 2 by $\sqrt\gamma$ — so quoting a Mach number for a radiating shock without saying which one is quoted is an error of a factor $1.29$ at $\gamma = 5/3$. This book uses $M$ for adiabatic and $\mathcal{M}$ for isothermal throughout this module and nowhere else.

4. The Sedov–Taylor blast wave

Deposit energy $E$ at a point at $t = 0$, in a uniform gas of density $\rho_0$ and negligible pressure, and follow the shock that runs outward. The problem is the cleanest use in this book of an argument that needs no equations at all.

Proposition 5 (the exponent, from dimensions alone). If the flow depends on no quantity other than $E$, $\rho_0$ and $t$, then the shock radius is $$ R(t) \;=\; \xi_0\left(\frac{E\,t^2}{\rho_0}\right)^{1/5}, \tag{4.1}$$ with $\xi_0$ a dimensionless number. The exponent $2/5$ is forced, and no property of the gas enters it.
In the dimensions $[\text{mass}]$, $[\text{length}]$, $[\text{time}]$, $E$ has dimensions $\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}$, $\rho_0$ has $\mathrm{M\,L^{-3}}$ and $t$ has $\mathrm{T}$. Seek $R = E^a\rho_0^b t^c$ with $\mathrm{L}$ on the left: matching mass gives $a + b = 0$; matching time gives $-2a + c = 0$; matching length gives $2a - 3b = 1$. The first and third give $a = 1/5$, $b = -1/5$, and the second then gives $c = 2/5$. The solution is unique, so (4.1) is the only possible form. ∎

Three consequences follow immediately and none needs the interior solution. The shock speed is $v_s = \mathrm{d}R/\mathrm{d}t = \tfrac25 R/t \propto t^{-3/5}$, so the blast decelerates. By Proposition 7 below, which uses nothing from this section, the post-shock temperature goes as $v_s^2 \propto t^{-6/5}$. And, because $R^5t^{-2} = \xi_0^5E/\rho_0$ is a constant of the motion, a single measurement of $R$ at a single $t$ determines $E$ once $\xi_0$ and $\rho_0$ are known. Taylor used exactly that, and Problem K3 shows what it hides.

What dimensional analysis cannot supply is $\xi_0$. That number contains all the dynamics, and getting it requires solving the interior flow.

Proposition 6 (similarity solution and $\xi_0$). Define $\lambda=r/R(t)$ and write $$ v(r,t) = \frac{r}{t}\,u(\lambda), \qquad \rho(r,t) = \rho_0\,g(\lambda), \qquad p(r,t) = \rho_0\frac{r^2}{t^2}\,\Pi(\lambda). \tag{4.2}$$ The spherical Euler equations then reduce to three ordinary differential equations in $\ln\lambda$. The strong-shock boundary values at $\lambda=1$ are $$ u(1) = \frac{4}{5(\gamma+1)}, \qquad g(1) = \frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}, \qquad \Pi(1) = \frac{8}{25(\gamma+1)}, \tag{4.3}$$ Energy conservation fixes the remaining constant through $$ 1 \;=\; 4\pi\,\xi_0^5\,J, \qquad J \;=\; \int_0^1 \lambda^4\left[\tfrac12 g u^2 + \frac{\Pi}{\gamma-1}\right]\mathrm{d}\lambda. \tag{4.4}$$ Numerically, $\xi_0=1.151666$ for $\gamma=5/3$ and $1.032777$ for $\gamma=7/5$.
Substitute (4.2) into continuity, Euler and the entropy equation, using $\partial\lambda/\partial t = -\tfrac25\lambda/t$ and $\partial\lambda/\partial r = \lambda/r$, which follow from $R \propto t^{2/5}$. Writing $'$ for $\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\ln\lambda$ and $a \equiv u - \tfrac25$, every factor of $r$ and $t$ cancels and the three equations become $$ a\,\frac{g'}{g} + 3u + u' = 0, \qquad a\,u' + u^2 - u + \frac{\Pi}{g}\left(2 + \frac{\Pi'}{\Pi}\right) = 0, \qquad a\left(\frac{\Pi'}{\Pi} - \gamma\frac{g'}{g}\right) + 2(u-1) = 0. $$ That every factor cancels is the content of the similarity hypothesis, and it is a check on (4.2): had the substitution left an explicit $t$, no solution of this form would exist. Eliminating $g'/g$ and $\Pi'/\Pi$ from the middle equation gives $u'$ explicitly, $$ u'\left(a^2 - \gamma\frac{\Pi}{g}\right) \;=\; a\left(u - u^2\right) + \frac{\Pi}{g}\left(3\gamma u - \tfrac65\right), $$ and the system is integrated inward from $\lambda = 1$ with (4.3).

For (4.3): in the strong-shock limit Proposition 2 gives $\rho_2 = \rho_0(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$, $v_2 = D(\gamma-1)/(\gamma+1)$ and $p_2 = 2\rho_0D^2/(\gamma+1)$, with $D = \mathrm{d}R/\mathrm{d}t = \tfrac25R/t$ supplied by (4.1) itself. Change frame before using $v_2$. Definition 1 measures $v_2$ in the rest frame of the surface, while $v(r,t)$ in (4.2) is a laboratory-frame velocity, in the frame where the unshocked gas ahead is at rest. The two differ by $D$: the shocked gas moves outward at $v_{2,\rm lab} = D - v_2 = D[1 - (\gamma-1)/(\gamma+1)] = 2D/(\gamma+1)$, which is $D/2$ at $\gamma = 5/3$ while $v_2$ itself is $D/4$. Evaluating (4.2) at $r = R$ and dividing gives $u(1) = v_{2,\rm lab}\,t/R = \tfrac25\cdot 2/(\gamma+1)$, $g(1) = \rho_2/\rho_0$ and $\Pi(1) = p_2/[\rho_0(R/t)^2] = 2(4/25)/(\gamma+1)$, which are (4.3). So §4 introduces no new physical input: the boundary condition of the blast wave is Proposition 2 plus similarity.

For (4.4): the total energy inside the shock is $$ E = \int_0^R\left[\tfrac12\rho v^2 + \frac{p}{\gamma-1}\right]4\pi r^2\,\mathrm{d}r = 4\pi\rho_0\frac{R^5}{t^2}\,J $$ after substituting (4.2) and $r = \lambda R$. But $R^5/t^2 = \xi_0^5E/\rho_0$ by (4.1), so $E$ cancels from both sides and what is left is $1 = 4\pi\xi_0^5J$. The energy integral does not determine the energy. It determines the constant. ∎

The integration is done in $\ln\lambda$ rather than $\lambda$ because $g \to 0$ and $\Pi \to \infty$ at the centre as pure powers of $\lambda$, so their logarithms are linear and the integration stays well conditioned. It is stopped at $\lambda = 9.1\times10^{-4}$ rather than at $\lambda = 0$, and nothing is lost: the integrand of (4.4) carries $\lambda^4$, so everything inside $\lambda = 10^{-3}$ contributes less than $10^{-15}$ of the total, and $\xi_0$ is unchanged in its eighth digit whether the integration stops at $6.7\times10^{-3}$ or at $3.4\times10^{-4}$. The reason a stopping point is needed at all is that the system has a fixed point at $u = (2/5)/\gamma$ towards which the physical solution is unstable going inward, so rounding error overwhelms it below about $\lambda = 10^{-4}$.

Two independent verifications are worth stating, because a constant obtained by numerical integration of an eliminated system is exactly the sort of number that can be wrong without looking wrong.

The numerical Sedov solution satisfies equations that were not used to construct the reduced ODE. Substituting numerical derivatives into the discarded continuity and entropy equations gives residuals below $7\times10^{-9}$ for $0.1\le\lambda\le0.9$. This checks the algebra independently of integrator convergence.

Published similarity constants provide an external check. For $\gamma=5/3$, Tang and Chevalier quote $2.026$ in $R=(2.026Et^2/\rho)^{1/5}$, equivalent to $\xi_0=1.151670$. The present result is $1.151666$. For $\gamma=1.4$, the Kamm–Timmes standard problem implies $\xi_0=1.032777$, matching all six reported figures.

A third comparison, and the one that matters for §5. Taylor obtained $K \equiv 1/\xi_0^5$ for $\gamma = 1.40$ in 1941 by hand integration and printed $K = 0.856$. The exact value is $0.851072$. His approximate quadratures were therefore good to 0.58 per cent — and because $E$ is linear in $K$, that error propagates to his energy as itself, which is small compared with everything else that will go wrong in §5.

The Sedov solution places most swept-up mass near the shock. For $\gamma=5/3$, half the mass lies outside $0.9392R$, and the density at $0.5R$ is only 1.53 per cent of its post-shock value. Pressure approaches a nearly uniform interior plateau at 30.6 per cent of the post-shock pressure. The remnant is therefore a hot, dilute interior driving a thin dense shell.

5. The anchor: Trinity, 16 July 1945

Every module in this book is checked against a published measurement, and the checks have been of uneven quality. Module 3's solar comparison was partly circular, because the model it tested had gone into the model that produced the "measurement". Module 7's laboratory check carried a standard deviation but its astronomical half carried none. This module's anchor has neither defect, and it is worth saying why before any number appears.

Taylor's Table 1 is twenty-five radii read off photographic film with a length scale in the frame, against twenty-five shutter times read off the same film. Nothing about the blast-wave solution went into producing those numbers. No one solved (4.1) to obtain them and no fitted model stands between the film and the table. So the table can test the theory, which is precisely what Module 3's solar model could not do. The prediction, moreover, was published in 1941 — four years before the explosion, nine years before Taylor saw the film — so there was no opportunity to tune it.

The weaknesses are worth stating with equal directness. The radii are read by eye from 1945 photographs and carry no quoted uncertainty. The times come from the camera. Taylor labels every row with the source of its photograph, his "authority" column, in four groups — A, rows 1–14, a strip of small images from MDDC-221. B, rows 15–20, declassified photographs lent by the Ministry of Supply. C, rows 21–23, a second strip of small images from MDDC-221. D, rows 24–25, large single photographs from MDDC-221 — and the source turns out to matter. The reader is asked to believe a table transcribed from a 1950 letterpress page; §5.1 closes that gap with a check of the data file against the paper's own logarithm columns.

The mean intercept is $11.9159$, compared with Taylor's $11.915$. These checks establish the data transcription; they do not test the blast-wave model.

6. The astrophysical check: Voyager 2 inside a shock

Voyager 2 crossed the solar-wind termination shock repeatedly between 30 August and 1 September 2007 at about 84 au. Its plasma instrument recorded the upstream deceleration and downstream state directly, providing an in situ test rather than a remote inference.

The paper reporting it supplies something rarer than the crossing itself: a control. The same instrument on the same spacecraft crossed Neptune's inbound bow shock in August 1989, eighteen years earlier, at 30 AU. So the comparison below is not merely theory against observation. It is one shock against another, through one instrument, with the theory saying what should differ and what should not.

6.1 The strong-shock compression ceiling at Neptune

At Neptune’s bow shock, the measured speed and density change by factors of four in opposite directions, while temperature rises by about a factor of 100. These values provide independent consistency checks on the strong-shock limits.

Figure 4 places all five comparisons on a common logarithmic axis as measured divided by predicted. Ratios near unity indicate agreement, while their distance from unity displays the magnitude and direction of each discrepancy.

Equation (2.2) requires the density jump to equal the inverse velocity jump in the shock frame. The measured ratios agree within the quoted precision. A planetary bow shock is nearly stationary in the frame used for these spacecraft measurements, so the observed speeds provide the required shock-frame comparison.

The temperature supplies a second, independent test of the same shock. A temperature ratio of 100 implies, by inverting (3.2) at $\gamma = 5/3$, an upstream Mach number $M_1 = 17.8$. That Mach number then predicts a density ratio of $3.96$ through the first of (3.1), against the measured 4. Consistency ratio $0.99$. One Mach number, two independent Rankine–Hugoniot relations, one answer: that is the shape of a real test, and it is what Proposition 2 is for.

Check 4, Agreement with the prediction. At Neptune's bow shock the measured density jump is the compression ceiling $(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1) = 4$ for a monatomic gas, with $\gamma$ fixed by the gas and not fitted. The measured speed drop is the same factor, as (2.2) requires; and the measured temperature jump, run back through (3.2) to a Mach number and forward again through (3.1), returns the density jump to $0.99$. Every input is read to one significant figure from a figure caption that quotes no uncertainty, so these ratios are claimed to two figures and no further.
The caption gives no uncertainty on any of the three factors, and the Neptune curves are normalised before comparison — speed divided by 1.3, density by five, temperature by two — so that the two shocks can be overlaid. The numbers are therefore read to one or two significant figures, and nothing finer is claimed from them. This is the same weakness Module 7's solar anchor had, declared here for the same reason.

6.2 Failure of the one-temperature closure

At the termination shock, density rises only by about two and the thermal-ion temperature by about ten. A single-fluid conversion of the lost bulk energy would instead predict roughly $10^6$ K, compared with the measured $10^5$ K.

That $10^6$ K is not taken on trust; Proposition 7 reproduces it. The same paper reports the solar wind decelerating "from 400 to 300 km s$^{-1}$ in three steps" ahead of the crossing. Putting $v_s = 300\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and $\mu = 0.5$, for a fully ionised hydrogen plasma, into (7.1) at $\gamma = 5/3$ gives $T_2 = 1.01\times10^{6}$ K, which is their figure to within 2 per cent. So Check 5 below is this book's prediction against their measurement and not their prediction against their measurement.

Reference-frame dependence. The quoted $300\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ speed is measured in the spacecraft frame, while equation (7.1) requires the upstream speed in the shock frame. Subtracting the measured $94.0\pm3.4\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ shock speed gives $206\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and $T_2=4.8\times10^5$ K. The observed $10^5$ K remains lower by a factor of $4.8$, compared with a factor of ten in the spacecraft-frame calculation.
Check 5, Disagreement with the prediction. Observed over predicted, $10^5/10^6 = \mathbf{0.10}$: a factor of ten short. What fails is the closure — one fluid, one temperature — and not the conservation laws of Proposition 1.

Approximately 80 per cent of the proton bulk energy is transferred to pickup ions rather than to the measured thermal population. A single-temperature fluid has no variable for this separate ion component. The solar wind is collisionless at 84 au, so collisions cannot rapidly equilibrate the two populations. The observation therefore rejects the one-temperature closure while preserving the conservation laws.

6.3 The comparison this module refuses to make

For the second crossing, the fitted compression ratio is $2.38\pm0.14$ and the fitted upstream fast-mode Mach number is $4.9\pm0.1$. Gas dynamics would predict 3.556. Although the nominal discrepancy is $8.4\sigma$, it is not an independent test because both quantities come from the same shock fit.

The shock orientation is chosen to minimise Rankine–Hugoniot residuals, after which the speed, field angle, and Mach numbers are derived. Compression and Mach number are therefore correlated outputs of one fit. Substituting one into the same jump relation tests internal fit consistency rather than the physical law independently.

The shock is also quasi-perpendicular: the measured angle between its normal and the magnetic field is $82.8^\circ\pm3.9^\circ$. Equation (3.1) is gas-dynamic and omits magnetic pressure and tension. A quantitative Voyager comparison therefore requires the oblique-MHD jump conditions, which lie beyond the theory developed here.

The temperature comparison of §6.2 has neither defect. The observed $10^5$ K and the predicted $10^6$ K are separate statements: the first is a measurement, the second is what full thermalisation of the measured flow energy would give, and no fit connects them.

7. A supernova remnant through its phases

The Sedov solution is exact for a point explosion in a pressureless uniform gas, and a supernova remnant is neither at the start nor at the end of its life. This section puts brackets around the interval in which (4.1) is the right description, and the brackets are as much a result as the solution is.

Adopt explosion energy $10^{51}$ erg and ambient hydrogen density $1\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. Including helium gives $\rho_0=2.325\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$. For the ionised post-shock gas, take $\mu=0.61$ and $\gamma=5/3$.

Proposition 7 (the post-shock temperature of a strong shock). In the limit $M_1 \gg 1$, $$ T_2 \;=\; \frac{2(\gamma-1)}{(\gamma+1)^2}\,\frac{\mu m_u v_s^2}{k_B}, \tag{7.1}$$ which for $\gamma = 5/3$ is $T_2 = 3\mu m_u v_s^2/(16 k_B)$.
In the strong-shock limit Proposition 2 gives $p_2 = 2\rho_1v_s^2/(\gamma+1)$ and $\rho_2 = \rho_1(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$. The ideal gas law in the form $T = \mu m_u p/(k_B\rho)$ then gives $$ T_2 = \frac{\mu m_u}{k_B}\frac{p_2}{\rho_2} = \frac{\mu m_u}{k_B}\cdot\frac{2\rho_1v_s^2}{\gamma+1}\cdot\frac{\gamma-1}{\rho_1(\gamma+1)}, $$ which is (7.1). At $\gamma = 5/3$ the prefactor is $2(2/3)/(64/9) = 3/16$. ∎
$\mu$ must be stated with every answer from (7.1), and the book's convention from Module 6 — $\mu$ measured against $m_u$ — is used here. Bremsstrahlung alone is not an adequate cooling law. Omitting line cooling delays the transition to $3.32\times10^5$ yr, a factor of eight. Line emission dominates below $10^7$ K, where the remnant spends most of its evolution. The simplified cooling law therefore changes the phase structure, not merely a small numerical correction.

7.1 What the transition criterion is worth

The transition criterion is an order-of-magnitude estimate rather than a hydrodynamic model. Cioffi, McKee, and Bertschinger (1988) calculated the radiative transition numerically. For solar abundance and $E_{51}=n_0=1$, their equations (3.33a) and (3.33b) give

$$ R_{\rm PDS} = 14.0\ \mathrm{pc}, \qquad v_{\rm PDS} = 413\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}, \qquad t_{\rm PDS} = \tfrac25 R_{\rm PDS}/v_{\rm PDS} = 1.326\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{yr}. $$ $t_{\rm PDS}$ above is derived from their two fitted quantities using $R = \tfrac25v_st$, which holds at the end of the Sedov stage. It can be checked against the same paper by a second route: their equation (3.10) gives a shell-formation time $t_{\rm sf} = 3.61\times10^4$ yr for these parameters, and their equation (3.11) sets $t_{\rm PDS} = t_{\rm sf}/e$, which is $1.328\times10^4$ yr. The two routes agree to 0.15 per cent.

Comparing:

Calibration against a radiative-hydrodynamic model. The simple criterion gives $R_{\rm rad}/R_{\rm PDS}=1.573$ and $t_{\rm rad}/t_{\rm PDS}=3.081$. The radius is more accurate because $R\propto t^{2/5}$ compresses a factor of $3.08$ in time to a factor of $1.57$ in radius. These ratios quantify the accuracy that should be assigned to the one-line estimate.
The two calculations do not use the same cooling function. Cioffi et al. use $\Lambda = 1.6\times10^{-19}\,\zeta_m T^{-1/2}$ for $10^5 \lt T \lt 10^{7.5}$ K. This section uses the $T^{-0.7}$ branch of (7.2). So the ratios 1.573 and 3.081 test the criterion and the cooling curve together, and neither can be blamed separately on this evidence.

After $t_{\rm rad}$ the shell is thin, dense and driven by the pressure of the hot interior — the interior that Proposition 6 showed is nearly empty and nearly uniform in pressure. Then $R \propto t^{0.30}$ rather than $t^{0.40}$, and the similarity solution of §4 no longer applies. That phase is not derived in this book.

8. The limitation from Module 7: Rayleigh–Taylor at Tycho

Module 7 derived the history-dependent Rayleigh–Taylor width but lacked an astronomical comparison. Tycho's supernova remnant supplies one because its forward and reverse shock radii constrain the deceleration history. The comparison tests the shell model rather than the laboratory coefficient alone.

A young supernova remnant is not one shock but three surfaces. The ejecta drive a forward shock (the blast wave) into the interstellar medium. The ambient gas pushes back on the ejecta through a reverse shock running into them. Between the two lies the contact discontinuity, separating shocked ejecta from shocked ambient gas. Shocked ejecta are the heavier fluid and sit inside; shocked ambient gas is lighter and sits outside; and the shell decelerates. In the frame of the decelerating interface that is exactly Module 7's unstable ordering, and the contact discontinuity grows Rayleigh–Taylor fingers.

8.1 Why the acceleration history matters

The planar relation $h=\alpha Agt^2$ used in Module 7 assumes constant acceleration. A self-similar shell with $R=Ct^m$ has $g(t)=m(1-m)Ct^{m-2}$, so substituting the instantaneous value of $g$ into the constant-acceleration formula does not give a bound on the accumulated width.

For comparison, the history-dependent Read prescription quoted in Module 7 gives

$$ \frac{h}{R}=\frac{4\alpha A(1-m)}{m}\left[1-\left(\frac{t_0}{t}\right)^{m/2}\right]^2,\qquad 0

Here the integration begins at $t_0$. The result differs from $\alpha A m(1-m)$ and depends explicitly on the onset time. At $m=1/2$ and $t_0=0$, the two expressions differ by a factor of 16. A quantitative shell model must therefore retain the acceleration history, curvature, and density evolution.

8.2 What the Tycho comparison can establish

The published radii remain informative. A one-dimensional hydrodynamic calculation places the contact discontinuity near $0.77R_{\rm BW}$, a two-dimensional calculation places Rayleigh–Taylor fingers near $0.85R_{\rm BW}$, and the reported mean contact discontinuity in Tycho is near $0.93R_{\rm BW}$. These positions show that the real or simulated shell contains structure absent from the smooth one-dimensional profile.

Check 7 — no quantitative transfer test is established. The constant-acceleration plane-interface law cannot be converted into a shell-width upper bound by inserting the shell's instantaneous deceleration. The observed widths therefore cannot be used here to reject that law by a factor of eleven. A quantitative comparison requires an expanding-shell model that specifies the acceleration history, curvature, density evolution, bubble-versus-spike coefficient and onset time.

8.3 Physical complications that a shell model must include

Several effects can change the interface position or apparent width: dense ejecta clumps retain information about the initial conditions. Spikes and bubbles need not share one coefficient at large Atwood number. Projection and threshold choices alter the inferred radii. Cosmic-ray acceleration can move the blast wave relative to the contact discontinuity. These effects explain why a planar coefficient cannot be transferred without a dynamical shell calculation. They do not, by themselves, identify a unique corrected coefficient.

The quoted radii and caveats are retained from Wang & Chevalier (2001) and Warren et al. (2005). The former comparison is descriptive. No significance or factor-of-eleven rejection is assigned.

9. What this model captures, and what it misses

Every idealisation used above is named here, with the module that addresses it where one exists and a plain statement of "not addressed in this book" where none does.

  • Zero thickness. The layer has structure — viscous stress, heat conduction, ionisation — and none of it appears in Proposition 1. Problem D2 bounds the error in air at eight orders of magnitude and finds the opposite conclusion at the termination shock, where the layer is $10^5$–$10^6$ times thinner than a collisional mean free path. Not addressed: this book never solves the internal structure of a shock.
  • One fluid and one temperature. The termination-shock measurement is inconsistent with this closure. Electrons, thermal ions, and pickup ions need not equilibrate, and pickup ions carry about 80 per cent of the measured energy. A multi-population shock model is required.
  • Constant $\gamma$ across the front. Broken at Trinity, by Taylor's own account and in the direction his own argument predicts. Not addressed: a variable-$\gamma$ equation of state is beyond this book.
  • No magnetic field. Proposition 1 has no field, and §6.3 refuses a comparison for exactly that reason. Module 12 builds magnetohydrodynamics but stops short of oblique shock jump conditions, so this one is not addressed either, and the refusal in §6.3 is permanent rather than provisional.
  • The Sedov solution assumes an instantaneous point explosion in a uniform medium with negligible ambient pressure. Finite source size and radiative energy appear in the earliest Trinity data, while ambient pressure matters after several milliseconds. Circumstellar winds also violate uniform density and require a profile $\rho\propto r^{-s}$.
  • No radiative losses during the Sedov phase. That is precisely the assumption whose failure defines the end of the phase, in Proposition 8, and it is handled by bracketing rather than by correction.
  • The cooling model in equation (7.2) is a two-power-law approximation rather than a calculated ionisation-equilibrium curve. It predicts a transition radius 57 per cent too large and a transition time 3.1 times too late. These errors quantify the cost of the adopted cooling function.
  • Spherical symmetry. Dropped explicitly in §8, where the interface is unstable and the fingers are the subject. Everywhere else in the module it is assumed and never tested.
  • The plane-interface mixing law. Section 8 shows that the attempted instantaneous-acceleration substitution is invalid. No quantitative transfer test is established for an expanding supernova shell.

The Trinity comparison uses a directly recorded radius-time table, and the Voyager comparison uses in-situ measurements. Neither test is circular in the sense discussed in Module 3. Their principal limitation is the sparse uncertainty information, especially for the Trinity images. Selby's quoted standard deviation is therefore only one component of the yield uncertainty.

C3. Compute the entropy jump at $M_1 = 3$ for both $\gamma = 5/3$ and $\gamma = 7/5$. Then evaluate the same formulae at $M_1 = 0.9$, $0.7$ and $0.5$, and give the two separate reasons a rarefaction shock does not exist.
Solution

At $M_1 = 3$: $\gamma = 5/3$ gives $\rho_2/\rho_1 = 3.0000$, $p_2/p_1 = 11.0000$ and $\Delta s/k_B = 0.8503$; $\gamma = 7/5$ gives $3.8571$, $10.3333$ and $1.1137$.

Reason one, thermodynamic. Every one of those entropy jumps is negative, so every one is forbidden by the second law. This applies on the whole range $M_1 \lt 1$.

Reason two, and it is not thermodynamic. Below $M_1 = \sqrt{(\gamma-1)/2\gamma} = 0.4472$ for $\gamma = 5/3$, the formula for $p_2/p_1$ returns a negative pressure — $-0.0500$ at $M_1 = 0.4$. For $\gamma = 7/5$: $M_1 = 22.2$, where it is $5.9400$ against $6.0000$. The diatomic gas needs a stronger shock because its ceiling is further away in ratio terms.

Trinity at 1 ms: from Taylor's table $R \approx 38\ \mathrm{m}$, so $v_s = \tfrac25R/t = 14.4\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. The ambient sound speed in air at one atmosphere with Taylor's $\rho_0$ is $0.337\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, giving $M_1 = 43$. Strong by any measure, and comfortably past the 22.2 that $\gamma = 7/5$ requires.

D2. An ideal-gas shock has no thickness at all in the equations of Module 2, because those equations contain no length. Estimate the real thickness two ways — in sea-level air, and at the solar-wind termination shock — and say what each answer means for Definition 1.
Solution

Air. Adding viscosity gives the layer a width of a few mean free paths. In sea-level air $\lambda_{\rm mfp} \sim 6.5\times10^{-6}$ cm, so the layer is $\sim 2\times10^{-5}$ cm. The Trinity fireball at 1 ms had a radius of 38 m $= 3.8\times10^3$ cm, so the layer is $2\times10^{8}$ times thinner than the object it bounds. Definition 1 is safe by eight orders of magnitude.

The measured termination-shock width is $10^{10}$–$3\times10^{10}$ cm. For $n\approx2\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $T\approx10^5$ K, and $\ln\Lambda=25$, the proton Coulomb mean free path is $2.28\times10^{16}$ cm, or 1524 au.

The shock is roughly $8\times10^5$–$2\times10^6$ times thinner than a collisional mean free path. Electromagnetic collective processes, rather than binary collisions, mediate the transition. Conservation laws still balance the fluxes, but collisions cannot impose a common ion temperature.

D3. Using Proposition 6, find the radius inside which half the shocked mass lies, and describe the pressure in the interior. Do it for both values of $\gamma$.
Solution

Integrate $\lambda^2 g(\lambda)$ outward and find where it reaches half its total. For $\gamma = 5/3$ the mass median is at $r/R = 0.9392$; for $\gamma = 7/5$ at $r/R = 0.9581$. Nearly all the mass is in a thin shell just behind the front.

The interior is correspondingly empty. For $\gamma = 5/3$, $\rho/\rho_2$ is $0.3563$ at $r/R = 0.9$, $0.01531$ at $0.5$ and $3.4\times10^{-10}$ at $0.01$; for $\gamma = 7/5$, $0.2054$, $0.00172$ and $3.1\times10^{-16}$.

The pressure does the opposite: $p/p_2$ is $0.3098$ at $r/R = 0.5$ and $0.3062$ at $r/R = 0.01$ for $\gamma = 5/3$ — flat. That is what it must be. A region with almost no mass in it cannot support a pressure gradient, because there is nothing for the gradient to accelerate. The snowplough phase of §7 is the limit of this picture: a thin dense shell driven by a uniform interior pressure.
