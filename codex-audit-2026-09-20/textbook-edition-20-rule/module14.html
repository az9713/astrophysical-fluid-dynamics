14. Numerical Methods

Numerical methods approximate fluid equations on a finite grid or with particles. This chapter derives their stability and truncation limits, then tests both approaches against shock-tube and blast-wave solutions.

A simulation result depends on the equations, the discretisation, and the resolution. A method may conserve mass and energy while smearing a contact or shock. We first ask what conservation guarantees, then examine how a finite time step and grid spacing alter the computed solution. The Sod and Sedov problems provide quantitative tests.

The chapter also distinguishes calculations from observations. Its final census identifies results in earlier modules that came from simulations, numerical models, or numerical integration. That provenance changes what a comparison can establish.

The examples here use one-dimensional grid and particle methods, with a spherical-grid Sedov calculation. They illustrate stability, convergence, and resolution requirements. Higher-order reconstruction, multidimensional flow, gravity, magnetic fields, and radiation require additional numerical techniques.

1. What a code solves

The Euler equations conserve mass, momentum, and energy. A finite-volume method applies those balances to cells rather than point values. Its update is determined by fluxes through cell faces, so neighbouring cells exchange equal and opposite amounts. This construction matters most when the solution contains a shock.

Definition 1 (a conservative finite-volume scheme). Divide the line into cells of width $\Delta x$ centred on $x_i = (i + \tfrac12)\Delta x$. Let $U_i^n$ be the average of $U$ over cell $i$ at time $t_n = n\Delta t$. A conservative scheme updates it by $$ U_i^{n+1} \;=\; U_i^n - \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(F_{i+1/2} - F_{i-1/2}\right), \tag{1.1}$$ where the numerical flux $F_{i+1/2}$ depends on the cell averages on either side of the face between cells $i$ and $i+1$. The Courant number of a step is $C_{\rm CFL} = S_{\max}\Delta t/\Delta x$, with $S_{\max}$ the largest signal speed $|u| + c_{\rm s}$ on the grid.
Proposition 1 (conservation and the weak solution). (a) Update (1.1) conserves $\sum_iU_i\Delta x$ apart from flux through the grid ends. (b) If a consistent conservative scheme converges boundedly and almost everywhere as $\Delta x,\Delta t\to0$, its limit is a weak solution of the conservation law. Its shocks therefore satisfy the jump conditions of Module 8. We derive (a) below and state (b), the Lax–Wendroff theorem, with its convergence hypothesis explicit.
(a) Sum (1.1) over $i = 0, \dots, N-1$ and multiply by $\Delta x$. Every interior face flux appears twice, once with each sign, so $\sum_i U_i^{n+1}\Delta x = \sum_i U_i^{n}\Delta x - \Delta t\,(F_{N-1/2} - F_{-1/2})$. What is stated. Part (b) is the Lax–Wendroff theorem. Its hypothesis is the convergence itself: the theorem says what a convergent conservative scheme converges to, and nothing about whether it converges. §2 supplies one necessary condition for convergence. ∎

Conservation is necessary for a numerical shock to approach the correct weak solution. It does not guarantee convergence, stability, or small error on a finite grid. The Lax–Wendroff theorem identifies the weak solution if a consistent conservative scheme converges under its stated conditions; it does not prove that a chosen scheme will converge.

2. The Courant condition

Constant-speed advection isolates the time-step question. Its exact solution translates the initial profile by $at$, where $a>0$. The upwind and centred schemes below use the same neighbouring cells but behave differently. The upwind update is $$ u_i^{n+1} = u_i^n - C\,(u_i^n - u_{i-1}^n), \qquad C = \frac{a\Delta t}{\Delta x}. \tag{2.1}$$ Comparing the methods separates the necessary domain-of-dependence condition from stability of a particular update.

Proposition 2 (the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition). An explicit scheme in which $u_i^{n+1}$ depends only on $u_{i-1}^n$, $u_i^n$ and $u_{i+1}^n$ cannot converge to the solution of $\partial_t u + a\,\partial_x u = 0$ for all initial data unless $$ C = \frac{|a|\,\Delta t}{\Delta x} \;\le\; 1. \tag{2.2}$$ The condition is necessary and not sufficient.
After $n$ steps, $u_i^n$ depends on the initial data only through the values on the cells $i-n$ to $i+n$, which cover the interval $[x_i - n\Delta x,\, x_i + n\Delta x]$: the numerical domain of dependence. The exact solution at the same point and time is $u_0(x_i - a t_n)$ with $t_n = n\Delta t$, so it depends on the single point $x_i - an\Delta t$. If $|a|\Delta t \gt \Delta x$, that point lies outside the numerical domain of dependence. Change $u_0$ in a small neighbourhood of that point and nowhere else. The exact solution at $(x_i, t_n)$ changes; the numerical one does not. Refine $\Delta x$ and $\Delta t$ at fixed ratio: the same argument holds at every refinement, so the numerical solution cannot converge to the exact one for both sets of data. ∎

Courant, Friedrichs, and Lewy showed that the numerical domain of dependence must contain the physical one for convergence. For the local stencil in Proposition 2 this requires $a\Delta t/\Delta x\le1$. The condition is necessary, not sufficient: a scheme may satisfy it and still amplify numerical disturbances.

Proposition 3 (von Neumann stability of the two schemes). Substitute a single Fourier mode, $u_i^n = G(\theta)^n e^{\mathrm{i}i\theta}$, with $\theta = k\Delta x \in [0, \pi]$ and $G(\theta)$ the amplification factor per step. Then $$ |G_{\rm up}(\theta)|^{2} = 1 - 2C(1-C)(1-\cos\theta), \qquad |G_{\rm FTCS}(\theta)|^{2} = 1 + C^{2}\sin^{2}\theta. \tag{2.3}$$ The upwind scheme is stable, $|G| \le 1$ for every $\theta$, if and only if $0 \le C \le 1$, and at $\theta = \pi$, $|G(\pi)| = |1 - 2C|$: the most damped mode when $0 \le C \le 1$, and the fastest-growing mode when $C \gt 1$. FTCS is unstable for every $C \gt 0$, including every $C$ that satisfies (2.2).
For upwind, $G = 1 - C(1 - e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}) = (1 - C + C\cos\theta) - \mathrm{i}C\sin\theta$. Then $|G|^{2} = (1-C)^{2} + 2C(1-C)\cos\theta + C^{2}\cos^{2}\theta + C^{2}\sin^{2}\theta = (1-C)^{2} + C^{2} + 2C(1-C)\cos\theta$, and since $(1-C)^{2} + C^{2} = 1 - 2C(1-C)$ this is $1 - 2C(1-C)(1-\cos\theta)$. For $0 \le C \le 1$ the product $C(1-C)$ is non-negative and $1 - \cos\theta \ge 0$, so $|G|^2 \le 1$. For $C \gt 1$ or $C \lt 0$ the product is negative and $|G|^2 \gt 1$ at every $\theta \ne 0$; at $\theta = \pi$, $G = 1 - 2C$. For FTCS, $G = 1 - \tfrac{C}{2}(e^{\mathrm{i}\theta} - e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}) = 1 - \mathrm{i}C\sin\theta$, so $|G|^{2} = 1 + C^{2}\sin^{2}\theta \gt 1$ for every $C \gt 0$ and every $\theta \in (0,\pi)$. ∎

At $C=1$, upwinding shifts every value exactly one cell per step and reproduces constant-speed advection on the grid. At $C=0$, no time elapses. For $0<C<1$, the scheme is stable but spreads a sharp profile. The centred FTCS scheme instead amplifies nonconstant Fourier modes for every positive $C$.

CHECK 1 — stability in a numerical example. For a sawtooth mode, the upwind amplification factor is $|1-2C|$: it is $0.8$ at $C=0.9$ and $1.2$ at $C=1.1$. A sampled Gaussian at $C=1.1$ exceeds amplitude $10^3$ after 210 steps. These runs illustrate the stability calculation in Proposition 3. They do not provide an independent empirical test of the scheme.

An explicit compressible-flow code must resolve its fastest characteristic speed, approximately $|u|+c_{\rm s}$. Module 10 estimated a step from the flow speed $U$ alone. For its laboratory-air example, the compressible Courant step is smaller by a factor $1+1/M=35.01$, where $M=U/c_{\rm s}=0.0294$. In its supersonic cloud example the factor is only $1.0924$. This distinction changes computational cost without changing the physical Reynolds number.

3. The viscosity every grid carries

Stability does not mean that a grid reproduces the original differential equation exactly. A Taylor expansion of the upwind update reveals an additional diffusion term. This modified equation explains why a stable first-order scheme broadens smooth profiles and discontinuities.

Proposition 4 (the modified equation of the upwind scheme). A smooth function that satisfies (2.1) exactly at the grid points satisfies, to second order in $\Delta x$ and $\Delta t$, $$ \partial_t u + a\,\partial_x u \;=\; D_{\rm num}\,\partial_x^{2}u, \qquad D_{\rm num} = \frac{a\Delta x}{2}\,(1 - C). \tag{3.1}$$ The upwind scheme solves the advection equation with an added diffusion, and the diffusivity is proportional to the cell size.
Expand about $(x_i, t_n)$: $u_i^{n+1} = u + \Delta t\,u_t + \tfrac12\Delta t^{2}u_{tt} + O(\Delta t^{3})$ and $u_{i-1}^n = u - \Delta x\,u_x + \tfrac12\Delta x^{2}u_{xx} + O(\Delta x^{3})$. Substituting into (2.1) and dividing by $\Delta t$: $$ u_t + \tfrac12\Delta t\,u_{tt} = -a\,u_x + \tfrac12 a\Delta x\,u_{xx} + O(\Delta^{2}).$$ To the order kept, $u_t = -a u_x + O(\Delta)$, so $u_{tt} = a^{2}u_{xx} + O(\Delta)$. Substituting, $u_t + a u_x = \tfrac12 a\Delta x\,u_{xx} - \tfrac12\Delta t\,a^{2}u_{xx} + O(\Delta^{2}) = \tfrac12 a\Delta x(1 - a\Delta t/\Delta x)\,u_{xx}$, which is (3.1). ∎

At $C=1$, the numerical diffusivity vanishes because the update is an exact cell shift. As $C$ decreases, the diffusion coefficient $a\Delta x(1-C)/2$ increases, although the physical time advanced per step also decreases. The coefficient describes the leading error for a smooth solution; the next proposition gives an exact variance result for the discrete update.

Proposition 5 (the variance grows by exactly $C(1-C)\Delta x^{2}$ per step). Let $u_i \ge 0$ be advanced by (2.1) with $0 \le C \le 1$ on a grid long enough that nothing reaches its ends. Then the total $\sum_i u_i$ is unchanged, the mean position moves by $C\Delta x$ per step, and the variance of the profile grows by exactly $$ \Delta(\sigma^{2}) \;=\; C(1-C)\,\Delta x^{2} \;=\; 2D_{\rm num}\,\Delta t \quad\text{per step}. \tag{3.2}$$
Write (2.1) as $u_i^{n+1} = (1-C)u_i^n + C u_{i-1}^n$. Read $u_i/\sum_j u_j$ as a probability distribution on the cells. One step moves each unit of $u$ zero cells with weight $1-C$ and one cell with weight $C$, independently of where it is: the new distribution is the old one convolved with a two-point distribution of mean $C\Delta x$ and variance $C\Delta x^{2} - (C\Delta x)^{2} = C(1-C)\Delta x^{2}$. Under convolution, means and variances add. The last equality in (3.2) is $C(1-C)\Delta x^{2} = 2\cdot\tfrac12 a\Delta x(1-C)\cdot(C\Delta x/a)$. A diffusion equation with diffusivity $D$ grows a variance by $2D\Delta t$ in a time $\Delta t$, so the scheme diffuses exactly at the rate (3.1) predicts. ∎

A Gaussian advected for 200 steps shows the predicted increase in variance at $C=0.25$, $0.5$, and $0.9$. The measured-to-predicted ratios are $1.000000000$ to the displayed precision. This agreement checks the implementation of the discrete update. For $N$ cells across length $L$, the effective numerical Reynolds number is $$ \mathrm{Re}_{\rm num} \;=\; \frac{UL}{D_{\rm num}} \;=\; \frac{2N}{1 - C}. \tag{3.3}$$ It depends on $N$ and $C$ rather than on the simulated fluid. At $N=1024$, it is $4096$ for $C=0.5$ and $2.048\times10^4$ for $C=0.9$.

4. The Riemann problem and Godunov's method

A scalar has one propagation speed, so upwinding selects a single side of each face. The Euler equations have three characteristic speeds: $u-c_{\rm s}$, $u$, and $u+c_{\rm s}$. A face can therefore receive information from both directions. A Riemann solver determines the flux from the local left and right states.

Definition 2 (the Riemann problem). The Riemann problem for the Euler equations is the initial state $(\rho, u, P) = (\rho_{\rm L}, u_{\rm L}, P_{\rm L})$ for $x \lt 0$ and $(\rho_{\rm R}, u_{\rm R}, P_{\rm R})$ for $x \gt 0$. Its solution depends on $x/t$ only. It has four uniform regions separated by three waves: a left wave (shock or rarefaction), a contact discontinuity moving at $u^{*}$, and a right wave. Between the two outer waves the pressure $P^{*}$ and velocity $u^{*}$ are uniform; the density jumps at the contact.
Definition 3 (Godunov's method). Godunov's method is the scheme (1.1) in which $F_{i+1/2}$ is the flux $F(U)$ of the exact solution of the Riemann problem between the states $U_i$ and $U_{i+1}$, evaluated at $x/t = 0$ on the face.
Proposition 6 (the star pressure). For an ideal gas with index $\gamma$, sound speeds $c_K = (\gamma P_K/\rho_K)^{1/2}$ and $K = {\rm L}$ or ${\rm R}$, the star pressure $P^{*}$ is the root of $$ f_{\rm L}(P) + f_{\rm R}(P) + u_{\rm R} - u_{\rm L} = 0, \qquad f_K(P) = \begin{cases} (P - P_K)\left[\dfrac{A_K}{P + B_K}\right]^{1/2}, & P \gt P_K \ \text{(shock)},\\[1.2ex] \dfrac{2c_K}{\gamma-1}\left[\left(\dfrac{P}{P_K}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/2\gamma} - 1\right], & P \le P_K\ \text{(rarefaction)},\end{cases} \tag{4.1}$$ with $A_K = 2/((\gamma+1)\rho_K)$ and $B_K = P_K(\gamma-1)/(\gamma+1)$, and $u^{*} = \tfrac12(u_{\rm L} + u_{\rm R}) + \tfrac12(f_{\rm R}(P^{*}) - f_{\rm L}(P^{*}))$. The root is unique, and it exists with $P^{*} \gt 0$ if and only if $$ \frac{2}{\gamma - 1}\,(c_{\rm L} + c_{\rm R}) \;\gt\; u_{\rm R} - u_{\rm L}. \tag{4.2}$$
The shock branch is Module 8's jump conditions. In the frame of a shock that moves into the state $K$, let $j$ be the mass flux through it. Conservation of mass and momentum give $j^{2} = (P - P_K)/(1/\rho_K - 1/\rho^{*})$, and the Hugoniot relation gives $\rho^{*}/\rho_K = (P/P_K + b)/(bP/P_K + 1)$ with $b = (\gamma-1)/(\gamma+1)$. This is Module 8's (3.1) with the Mach number eliminated: write $\Pi = P/P_K$; the pressure ratio of (3.1) gives $M_1^{2} = [(\gamma+1)\Pi + (\gamma-1)]/(2\gamma)$, and substituting this into its density ratio gives $\rho^{*}/\rho_K = [(\gamma+1)\Pi + (\gamma-1)]/[(\gamma-1)\Pi + (\gamma+1)] = (\Pi + b)/(b\Pi + 1)$. At $\Pi = 1$ the ratio is $1$, and as $\Pi \to \infty$ it tends to $1/b = (\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$, Module 8's ceiling (3.3). Then $1/\rho_K - 1/\rho^{*} = (1-b)(P/P_K - 1)/[\rho_K(P/P_K + b)]$, so $j^{2} = \rho_K(P + bP_K)/(1-b)$. With $1 - b = 2/(\gamma+1)$ this is $j^{2} = (P + B_K)/A_K$. The velocity jump across the shock is $j(1/\rho_K - 1/\rho^{*}) = (P - P_K)/j$, which is the first line of (4.1). The rarefaction branch. Across a rarefaction the flow is isentropic, so $P \propto \rho^{\gamma}$ and $c \propto P^{(\gamma-1)/2\gamma}$. The Riemann invariant $u + 2c/(\gamma-1)$ is constant across a left-facing fan ($u - 2c/(\gamma-1)$ across a right-facing one). This result is stated, not derived: no module of this book derives the Riemann invariants, and Module 8 §3.1 names the fan and does not derive it. The velocity change from $P_K$ to $P$ is therefore $(2/(\gamma-1))(c^{*} - c_K)$ in magnitude, which is the second line. The equation. The left wave gives $u^{*} = u_{\rm L} - f_{\rm L}(P^{*})$ and the right wave gives $u^{*} = u_{\rm R} + f_{\rm R}(P^{*})$. Setting them equal gives (4.1), and adding them gives $u^{*}$. Uniqueness and existence. Both branches of $f_K$ increase with $P$ (the rarefaction branch visibly; the shock branch by the derivative written out in Problem D2) and join with equal value and equal slope $1/(\rho_Kc_K)$ at $P = P_K$, so the left side of (4.1) is strictly increasing and has at most one root. It tends to $+\infty$ as $P \to \infty$, and as $P \to 0$ it tends to $-2(c_{\rm L} + c_{\rm R})/(\gamma-1) + u_{\rm R} - u_{\rm L}$, which is negative exactly when (4.2) holds. What is stated. The rarefaction branch rests on the Riemann invariant, which this book states; everything else in the proof is derived. ∎

Identical left and right states remain unchanged: the Riemann solution has $P^*=P_{\rm L}$ and $u^*=u_{\rm L}$. When the star pressure approaches zero, the two rarefactions can separate and create a vacuum; equation (4.2) states the corresponding limit. These cases check the solver beyond the standard shock-tube example.

5. The anchor: Sod's shock tube

The Sod shock tube starts from two stationary ideal gases separated by a diaphragm. With $\gamma=1.4$, the left state has $(\rho,P)=(1,1)$ and the right state $(0.125,0.1)$. Removing the diaphragm produces a left-moving rarefaction, a contact, and a right-moving shock. The exact Riemann solution provides a reference for measuring numerical error.

CHECK 2(a) — the exact Sod solution. Proposition 6 gives $P^*=0.303130$, $u^*=0.927453$, $\rho^*_{\rm L}=0.426319$, $\rho^*_{\rm R}=0.265574$, and shock speed $1.752156$. The five values agree with Marzouk (2020), Table 2, to its printed precision. The computed shock satisfies the mass and momentum jump conditions, while the rarefaction follows the isentropic branch. This checks the implementation of both branches of the Riemann solver against known reference values.

We use the exact solution to measure density error separately in the fan, at the contact, and at the shock. First-order Godunov runs cover $N=100$ through $6400$ cells with Courant coefficient $0.9$. These regions converge at different rates because their solution profiles have different regularity.

The contact is a particularly useful test because pressure and velocity are constant across it while density jumps. Godunov's mass flux there reduces to the upwind flux $\rho_i u^*$. The contact therefore obeys the scalar update (2.1) with $C_{\rm c}=u^*\Delta t/\Delta x$. At $N=6400$, the time-averaged contact Courant number is $0.3810$.

To predict its width, apply Proposition 5 to density differences $d_i=\rho_{i-1}-\rho_i$. Initially only one $d_i$ is nonzero. Upwinding replaces each difference by $(1-C_{\rm c})d_i+C_{\rm c}d_{i-1}$, so its total remains $\Delta\rho_{\rm c}$ and its variance grows by $C_{\rm c}(1-C_{\rm c})\Delta x^2$ per step. Summing over steps gives $\sigma^2=\sum_n C_{\rm c}(1-C_{\rm c})\Delta x^2$. With $\Delta t\propto\Delta x$, there are $O(1/\Delta x)$ steps at fixed physical time. Consequently $\sigma\propto\Delta x^{1/2}$.

For many steps, the distribution of differences approaches a Gaussian. Integrating the difference between the resulting smoothed step and the exact step gives $$ E_{\rm c} \;=\; \Delta\rho_{\rm c}\,\sigma\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{1/2}, \qquad \sigma \propto \Delta x^{1/2}. \tag{5.1}$$ Here $\Delta\rho_{\rm c}=0.160746$; no width parameter is fitted. A shock behaves differently because characteristics converge toward it, keeping its numerical width to a few cells as the grid is refined.

CHECK 2(b) — different convergence rates within one flow. From $N=100$ to $6400$, the measured density $L_1$ error falls with order $1.013$ at the shock, $0.511$ at the contact, $0.752$ in the fan, and $0.646$ overall. The contact error is $1.1293$ times equation (5.1) at $N=100$ and $1.0659$ times at $N=6400$. Its measured rate is consistent with the predicted $\Delta x^{1/2}$ scaling. The method's formal first-order estimate assumes smooth solutions; the discontinuous contact changes the observed global rate in this norm. These results do not invalidate the formal order under its stated regularity assumptions.

The fan has a measured density-error order of $0.752$ over the sampled resolutions. Its head and tail are continuous corners where derivatives jump, so smooth-solution truncation estimates need not describe them. Equation (5.1) predicts only the contact contribution. Its remaining discrepancy with the measured contact error decreases as resolution rises.

6. A closed form tests a code: the Sedov blast

The Sedov–Taylor blast provides a second test with a known shock trajectory. For $\gamma=1.4$, $\rho_0=1$, and explosion energy $E=0.851072$, the similarity solution puts the shock at $r=1$ when $t=1$. We compare that radius and the strong-shock density jump with a spherical Godunov calculation.

The spherical update uses cell volumes $(r_+^3-r_-^3)/3$ per steradian and face areas $r^2$. A geometric pressure source preserves a uniform-pressure state. Energy begins in the innermost cell, and the ambient pressure is $10^{-6}$. The grid spans $0\le r\le1.2$ with 100, 200, or 400 cells at Courant coefficient $0.5$.

CHECK 3 — convergence in the Sedov blast. At 100, 200, and 400 cells, the half-height estimates of the shock radius are $1.02764$, $1.01365$, and $1.00663$. Their errors relative to the exact radius 1 roughly halve with each doubling of resolution. Peak densities are $3.0559$, $3.7619$, and $4.4010$, below the exact strong-shock jump value 6 because the shock is spread over several cells. The update conserves total energy to the reported roundoff level. Conservation supports convergence to the correct weak solution; it does not make the shock position exact on a finite grid.

The Sedov solution concentrates swept-up mass in a thin shell behind the shock. A first-order calculation spreads that shell across cells, reducing its peak density. The chosen half-height radius also lies outside the exact radius at finite resolution, but approaches it as the grid is refined. The peak and position therefore measure different aspects of error; neither should be reported as exact merely because the discrete update conserves energy.

7. Smoothed particle hydrodynamics

A grid fixes spatial cells and moves fluid through them. Smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH) instead follows particles of fixed mass. Fields are reconstructed from neighbouring particles using a smoothing kernel. The method follows a collapsing concentration without adding grid cells, but fixed particle mass still limits the smallest resolved gravitating mass.

Definition 4 (the kernel and the density sum). Each particle $a$ has mass $m_a$, position $x_a$ and a smoothing length $h_a$. In one dimension the M4 cubic spline kernel is $$ W(x, h) = \frac{2}{3h}\begin{cases} 1 - \tfrac32 q^{2} + \tfrac34 q^{3}, & 0 \le q \lt 1,\\ \tfrac14(2 - q)^{3}, & 1 \le q \lt 2,\\ 0, & q \ge 2,\end{cases} \qquad q = |x|/h, \tag{7.1}$$ normalised so that $\int W\,\mathrm{d}x = 1$ (Monaghan 1992, pp. 554–555, with the constant $2/3$ in one dimension). The density is the sum $\rho_a = \sum_b m_b W(x_a - x_b, h_a)$, including $b = a$, and this module sets $h_a = 1.2\,m_a/\rho_a$, iterated with the sum.

The smoothing length $h$ sets the kernel's spatial support. It differs from the mixing-layer width $h$ used in Module 8. With $h=1.2\Delta x$, the chosen cubic-spline kernel extends $2.4$ particle spacings to either side.

Proposition 7 (SPH conserves momentum exactly). Let the particles move by $$ \frac{\mathrm{d}v_a}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\sum_{b\ne a} m_b\left(\frac{P_a}{\rho_a^{2}} + \frac{P_b}{\rho_b^{2}} + \Pi_{ab}\right)\partial_{x_a}\overline{W}_{ab}, \qquad \overline{W}_{ab} = \tfrac12\left[W(x_a - x_b, h_a) + W(x_a - x_b, h_b)\right], \tag{7.2}$$ where $\Pi_{ab} = \Pi_{ba}$ is any symmetric pair term. Then $\sum_a m_a v_a$ is constant in time.
$\overline{W}_{ab}$ depends on the positions only through $x_a - x_b$ and is even in it, so $\partial_{x_b}\overline{W}_{ab} = -\partial_{x_a}\overline{W}_{ab}$, and $\overline{W}_{ba} = \overline{W}_{ab}$. The force on $a$ from $b$ is $-m_am_bT_{ab}\,\partial_{x_a}\overline{W}_{ab}$, with $T_{ab}$ the bracket in (7.2), which is symmetric in $a$ and $b$. The force on $b$ from $a$ is $-m_bm_aT_{ab}\,\partial_{x_b}\overline{W}_{ab} = +m_am_bT_{ab}\,\partial_{x_a}\overline{W}_{ab}$. Each pair contributes forces that are equal and opposite, so $\mathrm{d}(\sum_a m_av_a)/\mathrm{d}t = 0$. The trivial limit: with one particle there are no pairs, and it moves at constant velocity. ∎

Shocks require dissipation in the SPH equations. Here the pairwise artificial-viscosity term uses Monaghan's form with $\alpha=1$ and $\beta=2$ for approaching particles. The thermal-energy update is paired with the momentum equation so that pairwise exchanges conserve total energy. Time integration uses kick–drift–kick with a step based on $0.2h/(c_{\rm s}+|v|)$. These choices define the particular SPH calculation tested below; other formulations may behave differently.

8. Grid against particles at a contact

The Sod contact tests how the particle method handles a jump in density at nearly uniform pressure. Equal-mass particles are eight times farther apart on the low-density side. We run 200, 400, and 800 left-side particles per unit length, then compare pressure near the moving contact at $t=0.2$ with the exact $P^*=0.303130$.

CHECK 4 — pressure error at an SPH contact. For standard SPH, the maximum relative pressure error within $0.05$ of the contact is $0.0893$, $0.0938$, and $0.0942$ at 200, 400, and 800 particles per unit length. These runs show no decrease in that maximum-norm error. The corresponding volume-weighted relative $L_1$ errors are $0.0511$, $0.0241$, and $0.0129$, so integrated error does decrease. With Price's artificial conductivity, the maximum error falls to $0.0371$, $0.0310$, and $0.0216$. The added term improves this test; no norm-independent convergence claim follows from three resolutions.

Price (2008) identifies a pressure blip at an SPH contact and traces it in part to a thermal-energy overshoot. The present run also has such an overshoot: near the contact, specific thermal energy reaches $3.0943$ rather than the exact hot-side value $2.8535$. Since $P=(\gamma-1)\rho u$, that error affects pressure. Price's published shock tube uses $\gamma=5/3$; this Sod test uses $\gamma=1.4$. Their numerical values should therefore not be identified, even though they illustrate the same mechanism.

A spurious pressure gradient at an entropy contrast can resist interpenetration of two fluids. Price relates this effect to artificial surface tension at shearing contacts and shows how conductivity reduces it. Agertz and colleagues found that a standard SPH calculation suppressed Kelvin–Helmholtz rolls that developed in a grid calculation. Their two-dimensional result is consistent with this contact mechanism, but the one-dimensional Sod runs here do not reproduce the full instability test.

9. Following a collapse

Gravity introduces a physical instability scale. Module 5 derived the Jeans length $\lambda_{\rm J}=(\pi c_{\rm s}^2/G\rho_0)^{1/2}$: perturbations longer than this scale can collapse. If a numerical grid represents that length with too few cells, it may produce fragmentation driven by discretisation rather than by the fluid equations.

Proposition 8 (the Jeans resolution condition). Truelove and colleagues (1997) define $J=\Delta x/\lambda_{\rm J}$ and report that $J\le0.25$ avoided artificial fragmentation in their tested isothermal collapse. This is an empirical resolution criterion, not a general convergence theorem. Since $\lambda_{\rm J}\propto\rho^{-1/2}$ at fixed temperature, following collapse from $\rho_0$ to $\rho$ requires cell width to shrink by $(\rho/\rho_0)^{1/2}$ to maintain the same $J$.
What is stated. The value $0.25$ is empirical. Truelove et al. established it on the collapse of a centrally condensed isothermal cloud computed with adaptive refinement at $J_{\max} = 0.5$, $0.375$, $0.25$ and $0.125$, and it is not derived from a stability analysis. Truelove et al. call the resolution of $\lambda_{\rm J}$ "a necessary (but not, in general, sufficient) condition for convergence" (p. L181). What is derived. By Module 5's (3.2), $\lambda_{\rm J} = (\pi c_{\rm s}^{2}/G\rho)^{1/2}$ with $c_{\rm s}$ fixed by the temperature, so $\lambda_{\rm J}(\rho)/\lambda_{\rm J}(\rho_0) = (\rho_0/\rho)^{1/2}$, and a cell that satisfies $\Delta x \le 0.25\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ at the density reached must be smaller by that factor. ∎

For the 10 K core used in Module 5, $\lambda_{\rm J}=0.19479$ pc at $n(\mathrm{H_2})=10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. The Truelove condition requires cells no wider than about $10^4$ AU initially. If the gas remains isothermal up to $10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, the limit falls to $10.04$ AU. A uniform box four initial Jeans lengths across would then need about $1.6\times10^4$ cells per side, or $4.096\times10^{12}$ cells in three dimensions. Adaptive refinement places the required cells only where the density rises. The isothermal estimate ceases to apply when cooling can no longer hold the gas at 10 K.

SPH has a corresponding mass-resolution condition. Bate and Burkert (1997) require the Jeans mass to exceed roughly $2N_{\rm neigh}m$, where $m$ is particle mass and $N_{\rm neigh}=50$ in their example. At $10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, the core's quoted Jeans mass is $0.002658\,M_\odot$, giving $m\lesssim2.66\times10^{-5}\,M_\odot$. Particles move closer during collapse, improving spatial resolution, but their individual masses do not shrink. A fixed-mass SPH run must therefore choose its particle mass for the highest density it intends to resolve.

10. The price of resolution

Resolving turbulence and resolving a contact impose different computational costs. The first depends on the smallest dissipative scale represented by the method. The second depends on how rapidly error decreases as a discontinuity is refined. Both estimates require a specified numerical representation and accuracy criterion.

A grid-scale estimate. Suppose a local discretisation has one characteristic spatial scale $\Delta x=L/N$ and requires the Kolmogorov scale $\eta$ to be no smaller than $\Delta x$. Since $L/\eta=\mathrm{Re}^{3/4}$, this gives the necessary estimate $\mathrm{Re}\lesssim N^{4/3}$. It yields $1.032\times10^4$ for $N=1024$ and $6.554\times10^4$ for $N=4096$. This is not a universal accuracy bound. Practical methods need several effective degrees of freedom across a relevant structure, and higher-order or subcell representations do not fit the literal one-scale assumption.

The cost of a contact. Over the last measured refinement, the contact error decreases with fitted order $0.5209$. Extrapolating that rate from $N=6400$ to an error of $10^{-4}$ gives about $1.864\times10^5$ cells in one dimension. For a uniform three-dimensional explicit grid, work scales approximately as $N^4$: $N^3$ cells times $N$ Courant-limited steps. The projected work is therefore about $7.19\times10^5$ times greater. This is an extrapolation for this method and error measure, not a universal cost law.

12. Scope and limitations

This chapter has derived the Courant condition, the leading numerical diffusivity of upwinding, and resolution tests for grid and particle methods. It has also shown how shocks and contacts can converge at different rates. Multidimensional turbulence, magnetic fields, radiation, and self-gravity require further numerical machinery beyond these examples.

13. Problems

C1. Use (2.3) to find the largest amplification factor of FTCS at $C = 0.5$ and the mode that has it. How many steps does that mode take to grow by a factor of $10^{3}$?
Solution
$|G|^2 = 1 + C^2\sin^2\theta$ is largest at $\theta = \pi/2$, a wavelength of four cells, where $|G| = (1 + 0.25)^{1/2} = 1.118034$. It grows by $10^{3}$ in $\ln 10^{3}/\ln 1.118034 = 61.91$ steps, so 62. The Courant condition (2.2) holds at $C = 0.5$, and the scheme still fails, because Proposition 2 is necessary and not sufficient.
C2. Sod's problem is run by Godunov's method on 400 cells of $[0, 1]$ at $C_{\rm CFL} = 0.9$. What is the first time step? Why does the run of §5 need $194$ steps to reach $t = 0.2$ rather than the $105$ this step would give?
Solution
At $t = 0$ the gas is at rest and the largest signal speed is the left sound speed, $c_{\rm L} = (1.4)^{1/2} = 1.183216$. So $\Delta t = 0.9\times(1/400)/1.183216 = 1.901597\times10^{-3}$, and $0.2/\Delta t = 105.17$. Once the flow starts, $|u| + c_{\rm s}$ rises: behind the shock the gas moves at $u^{*} = 0.927453$ with $c_{\rm s} = (1.4\times0.303130/0.265574)^{1/2} = 1.264$, so $S_{\max}$ nearly doubles and the step nearly halves.
C3. A code runs the upwind scheme on $N = 512$ cells at $C = 0.8$. Compute the effective numerical Reynolds number from (3.3). Compare it with the one-cell Kolmogorov-scale estimate of §10 and explain why they answer different questions.
Solution
$\mathrm{Re}_{\rm num} = 2\times512/0.2 = 5120.0$. The one-cell scale estimate is $512^{4/3}=4096.0$. The first number describes the leading diffusive error of upwind advection; the second estimates whether a Kolmogorov scale occupies at least one cell. Their numerical ordering does not define a universal resolvable Reynolds number or prove that either criterion is sufficient for accuracy.
D1. Derive the modified equation (3.1) of the upwind scheme, and show that at $C = 1$ the scheme is exact.
Solution
See the proof of Proposition 4. At $C = 1$, (2.1) gives $u_i^{n+1} = u_{i-1}^n$: the data move exactly one cell, $a\Delta t = \Delta x$, which is the exact solution. (3.1) gives $D_{\rm num} = 0$, and (2.3) gives $|G| = 1$ for every mode. Every higher term of the modified equation also vanishes, because the scheme and the exact solution agree at the grid points.
D2. For a Riemann problem in which both waves are shocks, derive (4.1) from Module 8's jump conditions, and show that the root is unique.
Solution
See the proof of Proposition 6, shock branch, for each side. Both $f_{\rm L}$ and $f_{\rm R}$ increase with $P$ for $P \gt P_K$: $f_K' = [A_K/(P + B_K)]^{1/2}[1 - (P - P_K)/(2(P + B_K))]$, and the second factor is positive because $P - P_K \lt 2(P + B_K)$. So the left side of (4.1) is strictly increasing and has at most one root.
D3. Show that the SPH equations of §7, (7.2) with the energy equation given there, conserve $\sum_a m_a(\tfrac12v_a^{2} + u_a)$ exactly in continuous time.
Solution
$\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t\sum_a m_a\tfrac12v_a^{2} = -\sum_a\sum_b m_am_bT_{ab}v_a\,\partial_{x_a}\overline W_{ab}$. Symmetrise over the pair using $\partial_{x_b}\overline W_{ab} = -\partial_{x_a}\overline W_{ab}$: it is $-\tfrac12\sum_{a,b}m_am_bT_{ab}v_{ab}\,\partial_{x_a}\overline W_{ab}$. The thermal term is $\sum_a m_a\mathrm{d}u_a/\mathrm{d}t = \sum_{a,b}m_am_b(P_a/\rho_a^2 + \Pi_{ab}/2)v_{ab}\partial_{x_a}\overline W_{ab}$, and symmetrising it gives $\tfrac12\sum_{a,b}m_am_b(P_a/\rho_a^2 + P_b/\rho_b^2 + \Pi_{ab})v_{ab}\partial_{x_a}\overline W_{ab}$, because $v_{ab}\partial_{x_a}\overline W_{ab}$ is symmetric in $a$ and $b$. The two sums cancel.
K1. Solve Toro's "123 problem" exactly: $\rho = 1$, $P = 0.4$ on both sides, $u_{\rm L} = -2$, $u_{\rm R} = +2$, $\gamma = 1.4$. Find $P^{*}$, $u^{*}$ and $\rho^{*}$, and check (4.2).
Solution
$c = (1.4\times0.4)^{1/2} = 0.748331$ on both sides, and (4.2) is $5\times1.496663 = 7.483$ against $u_{\rm R} - u_{\rm L} = 4$: no vacuum. Both waves are rarefactions. By symmetry $u^{*} = 0.000000$, and (4.1) gives $P^{*} = 0.001894$ and $\rho^{*} = 0.021852$ on both sides of the contact. The gas between the two fans is close to a vacuum without being one, which is why the problem is used to test whether a code keeps $\rho$ and $P$ positive.
K2. Module 5's $10$ K core, at $n(\mathrm{H_2}) = 10^{4}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, has $\lambda_{\rm J} = 0.19479$ pc. A uniform grid covers four of these Jeans lengths and is to follow the collapse, isothermally, to $n(\mathrm{H_2}) = 10^{8}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ with $J \le 0.25$. How many cells does it need?
Solution
At $10^{8}$, $\lambda_{\rm J} = 0.19479\times10^{-2} = 0.0019479$ pc by Proposition 8. The cell is at most a quarter of that, and the box is $4\times0.19479$ pc, so it needs $4\times0.19479/(0.25\times0.0019479) = 1600.0$ cells per side and $4.096\times10^{9}$ in three dimensions.
K3. CHECK 2 measures the contact error of Godunov's method on Sod's problem as $8.3074\times10^{-4}$ at $N = 3200$ and $5.7898\times10^{-4}$ at $N = 6400$. Assuming the same order continues, how many cells bring it to $10^{-4}$, and how much more work is that in three dimensions than $N = 6400$?
Solution
The order over the last doubling is $\ln(8.3074/5.7898)/\ln 2 = 0.5209$. Then $N = 6400\times(5.7898\times10^{-4}/10^{-4})^{1/0.5209} = 1.864\times10^{5}$. In three dimensions the work goes as $N^3$ cells times $N$ steps, so the ratio is $(1.864\times10^{5}/6400)^{4} = 7.19\times10^{5}$. At first order it would have been $(5.79)^{4} = 1.12\times10^{3}$.
