1. The problem: a speed of sound low by 15.5 per cent, and a star that rings

Module 3 described a gas in hydrostatic equilibrium. We now perturb that equilibrium and retain only terms linear in the disturbance. The calculation produces acoustic waves and introduces the perturbative method used for the instabilities in Modules 5–7.

The first calculation of the speed of sound from mechanics was Newton's. He treated the air as a gas whose pressure is proportional to its density at fixed temperature, and his result, in modern notation, is

$$ c_{\rm N} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{P}{\rho}} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{\mu\,m_u}}, \tag{1.1}$$

with $P$ the pressure, $\rho$ the mass density, $k_B$ Boltzmann's constant, $T$ the temperature, $\mu$ the mean molecular weight and $m_u$ the atomic mass unit, as in Module 3, equation (3.2). For dry air at $0\ ^\circ$C, (1.1) gives $280.02\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The measured speed is $331.45\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Newton's value is $0.8448$ of it, low by $15.5$ per cent. The error is not in the measurement and not in the arithmetic. It is in the relation assumed between pressure and density, the closure, and §3.1 finds it.

The Sun supports standing acoustic modes with periods near five minutes. Their frequencies are measured with high precision. Modes of fixed angular degree and consecutive radial order are separated by the large frequency separation, which is controlled mainly by the acoustic travel time across the star.

$$ \Delta\nu_{\odot} \;=\; 135.1\pm0.1\ \mu\mathrm{Hz}. \tag{1.2}$$

§5 shows that this spacing is set by the time sound takes to cross the star. That time depends on the sound speed at every depth, and the sound speed depends on the closure. The solar frequency spacing tests the interior sound-speed profile, just as the measured speed of sound in air tests the thermodynamic closure for air.

Sections 2 and 3 derive the acoustic wave equation and compare isothermal and adiabatic sound speeds with measurements in air. Sections 4 and 5 introduce gravitational stratification and standing modes. Sections 6 and 7 use the solar frequency separation to test the sound-speed profile and its thermodynamic closure.

2. Linear perturbation theory

The equations to be perturbed are those of an inviscid, adiabatic fluid. Module 2 derived them: continuity (6.3), the Euler equation (7.3), and the adiabatic energy equation in (8.4). With the Lagrangian derivative $D/Dt \equiv \partial/\partial t + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla$ of Module 2, (6.1), they read

$$ \frac{D\rho}{Dt} \;=\; -\rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}, \tag{2.1}$$ $$ \rho\,\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} \;=\; -\nabla P + \rho\,\mathbf{g}, \tag{2.2}$$

in which $\mathbf{u}$ is the bulk velocity and $\mathbf{g}$ the gravitational acceleration. Module 2 wrote the adiabatic condition for a gas with a fixed ratio of specific heats. A gas whose ionisation changes as it is compressed needs a more general coefficient.

Definition 1 (first adiabatic exponent). The first adiabatic exponent is $$ \Gamma_1 \;\equiv\; \left(\frac{\partial\ln P}{\partial\ln\rho}\right)_{s}, \tag{2.3}$$ the logarithmic response of the pressure to a compression at fixed specific entropy $s$. An adiabatic flow is one in which each parcel keeps its entropy, $Ds/Dt=0$, and then $$ \frac{DP}{Dt} \;=\; \frac{\Gamma_1P}{\rho}\,\frac{D\rho}{Dt}. \tag{2.4}$$ For an ideal gas of fixed composition and fixed ionisation, $\Gamma_1 = \gamma$, the ratio of specific heats: $5/3$ for a monatomic gas and $7/5$ for the diatomic molecules of air. Where a compression changes the ionisation, part of the work done goes into ionising atoms rather than into raising the pressure, and $\Gamma_1\lt\gamma$.

Equation (2.4) follows from (2.3) by the chain rule along a parcel's path, since $s$ is constant along that path. Module 2's condition $D(P/\rho^\gamma)/Dt = 0$ is (2.4) with $\Gamma_1=\gamma$ constant. We postpone the thermodynamics of partial ionisation to Module 6. Here it identifies one limitation of the constant-$\gamma$ closure and is used in §7.3 only as a candidate explanation of a measured shortfall.

Equations (2.1), (2.2) and (2.4) are nonlinear, because products such as $\rho\,(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}$ appear. Close to an equilibrium the products of small quantities are smaller still, and dropping them gives linear equations.

Definition 2 (linear perturbation). Let $\rho_0(\mathbf{x})$, $P_0(\mathbf{x})$ and $\mathbf{u}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ be a static solution of (2.1), (2.2) and (2.4), so that $\nabla P_0 = \rho_0\mathbf{g}$ (Module 3, (2.2)). A perturbation writes each field as its equilibrium value plus a small departure, $$ \rho = \rho_0 + \rho_1, \qquad P = P_0 + P_1, \qquad \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}_1, \tag{2.5}$$ with $|\rho_1|\ll\rho_0$, $|P_1|\ll P_0$, and $|\mathbf{u}_1|$ small compared with the sound speed. The linearised equations are obtained by substituting (2.5) and keeping only terms of first order in the subscript-1 quantities. The gravitational field $\mathbf{g}$ is held fixed. The perturbation of gravity by $\rho_1$ is neglected.
Proposition 1 (the linearised equations). About a static equilibrium, and with $\mathbf{g}$ fixed, the linearised forms of (2.1), (2.2) and (2.4) are $$ \frac{\partial\rho_1}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho_0\mathbf{u}_1) \;=\; 0, \tag{2.6}$$ $$ \rho_0\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_1}{\partial t} \;=\; -\nabla P_1 + \rho_1\,\mathbf{g}, \tag{2.7}$$ $$ \frac{\partial P_1}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u}_1\cdot\nabla P_0 \;=\; c^2\left(\frac{\partial\rho_1}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u}_1\cdot\nabla\rho_0\right), \qquad c^2 \;\equiv\; \frac{\Gamma_1P_0}{\rho_0}. \tag{2.8}$$
Continuity (2.1) is equivalent to $\partial\rho/\partial t + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u}) = 0$ (Module 2, Proposition 5). Substituting (2.5) gives $\partial\rho_1/\partial t + \nabla\cdot(\rho_0\mathbf{u}_1) + \nabla\cdot(\rho_1\mathbf{u}_1) = 0$, using $\partial\rho_0/\partial t = 0$. The last term is a product of two first-order quantities and is dropped, which leaves (2.6). In (2.2), the left side is $(\rho_0+\rho_1)[\partial\mathbf{u}_1/\partial t + (\mathbf{u}_1\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}_1]$, whose only first-order term is $\rho_0\,\partial\mathbf{u}_1/\partial t$. The right side is $-\nabla P_0 - \nabla P_1 + \rho_0\mathbf{g} + \rho_1\mathbf{g}$, and the zeroth-order terms cancel by hydrostatic equilibrium, which leaves (2.7). In (2.4), $DP/Dt = \partial P_1/\partial t + \mathbf{u}_1\cdot\nabla P_0 + \mathbf{u}_1\cdot\nabla P_1$, whose last term is second order; $D\rho/Dt$ is treated the same way. The coefficient $\Gamma_1P/\rho$ multiplies a first-order quantity, so only its zeroth-order value $\Gamma_1P_0/\rho_0 = c^2$ is kept. That gives (2.8). ∎

For these acoustic perturbations, the instantaneous ratio of the nonlinear advection term to the linear term is of order $|\mathbf u_1|/c_s$. A small ratio justifies linear evolution over sufficiently short times. Nonlinear steepening can nevertheless accumulate over many periods: for a compressive wave the characteristic steepening time is of order $(k|\mathbf u_1|)^{-1}$. Shock formation therefore does not require the perturbation velocity to first become comparable with the sound speed.

The quantity $c$ defined in (2.8) has the dimensions of a speed. §3 shows that it is the speed of sound.

3. Sound in a uniform gas

Take the simplest equilibrium: no gravity, so that hydrostatic balance gives $\nabla P_0 = \mathbf{0}$, and a uniform density $\rho_0$. Then $P_0$, $\rho_0$ and $c$ are constants.

Proposition 2 (the wave equation). In a uniform static gas with $\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{0}$, the linearised equations (2.6)–(2.8) imply $$ \frac{\partial^2\rho_1}{\partial t^2} \;=\; c^2\,\nabla^2\rho_1, \tag{3.1}$$ and the same equation for $P_1$. A plane wave $\rho_1 \propto \exp[i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x} - \omega t)]$ with wavevector $\mathbf{k}$ and angular frequency $\omega$ solves (3.1) if and only if $$ \omega^2 \;=\; c^2\,|\mathbf{k}|^2. \tag{3.2}$$ The phase speed $\omega/|\mathbf{k}|$ and the group speed $d\omega/d|\mathbf{k}|$ both equal $c$, for every wavelength.
With $\nabla P_0 = \nabla\rho_0 = \mathbf{0}$, (2.8) reduces to $\partial P_1/\partial t = c^2\,\partial\rho_1/\partial t$. Integrating in time, and choosing $P_1=\rho_1=0$ before the disturbance arrives, gives $P_1 = c^2\rho_1$. Take the time derivative of (2.6), which is $\partial^2\rho_1/\partial t^2 = -\rho_0\nabla\cdot(\partial\mathbf{u}_1/\partial t)$, and substitute the divergence of (2.7) with $\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{0}$, which is $\rho_0\nabla\cdot(\partial\mathbf{u}_1/\partial t) = -\nabla^2P_1$. The result is $\partial^2\rho_1/\partial t^2 = \nabla^2P_1 = c^2\nabla^2\rho_1$, which is (3.1). Because $P_1 = c^2\rho_1$ with $c$ constant, $P_1$ obeys the same equation. For the plane wave, $\partial^2/\partial t^2 \to -\omega^2$ and $\nabla^2\to -|\mathbf{k}|^2$, so (3.1) holds if and only if $\omega^2 = c^2|\mathbf{k}|^2$. Then $\omega/|\mathbf{k}| = c$, and $d\omega/d|\mathbf{k}| = c$. ∎

This book writes a perturbation as proportional to $\exp[i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\omega t)]$ and every dispersion relation in the form $\omega^2 = \ldots$. With that convention a real $\omega$ is an oscillation and $\omega^2\lt 0$ is exponential growth. Module 5 will write the Jeans relation in the same form, where the sign of the right-hand side decides stability.

The proof used only one property of $c^2$: that it is the constant ratio $P_1/\rho_1$ in the wave. Which ratio that is depends on whether the gas exchanges heat during an oscillation. The two predictions differ because one uses an isothermal pressure response and the other an adiabatic response.

3.1 Newton against Laplace: the terrestrial check

If heat flows fast enough to keep each parcel at the equilibrium temperature, the compression is isothermal. Then $P = \rho k_BT/(\mu m_u)$ at fixed $T$ gives $P_1/\rho_1 = P_0/\rho_0$, and $c$ is Newton's $c_{\rm N}$ of (1.1). If no heat flows during a period, the compression is adiabatic, (2.4) holds, and $c^2 = \Gamma_1P_0/\rho_0$. For an ideal gas this is Laplace's speed of sound,

$$ c_{\rm L} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\gamma\,k_BT}{\mu\,m_u}} \;=\; \sqrt{\gamma}\;c_{\rm N}. \tag{3.3}$$

Which limit applies is decided by comparing two times. Heat diffuses with a thermal diffusivity $\chi$, in $\mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$, and crosses a distance $\ell$ in a time of order $\ell^2/\chi$. The temperature contrast in a wave is between a compression and the neighbouring rarefaction, whose separation is set by the wavelength; take $\ell = 1/|\mathbf{k}|$. The oscillation lasts a time of order $1/\omega$. The compression is adiabatic when heat cannot cross $\ell$ within that time:

$$ \frac{1/\omega}{\ell^2/\chi} \;=\; \frac{\omega\chi\,|\mathbf{k}|^2}{\omega^2} \;=\; \frac{\omega\,\chi}{c^2} \;\ll\; 1, \tag{3.4}$$

where the middle step used (3.2). Criterion (3.4) is an order-of-magnitude estimate. It is not proved here, because the linearised energy equation with conduction is not solved in this module.

For dry air at $0\ ^\circ$C, with $\chi = 0.187\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $c = 331.32\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, the ratio in (3.4) is $1.07\times10^{-6}$ at $1$ kHz and $2.14\times10^{-5}$ at $20$ kHz, the upper limit of hearing. It reaches one only at $9.3\times10^{8}$ Hz, where the wavelength is $355$ nm, $5.2$ mean free paths of air. At that wavelength the continuum description of Module 1 is itself failing (Problem C1). Across the whole range in which air behaves as a fluid, sound in air is adiabatic.

The thermal diffusivity is computed from handbook values for dry air at $0\ ^\circ$C: thermal conductivity $0.0243\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, density $1.293\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, specific heat $1005\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. They are inputs, not checked against a source here. The conclusion depends only on the order of magnitude of $\chi$, and would survive an error of a factor of $10^3$ at every audible frequency (the ratio would stay below $0.022$). The mean free path of $68$ nm is the room-air value used in Module 1.

The two predictions can now be compared with a measurement. For dry air, $\mu = 28.9647$ as in Module 3 and $\gamma = 7/5$.

4. Sound in a stratified atmosphere: the acoustic cutoff

A star is stratified by gravity. Module 3 derived an isothermal plane-parallel atmosphere with $P,\rho\propto e^{-z/H}$ and $H=k_BT/(\mu m_ug)$. Perturbing that equilibrium introduces an acoustic cutoff. Waves below the cutoff frequency cannot propagate upward through the atmosphere.

Restrict attention to vertical motion, $\mathbf{u}_1 = u(z,t)\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}$, with all perturbations depending on $z$ and $t$ only. Waves with a horizontal wavenumber add a second restoring force, buoyancy, and are treated in Module 6.

Proposition 3 (vertical sound waves in an isothermal atmosphere). Let the equilibrium be a plane-parallel isothermal layer of ideal gas with uniform $\mu$, $g$ and $\Gamma_1$, so that $$ \rho_0(z) = \rho_0(0)\,e^{-z/H}, \qquad P_0 = g H\rho_0, \qquad c^2 = \Gamma_1 gH, \tag{4.1}$$ with $c$ uniform. For vertical perturbations, the velocity obeys $$ \frac{\partial^2u}{\partial t^2} \;=\; c^2\left(\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial z^2} - \frac{1}{H}\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\right). \tag{4.2}$$ The substitution $u = e^{z/2H}\,\psi$ turns (4.2) into $$ \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial t^2} \;=\; c^2\,\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial z^2} \;-\; \omega_{\rm ac}^2\,\psi, \qquad \omega_{\rm ac} \;\equiv\; \frac{c}{2H}, \tag{4.3}$$ and a wave $\psi\propto\exp[i(kz-\omega t)]$ solves (4.3) if and only if $$ \omega^2 \;=\; c^2k^2 + \omega_{\rm ac}^2. \tag{4.4}$$
The equilibrium (4.1) is Module 3's Proposition 3 with $P_0 = \rho_0k_BT/(\mu m_u) = gH\rho_0$ by the definition of $H$; then $c^2 = \Gamma_1P_0/\rho_0 = \Gamma_1gH$ is uniform. Two derivatives of the equilibrium are needed: $d\rho_0/dz = -\rho_0/H$ and $dP_0/dz = -\rho_0 g$. In one dimension, with $\mathbf{g} = -g\hat{\mathbf{z}}$, the linearised equations (2.6)–(2.8) are $$ \frac{\partial\rho_1}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial(\rho_0u)}{\partial z}, \qquad \rho_0\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial P_1}{\partial z} - g\rho_1, \qquad \frac{\partial P_1}{\partial t} - \rho_0gu = c^2\left(\frac{\partial\rho_1}{\partial t} - \frac{\rho_0u}{H}\right). $$ Step 1: eliminate $\rho_1$ from the energy equation. Expanding continuity, $\partial\rho_1/\partial t = -\rho_0\,\partial u/\partial z + \rho_0u/H$. Substituting into the energy equation, $$ \frac{\partial P_1}{\partial t} \;=\; \rho_0gu - c^2\rho_0\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} + \frac{c^2\rho_0u}{H} - \frac{c^2\rho_0u}{H} \;=\; -\rho_0\left(c^2\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} - gu\right). $$ The two terms in $c^2\rho_0u/H$ cancel. They would cancel for any $\rho_0(z)$, since both come from $u\,d\rho_0/dz$.
Step 2: differentiate the momentum equation in time and substitute both results: $$ \rho_0\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial t^2} \;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[\rho_0\left(c^2\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} - gu\right)\right] - g\left(-\rho_0\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} + \frac{\rho_0u}{H}\right). $$ Expand the first term with $d\rho_0/dz = -\rho_0/H$: $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[\rho_0\left(c^2u_z - gu\right)\right] \;=\; \rho_0\left[c^2u_{zz} - gu_z - \frac{c^2u_z}{H} + \frac{gu}{H}\right], $$ where subscripts denote partial derivatives. Adding the second term, $\rho_0[gu_z - gu/H]$, the terms in $g$ cancel in pairs and leave $\rho_0[c^2u_{zz} - c^2u_z/H]$. Dividing by $\rho_0$ gives (4.2). Before this expansion the result holds for any stratification: the terms in $g$ cancel by hydrostatic balance and uniform $g$ alone, and Steps 1 and 2 give $\rho_0\,\partial^2u/\partial t^2 = \partial(\Gamma_1P_0\,\partial u/\partial z)/\partial z$ for any $\rho_0(z)$ and $\Gamma_1(z)$. The isothermal equilibrium enters only in the expansion itself: with $c$ uniform and $d\rho_0/dz = -\rho_0/H$ with $H$ uniform, the right side becomes $\rho_0c^2(u_{zz} - u_z/H)$, an equation with constant coefficients. Step 3 needs those constant coefficients; in a non-isothermal layer $H$ and $c$ vary with $z$, and the substitution no longer produces a constant $\omega_{\rm ac}$.
Step 3: remove the first-derivative term. With $u = e^{z/2H}\psi$, $$ u_z = e^{z/2H}\left(\psi_z + \frac{\psi}{2H}\right), \qquad u_{zz} = e^{z/2H}\left(\psi_{zz} + \frac{\psi_z}{H} + \frac{\psi}{4H^2}\right). $$ So $u_{zz} - u_z/H = e^{z/2H}\,(\psi_{zz} - \psi/4H^2)$: the terms in $\psi_z$ cancel, and $\psi/4H^2 - \psi/2H^2 = -\psi/4H^2$. The left side of (4.2) is $e^{z/2H}\psi_{tt}$. Dividing through by $e^{z/2H}$ gives $\psi_{tt} = c^2\psi_{zz} - (c^2/4H^2)\psi$, which is (4.3) with $\omega_{\rm ac}^2 = c^2/4H^2$.
Step 4. For $\psi\propto\exp[i(kz-\omega t)]$, $\psi_{tt} = -\omega^2\psi$ and $\psi_{zz} = -k^2\psi$, so (4.3) holds if and only if $-\omega^2 = -c^2k^2 - \omega_{\rm ac}^2$, which is (4.4). ∎

Three consequences follow, and each is read directly from (4.4) or from the substitution.

A cutoff. For real $k$, (4.4) gives $\omega^2\ge\omega_{\rm ac}^2$. A wave with $\omega\lt\omega_{\rm ac}$ must have $k^2 = (\omega^2-\omega_{\rm ac}^2)/c^2 \lt 0$, so $k = \pm i\kappa$ with

$$ \kappa \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{\omega_{\rm ac}^2 - \omega^2}}{c}. \tag{4.5}$$

Such a wave does not propagate; $\psi$ varies as $e^{\pm\kappa z}$, and the physical solution in a semi-infinite atmosphere is the one that decays upward. The wave is evanescent. In cyclic frequency the cutoff is

$$ \nu_{\rm ac} \;=\; \frac{\omega_{\rm ac}}{2\pi} \;=\; \frac{c}{4\pi H} \;=\; \frac{\Gamma_1\,g}{4\pi c}, \tag{4.6}$$

The final form uses $H=c^2/(\Gamma_1g)$ from equation (4.1). It connects the acoustic cutoff frequency directly to the pressure scale height introduced in Module 3.

Growth of the amplitude with height. Since $\rho_0\propto e^{-z/H}$, the factor $e^{z/2H}$ in $u = e^{z/2H}\psi$ equals $[\rho_0(0)/\rho_0(z)]^{1/2}$. A propagating wave, with $|\psi|$ constant, has a velocity amplitude that grows as $\rho_0^{-1/2}$. The kinetic energy density $\tfrac12\rho_0u^2$ is proportional to $|\psi|^2$ and stays constant. Over $1000$ km of the solar atmosphere, with $H = 143.0$ km, the velocity grows by a factor of $33.0$ (Problem D1). Linear theory holds only while $|u|\ll c$, so a wave that propagates far enough upward must steepen into a shock, the subject of Module 8.

Energy in an evanescent wave. For $\omega\lt\omega_{\rm ac}$ the energy density $\tfrac12\rho_0u^2\propto|\psi|^2\propto e^{-2\kappa z}$ decays upward. The velocity amplitude varies as $e^{(1/2H-\kappa)z}$, and by (4.5) $\kappa\lt\omega_{\rm ac}/c = 1/2H$, so the velocity of an evanescent wave still grows with height, more slowly than that of a propagating one. "Evanescent" describes the energy, not the velocity (Problem C2).

Fig. 1 draws (4.4) and (4.5) for the solar photosphere. The dashed line is the uniform gas of Proposition 2. Stratification lifts the propagating branch above it and ends it at $\nu_{\rm ac}$ when $k=0$. Below $\nu_{\rm ac}$ the evanescent branch runs from $\kappa=0$ to $\kappa = 1/2H$ at zero frequency.

4.1 The solar cutoff

Evaluate equation (4.6) at the photosphere. The nominal solar $GM$ and the radius of the §6 model give $g_\odot=2.7398\times10^4\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}$. Its surface abundances are $X=0.7583$, $Y=0.22905$, and $Z=0.01265$. Treating the gas as neutral and assigning mass 16 per heavy-element particle gives

$$ \mu \;=\; \frac{1}{X + Y/4 + Z/16} \;=\; 1.2250. \tag{4.7}$$

At $T = T_{\rm eff} = 5772$ K with $\Gamma_1 = 5/3$, the sound speed is $c = 8.081\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, the scale height is $H = 143.0$ km, and

$$ \nu_{\rm ac} \;=\; \frac{c}{4\pi H} \;=\; 4497\ \mu\mathrm{Hz}. \tag{4.8}$$

Module 3 evaluated the photospheric scale height with a round input $\mu = 1.3$ and found $134.6$ km, with $g = 2.742\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}$ from the IAU radius. This module's table-radius $g$ gives $134.7$ km. The value $1.2250$ used here is computed from the model's composition. With $\mu = 1.3$ the cutoff would be $4633\ \mu$Hz; by (4.6) at fixed $T$ and $g$, $\nu_{\rm ac}\propto\mu^{1/2}$.

The measured cutoff of the Sun is "around $5000\ \mu$Hz" (Jiménez, García & Pallé 2011), varying by $100$ to $150\ \mu$Hz over the activity cycle. The prediction (4.8) is $0.8994$ of it. By (4.6), $\nu_{\rm ac}\propto T^{-1/2}$ at fixed $g$, $\mu$ and $\Gamma_1$, so matching $5000\ \mu$Hz requires $T = 4669$ K, which is $0.809\,T_{\rm eff}$. If the $150\ \mu$Hz maximum-to-minimum variation is centred on $5000\ \mu$Hz, the required temperature runs from $4532$ K at $5075\ \mu$Hz to $4812$ K at $4925\ \mu$Hz, that is from $0.785$ to $0.834\,T_{\rm eff}$.

This comparison is a consistency tie, not a check. The solar atmosphere is not isothermal, so no single temperature is the right one to put into (4.6); $T_{\rm eff}$ is a convenient reference, not a prediction of where the cutoff is set. The measured value carries no formal uncertainty in the source. What the comparison shows is that an isothermal atmosphere with $\Gamma_1 = 5/3$ and the neutral $\mu$ of (4.7) reproduces the measured cutoff at $4669$ K, $0.809\,T_{\rm eff}$, and at $0.785$–$0.834\,T_{\rm eff}$ across the activity variation. Within that model the gas must be cooler than $T_{\rm eff}$. The real atmosphere is not isothermal, so (4.6) is not the exact cutoff there, and the comparison does not locate the gas that sets it. The source's own derivation of $5000\ \mu$Hz is described there and not reproduced here.

The ratio of the measured cutoff to $\nu_{\max}$ is $5000/3090 = 1.618$. The five-minute modes, whose period at $\nu_{\max}$ is $5.39$ minutes, lie below the cutoff. Upward they are evanescent. The atmosphere reflects them, and they are trapped inside the star. That trapping is what makes the Sun a resonant cavity, and §5 finds the frequencies of the cavity.

5. Standing waves and the large separation

A wave trapped between two reflecting boundaries interferes with its own reflection, and only frequencies at which the reflections reinforce survive. The spacing of those frequencies is set by the time sound takes to cross the cavity.

Definition 3 (acoustic radius). The acoustic radius of a star of radius $R$ is the sound-crossing time from centre to surface, $$ \tau \;\equiv\; \int_0^R\frac{dr}{c(r)}. \tag{5.1}$$ For a one-dimensional cavity $0\le x\le L$ the same integral over $x$ is its acoustic length.
Proposition 4 (radial modes of a uniform sphere). Let a sphere of radius $R$ contain a uniform gas with sound speed $c$ and no gravity, and let the pressure perturbation vanish at the surface, $P_1(R)=0$. The spherically symmetric solutions of (3.1) that are finite at the centre have frequencies $$ \nu_n \;=\; \frac{n\,c}{2R}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots, \tag{5.2}$$ so consecutive modes are separated by exactly $c/(2R) = 1/(2\tau)$.
By Proposition 2, $P_1 = c^2\rho_1$ obeys (3.1). For a spherically symmetric standing wave $P_1 = f(r)\cos\omega t$, and the radial Laplacian is $\nabla^2P_1 = r^{-1}\,\partial^2(rP_1)/\partial r^2$. Writing $f = F(r)/r$, (3.1) becomes $F'' + k^2F = 0$ with $k = \omega/c$, whose general solution is $F = A\sin kr + B\cos kr$. Finiteness of $f = F/r$ at $r=0$ requires $F(0)=0$, so $B=0$ and $f = A\sin(kr)/r$. The surface condition $f(R)=0$ requires $\sin kR = 0$ with $A\ne0$, so $kR = n\pi$ for a positive integer $n$. Then $\omega = n\pi c/R$ and $\nu = \omega/2\pi = nc/(2R)$. The spacing is $c/(2R)$, and for uniform $c$ (5.1) gives $\tau = R/c$, so $c/(2R) = 1/(2\tau)$. ∎

The surface condition $P_1 = 0$ is an idealisation of a free surface. A different reflecting condition changes the phase of the reflection. In the uniform column of Proposition 5, $u=0$ instead of $P_1=0$ at one end gives $\nu_n = (n+\tfrac12)/(2\tau)$: every frequency shifts by $1/(4\tau)$ and the spacing is unchanged. In the sphere the rigid condition gives $\tan kR = kR$, whose roots approach $(n+\tfrac12)\pi$ only as $n$ grows, so there the spacing is unchanged only asymptotically. In a real star $c$ varies with radius. The next proposition shows, in one dimension, that the spacing is still $1/(2\tau)$ at high frequency, with $\tau$ the integral (5.1).

Proposition 5 (spacing in a cavity of varying sound speed). Let a one-dimensional column $0\le x\le L$ with no gravity have uniform $P_0$, a density $\rho_0(x)$ and an adiabatic exponent $\Gamma_1(x)$, so that $c(x)^2 = \Gamma_1(x)P_0/\rho_0(x)$. Let $P_1 = 0$ at both ends. Define the acoustic coordinate $s(x) = \int_0^x dx'/c$, with total $\tau = s(L)$, and the impedance $Z = \rho_0c$. Then the pressure amplitude $p(s)$ of a mode of angular frequency $\omega$ satisfies, with $p = Z^{1/2}q$, $$ \frac{d^2q}{ds^2} + \left[\omega^2 - V(s)\right]q \;=\; 0, \qquad V \;=\; a^2 - \frac{da}{ds}, \quad a \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\frac{d\ln Z}{ds}, \tag{5.3}$$ with $q(0) = q(\tau) = 0$. If $Z$ is uniform, $V=0$ and $\nu_n = n/(2\tau)$ exactly (uniform $Z$ requires only $\Gamma_1\rho_0$ constant, so $c$ may still vary). If $V$ is bounded, the mode with $n-1$ interior zeros satisfies $$ \left|\,\nu_n - \frac{n}{2\tau}\,\right| \;\le\; \frac{1}{4\pi^2\tau\,\nu_n}\int_0^\tau|V|\,ds, \tag{5.3a}$$ so the frequencies approach $n/(2\tau)$ as $\nu_n$ grows, and consecutive modes are separated by $1/(2\tau)$ up to a remainder of order $1/\nu_n$.
With $\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\nabla P_0 = \mathbf{0}$, (2.7) gives $\rho_0\,\partial u/\partial t = -\partial P_1/\partial x$. Equation (2.8) has a term $c^2u\,d\rho_0/dx$ on the right. Substituting $\partial\rho_1/\partial t = -\rho_0\,\partial u/\partial x - u\,d\rho_0/dx$ from (2.6) cancels it and leaves $\partial P_1/\partial t = -\rho_0c^2\,\partial u/\partial x = -\Gamma_1(x)P_0\,\partial u/\partial x$. For $P_1 = p(x)\cos\omega t$ and $u = v(x)\sin\omega t$, the two equations are $\rho_0\omega v = -p'$ and $\omega p = \Gamma_1P_0 v'$ (with $\Gamma_1 = \Gamma_1(x)$ here and below), where primes denote $d/dx$. Eliminating $v$ gives $(p'/\rho_0)' + \omega^2 p/(\Gamma_1P_0) = 0$.
Change variable to $s$, with $d/dx = c^{-1}d/ds$. Then $p'/\rho_0 = (\rho_0c)^{-1}dp/ds = Z^{-1}p_s$, and the equation becomes $c^{-1}\,d(Z^{-1}p_s)/ds + \omega^2p/(\rho_0c^2) = 0$. Multiplying by $c$ and using $\rho_0c = Z$ gives $d(Z^{-1}p_s)/ds + \omega^2Z^{-1}p = 0$, that is, $$ p_{ss} - 2a\,p_s + \omega^2p \;=\; 0. $$ Substitute $p = Z^{1/2}q$, so $p_s = Z^{1/2}(q_s + aq)$ and $p_{ss} = Z^{1/2}[q_{ss} + 2aq_s + (a^2 + a_s)q]$. Then $p_{ss} - 2ap_s = Z^{1/2}[q_{ss} + (a_s - a^2)q]$, and the equation is (5.3). Since $Z\gt0$, $p=0$ at an end if and only if $q=0$ there.
If $Z$ is uniform, $a = 0$ and $V = 0$, so $q = \sin\omega s$, and $q(\tau) = 0$ requires $\omega\tau = n\pi$, that is $\nu_n = n/(2\tau)$. If $V$ is bounded, write $q = r\sin\theta$ and $q_s = \omega r\cos\theta$ with $r\gt0$. Differentiating the first relation and comparing with the second gives $r_s\sin\theta = r\cos\theta\,(\omega - \theta_s)$; differentiating the second and using (5.3) gives $r_s\cos\theta = r\sin\theta\,(\theta_s - \omega + V/\omega)$. Multiplying the first by $\cos\theta$, the second by $\sin\theta$, and subtracting, $$ \theta_s \;=\; \omega - \frac{V}{\omega}\sin^2\theta. $$ Take $\theta(0) = 0$. Where $q = 0$, $\sin\theta = 0$ and $\theta_s = \omega\gt0$, so $\theta$ crosses each multiple of $\pi$ once and upward, and $q(\tau) = 0$ with $n-1$ interior zeros means $\theta(\tau) = n\pi$. Integrating from $0$ to $\tau$, $$ \left|\,\omega_n\tau - n\pi\,\right| \;=\; \frac{1}{\omega_n}\left|\int_0^\tau V\sin^2\theta\,ds\right| \;\le\; \frac{1}{\omega_n}\int_0^\tau|V|\,ds, $$ and dividing by $2\pi\tau$ with $\omega_n = 2\pi\nu_n$ gives (5.3a). The spacing $\nu_{n+1}-\nu_n$ differs from $1/(2\tau)$ by at most the sum of the two remainders, which is also of order $1/\nu_n$. ∎

Equation (5.3a) shows that the departure from $n/(2\tau)$ decreases as $1/\nu_n$, consistent with the second-order correction in equation (5.4). A spherical star adds a regularity condition at the centre and an upper turning point set by the acoustic cutoff. Tassoul’s asymptotic analysis gives the corresponding spectrum for radial order $n$ and angular degree $\ell$.

$$ \nu_{n,\ell} \;\simeq\; \left(n + \frac{\ell}{2} + \varepsilon\right)\Delta\nu_{\rm as}, \qquad \Delta\nu_{\rm as} \;=\; \left(2\int_0^R\frac{dr}{c}\right)^{-1} \;=\; \frac{1}{2\tau}, \tag{5.4}$$

with a phase constant $\varepsilon$, plus a second-order term in $1/\nu$. The boundaries change $\varepsilon$ and the second-order term, as the free-surface condition did in Proposition 4. The spacing stays $1/(2\tau)$, the result that Propositions 4 and 5 prove in the smaller settings.

Relation (5.4) is quoted, not derived for a sphere. It is stated in this form, with $\Delta\nu_{\rm as} = (2\int_0^R dr/c)^{-1}$, as equations (1) and (7) of Mosser et al. (2013), who attribute it to equations 65–74 of Tassoul (1980). It is trusted as standard asymptotic theory of stellar oscillations, valid for high radial order. The Sun's modes near $\nu_{\max}$ have radial orders near $n = \nu_{\max}/\Delta\nu \approx 23$.

A measured spacing is not exactly $\Delta\nu_{\rm as}$. The value (1.2) is an average of $\nu_{n+1}-\nu_n$ over the modes near $\nu_{\max}$, where the second-order term of Tassoul's expansion still bends the spacing. Mosser et al. (2013) fit that bending across many stars and give

$$ \Delta\nu_{\rm as} \;=\; (1+\zeta)\,\Delta\nu_{\rm obs}, \tag{5.5}$$

with a solar value $\zeta_\odot\simeq 2.6$ per cent. The asymptotic spacing of the Sun is therefore larger than the measured one, $1.026\times135.1 = 138.61\ \mu$Hz. Both values are carried through §7, and each conclusion there is stated for both.

The correction $\zeta_\odot\simeq2.6$ per cent is stated in section 4.2 of Mosser et al. (2013) without the step that produces it. Their main-sequence fit (equation 20), $\zeta = 0.57/n_{\max}$ with coefficient $0.57\pm0.02$ and $n_{\max} = \nu_{\max}/\Delta\nu_{\rm obs}$, gives $2.49$ per cent with the values of (1.2). The $0.1$ percentage-point difference changes no conclusion below. They give no separate uncertainty for the solar value; its trust is that of an empirical fit, and the checks below are built so that their conclusions do not depend on whether $\zeta$ is applied.

6. The acoustic radius of the tabulated solar model

The comparison object is the standard solar model BS2005-AGS,OP of Bahcall, Serenelli & Basu (2005), used in Module 3 and stored as data/bs05_agsop.dat. Its $1284$ rows give $P$ and $\rho$ from $r = 0.00161\,R$ to $r = 0.98308\,R$, with $R = 6.9598\times10^{10}$ cm. They do not give $\Gamma_1$, so the sound speed must be computed from an assumed closure, and that is what makes the table a test of closures.

Integrating (5.1) over the table, from its first to its last row, with the trapezoid rule and adding $r_0/c_0 = 2.2$ s for the innermost $0.00161\,R$, gives

$$ \tau_{\rm tab} \;=\; \int_0^{0.98308R}\frac{dr}{c} \;=\; \begin{cases} 2963.2\ \mathrm{s}, & c^2 = \tfrac{5}{3}P/\rho,\\[2pt] 3825.4\ \mathrm{s}, & c^2 = P/\rho. \end{cases} \tag{6.1}$$

Simpson’s rule gives $2963.15$ s, while the trapezoidal rule gives $2963.16$ s. Halving the number of rows changes the result to $2963.20$ s, so numerical quadrature contributes less than $0.1$ s. The isothermal-to-adiabatic ratio is $3825.4/2963.2=1.2910=\sqrt{5/3}$, as required by the constant sound-speed factor.

The table stops $11{,}776$ km below the surface, $1.69$ per cent of the radius. The outer layer is omitted, and the sound speed there is lowest, so it contributes a crossing time out of proportion to its thickness. Fig. 2 shows this on a logarithmic depth axis. What is known about the layer without a model is only its sign: every part of it adds a positive time $dr/c$ to $\tau$. The whole of §7 rests on that sign.

The model table is trusted as in Module 3: as a faithful copy of BS2005-AGS,OP, whose surface helium matches the paper, and not as a description of the Sun. Its $P$ and $\rho$ are the model's. The sound speed computed here is not the model's own sound speed, because the model's $\Gamma_1$ is not tabulated. The radius used is the table's own constant.

7. The solar checks

The measured spacing (1.2) implies an acoustic radius $\tau = 1/(2\Delta\nu)$. With $\Delta\nu_{\rm obs} = 135.1\pm0.1\ \mu$Hz it is $3701.0\pm2.7$ s. With the asymptotic value $138.61\ \mu$Hz of (5.5) it is $3607.2$ s. Each check compares a closure with these two totals.

7.1 Check 2: the isothermal solar model

An isothermal closure makes $c^2 = P/\rho$ at every depth. By (6.1), the tabulated interior alone then takes $3825.4$ s to cross. The untabulated layer adds a positive time, so the whole star takes longer, and

$$ \Delta\nu_{\rm iso} \;\le\; \frac{1}{2\times3825.4\ \mathrm{s}} \;=\; 130.70\ \mu\mathrm{Hz}. \tag{7.1}$$
Check 2: the standard solar stratification excludes the isothermal closure. With the tabulated $P$ and $\rho$, an isothermal Sun requires $\Delta\nu\le130.70\ \mu$Hz. The measured spacing is $135.1\ \mu$Hz, $3.25$ per cent above this bound. The asymptotic value, $138.61\ \mu$Hz, exceeds it by $5.71$ per cent.

The conclusion does not depend on the second-order correction. Mosser et al.'s correction moves the comparison value away from the bound. A correction of the opposite sign would have to exceed $3.25$ per cent to save the isothermal closure, and the measured statistical uncertainty is $0.07$ per cent. The strength of the check comes from its being a bound. A best estimate for the layer would need a model of the layer; an upper limit needs only the fact that crossing times add.

This comparison excludes the isothermal closure for the measured spacing and the standard model's pressure and density profiles. It does not exclude every conceivable structure with an isothermal sound speed. The dry-air experiment and the solar bound nevertheless reject the same closure under two explicitly stated sets of conditions.

7.2 Check 3: the adiabatic interior, consistent

With $c^2=\tfrac53P/\rho$, the tabulated interior contributes $2963.2$ s. The corresponding bound $\Delta\nu\le168.74\ \mu$Hz lies above both the measured and asymptotic spacings, so it does not exclude the adiabatic closure. The outer layers must supply the remaining acoustic travel time:

$$ \tau_{\rm layer} \;=\; \tau - \tau_{\rm tab} \;=\; \begin{cases} 3607.2 - 2963.2 \;=\; 644.0\ \mathrm{s}, & \Delta\nu_{\rm as},\\[2pt] 3701.0 - 2963.2 \;=\; 737.8\ \mathrm{s}, & \Delta\nu_{\rm obs}. \end{cases} \tag{7.2}$$
Check 3: the required outer-layer travel time is physically admissible. With $\Gamma_1=5/3$ in the tabulated interior, the outer $1.69$ per cent of the radius contributes $644.0$ s of the $3607.2$ s asymptotic acoustic radius. Using the measured spacing gives $737.8$ s of $3701.0$ s. Both remainders are positive, as the closure requires, but positivity alone does not establish that the closure is accurate near the photosphere.

A positive outer-layer remainder is only a weak constraint. Any closure with an interior crossing time below the measured total would satisfy it. The relevant question is whether a physically motivated outer-layer model supplies the required $644$ s; §7.3 evaluates one such model.

Adiabatic propagation also requires radiative diffusion to be slow, $\omega\chi/c^2\ll1$. This module does not evaluate that ratio through the solar envelope. It should hold in the deep interior and fail near the radiating photosphere, but Module 13 must quantify the transition.

7.3 Check 4: a perfect gas in the outer layer falls short

To estimate the missing $644$ s, model the untabulated outer layer between depth $D=11{,}776$ km, where $T=81{,}200$ K, and the photosphere, where $T=5772$ K. Assume that temperature varies linearly with depth. This form follows from a polytropic layer with constant $\mu$ and $g$. With uniform $\Gamma_1$ and $\mu$, the sound speed obeys $c^2=\Gamma_1k_BT/(\mu m_u)$, allowing the crossing time to be integrated analytically.

$$ \tau_{\rm layer} \;=\; \int_0^{D}\frac{dz}{c} \;=\; \frac{2\left(\sqrt{T_{\rm top}} - \sqrt{T_{\rm eff}}\right)}{\sigma\,\sqrt{\Gamma_1k_B/(\mu m_u)}}, \qquad \sigma \;=\; \frac{T_{\rm top}-T_{\rm eff}}{D}, \tag{7.3}$$

which follows from $dz = dT/\sigma$ and $\int T^{-1/2}dT = 2T^{1/2}$.

The adopted linear profile has slope $6.41\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$. A fully ionised $n=3/2$ adiabat gives $7.78\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$, whereas a neutral adiabat gives $16.15\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$. The tabulated endpoint is therefore closer to the ionised case. The actual Sun contains a thin superadiabatic region, so the linear profile remains an approximation whose crossing-time bias is not determined here.

C1. Criterion (3.4) says that sound in air becomes isothermal when $\omega\chi/c^2$ approaches one. Find the frequency and wavelength at which that happens, compare the wavelength with the mean free path of air, and explain why an isothermal sound wave in air is never observed in the continuum regime.
Solution
Setting $\omega\chi/c^2 = 1$ gives $\nu = c^2/(2\pi\chi)$. With $c = 3.3132\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$ and $\chi = 0.1870\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$, $\nu = 9.34\times10^{8}$ Hz and the wavelength is $c/\nu = 355$ nm, $5.22$ times the mean free path of $68$ nm. The length that enters (3.4) is $\ell = 1/|\mathbf{k}| = \lambda/2\pi = 56.4$ nm, and the Knudsen number $\lambda_{\rm mfp}/\ell$ is $1.20$. The $68$ nm is Module 1's room-air value; at $0\ ^\circ$C the mean free path is smaller, near $63$ nm by the ratio of temperatures, which moves the Knudsen number to about $1.1$ and changes no conclusion. Module 1 requires this number to be small for a continuum description. The coincidence is not accidental. Kinetic theory gives a diffusivity of order $\chi\sim\lambda_{\rm mfp}\bar v/3$, with $\bar v$ the mean molecular speed, which is of the order of $c$. Then $\omega\chi/c^2\sim\omega\lambda_{\rm mfp}/(3c) = |\mathbf{k}|\lambda_{\rm mfp}/3$, a Knudsen number. Heat can cross a wavelength within a period only when the wavelength is comparable with the mean free path, and there the continuum equations from which both closures were derived no longer hold. This estimate is order-of-magnitude only. The factors of order one are not computed.
C2. The solar five-minute modes near $\nu_{\max} = 3090\ \mu$Hz are called evanescent above the photosphere, yet (4.2) shows their velocity amplitude increasing with height in an isothermal atmosphere at $T_{\rm eff}$. Reconcile the two statements with numbers, and say which quantity decides whether the atmosphere traps the mode.
Solution
With $\nu_{\rm ac} = 4497\ \mu$Hz and $c = 8.081\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, (4.5) gives $\kappa = 2.540\ \mathrm{Mm^{-1}}$ at $3090\ \mu$Hz. The velocity is $u = e^{z/2H}\psi$ with $\psi\propto e^{-\kappa z}$, so $u\propto e^{(1/2H-\kappa)z}$. With $H = 143.0$ km, $1/2H = 3.497\ \mathrm{Mm^{-1}}$, and the exponent is $+0.956\ \mathrm{Mm^{-1}}$: the velocity grows. The energy density is $\tfrac12\rho_0u^2\propto e^{-z/H}e^{z/H}e^{-2\kappa z} = e^{-2\kappa z}$, which decays with an e-folding length $1/2\kappa = 196.8$ km, or $1.376\,H$. Trapping is decided by the energy. A standing mode carries no net energy flux upward, and the energy it stores above the photosphere falls off in about $200$ km. The velocity grows only because the density it multiplies falls faster.
C3. Check 2 refutes the isothermal closure with an upper bound on $\Delta\nu$, while Check 4 uses a best estimate of the outer layer and reaches a weaker conclusion. Explain why the bound needs no model of the layer, and state one change to the inputs that would weaken Check 2, and say what the same change does to Check 4.
Solution
The bound needs only that $dr/c\gt0$ for every $r$ in the layer, which holds for any real sound speed. So the tabulated $3825.4$ s is a lower limit on $\tau$ and $130.70\ \mu$Hz an upper limit on $\Delta\nu$, whatever the layer contains. Check 4 needs a temperature profile, a $\mu$ and a $\Gamma_1$ in the layer, and its conclusion is conditional on them. A change that weakens Check 2 but not Check 4 is a second-order correction of the opposite sign to Mosser et al.'s, with $\Delta\nu_{\rm as}$ below $\Delta\nu_{\rm obs}$ by more than $3.25$ per cent. Then the required $\tau$ would exceed $3825.4$ s and the isothermal bound would no longer exclude it. The same change would raise the time the outer layer must supply above $862$ s, which a perfect gas with $\Gamma_1 = 5/3$ falls even further short of, so Check 4 would be strengthened, not weakened.

Derivational ↑ contents

D1. For a uniform gas, show from (2.6)–(2.8) that the acoustic energy density $E = \tfrac12\rho_0|\mathbf{u}_1|^2 + P_1^2/(2\rho_0c^2)$ and the flux $\mathbf{F} = P_1\mathbf{u}_1$ satisfy $\partial E/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 0$. Then use the substitution of Proposition 3 to find how the velocity and density amplitudes of a propagating wave change over $500$ and $1000$ km of the solar atmosphere.
Solution
With $\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{0}$ and uniform equilibrium, (2.7) is $\rho_0\partial\mathbf{u}_1/\partial t = -\nabla P_1$, and (2.6) with (2.8) gives $\partial P_1/\partial t = c^2\partial\rho_1/\partial t = -\rho_0c^2\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}_1$. Then $$ \frac{\partial E}{\partial t} = \rho_0\mathbf{u}_1\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_1}{\partial t} + \frac{P_1}{\rho_0c^2}\frac{\partial P_1}{\partial t} = -\mathbf{u}_1\cdot\nabla P_1 - P_1\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}_1 = -\nabla\cdot(P_1\mathbf{u}_1). $$ In the stratified atmosphere, a propagating wave has $|\psi|$ constant, so $|u|\propto e^{z/2H}$. With $H = 143.0$ km, the velocity grows by $e^{500/286} = 5.7$ over $500$ km and by $33.0$ over $1000$ km. The equilibrium density falls by $e^{-z/H}$: $3.03\times10^{-2}$ and $9.18\times10^{-4}$. The density perturbation follows from continuity (2.6): with $u = e^{z/2H}\psi$ and $\psi\propto e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$, $\partial\rho_1/\partial t = \rho_0u\,(1/2H - ik)$, so $|\rho_1|\propto\rho_0|u|\propto e^{-z/2H}$. It falls by $e^{-500/286} = 0.174$ and $e^{-1000/286} = 3.03\times10^{-2}$, while the fractional perturbation $|\rho_1|/\rho_0$ grows by $5.7$ and $33.0$. The product $\rho_0|u|^2$ is unchanged, consistent with a wave whose energy flux is constant with height. The stratified energy equation itself is not derived here.
D2. Show that (4.6) can be written $\nu_{\rm ac} = \Gamma_1g/(4\pi c)$ and hence that, at fixed $\Gamma_1$ and $\mu$, $\nu_{\rm ac}\propto g\,T^{-1/2}$. Evaluate both forms for the Sun and confirm that they agree.
Solution
From (4.1), $c^2 = \Gamma_1gH$, so $H = c^2/(\Gamma_1g)$ and $c/(4\pi H) = c\,\Gamma_1g/(4\pi c^2) = \Gamma_1g/(4\pi c)$. With $c = (\Gamma_1k_BT/\mu m_u)^{1/2}$, $\nu_{\rm ac} = (\Gamma_1\mu m_u/k_B)^{1/2}\,g\,T^{-1/2}/(4\pi)$, which at fixed $\Gamma_1$ and $\mu$ is proportional to $gT^{-1/2}$. For the Sun, $\Gamma_1 = 5/3$, $g = 2.7398\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}$ and $c = 8.081\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$ give $\Gamma_1g/(4\pi c) = 4496.9\ \mu$Hz, and $c/(4\pi H)$ with $H = 143.0$ km gives $4496.9\ \mu$Hz. The two agree to rounding, as they must, since they are the same expression.
D3. Two stars are homologous if one is the other with every length scaled by $R$ and every mass by $M$. Using hydrostatic equilibrium, show that for homologous stars with the same $\Gamma_1$ profile, $\Delta\nu_{\rm as}\propto(G\bar\rho)^{1/2}$ with $\bar\rho = 3M/(4\pi R^3)$. Evaluate $\Delta\nu/\sqrt{G\bar\rho}$ for the Sun with both the measured and the asymptotic spacing, and use the measured ratio to estimate $\Delta\nu$ for a star of $1.2\,M_\odot$ and $10\,R_\odot$, stating the assumption.
Solution
Module 3's (2.4) gives $dP/dr = -Gm\rho/r^2$. Under homology write $r = Rx$, $m = M\hat m(x)$, $\rho = (M/R^3)\hat\rho(x)$ with $\hat m$ and $\hat\rho$ the same functions for both stars. Then $P = (GM^2/R^4)\hat P(x)$, and $P/\rho = (GM/R)\hat P/\hat\rho$. So $c^2 = \Gamma_1P/\rho = (GM/R)\,\hat c(x)^2$, and $$ \tau = \int_0^R\frac{dr}{c} = \left(\frac{R^3}{GM}\right)^{1/2}\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\hat c(x)} \;\propto\; (G\bar\rho)^{-1/2}, $$ so $\Delta\nu_{\rm as} = 1/(2\tau)\propto(G\bar\rho)^{1/2}$. For the Sun with the table's radius, $\bar\rho = 1.4081\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $\sqrt{G\bar\rho} = 3.0656\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. The ratio is $0.4407$ with $135.1\ \mu$Hz and $0.4522$ with $138.61\ \mu$Hz. A star of $1.2\,M_\odot$ and $10\,R_\odot$ has $\bar\rho = 1.6897\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, and scaling the measured solar value gives $\Delta\nu = 135.1\times(1.6897\times10^{-3}/1.4081)^{1/2} = 4.68\ \mu$Hz. The assumption is homology with the Sun, which a red giant, with its dense core and extended envelope, does not satisfy. The estimate shows the scaling, not a prediction for a real giant.

K1. Compute Newton's and Laplace's speeds of sound in helium at $273.15$ K, taking $\mu = 4.002602$ and $\gamma = 5/3$. Give their ratio and the ratio of Laplace's speed in helium to that in dry air, and check the second ratio against a closed form.
Solution
From (1.1), $c_{\rm N} = (k_BT/\mu m_u)^{1/2} = 753.26\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. From (3.3), $c_{\rm L} = \sqrt{5/3}\,c_{\rm N} = 972.46\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Their ratio is $1.2910 = \sqrt{5/3}$, larger than air's $\sqrt{7/5} = 1.1832$ because a monatomic gas stores no energy in rotation, so more of the work of compression raises the pressure. The ratio to dry air's $331.32\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ is $2.9351$. At equal temperature, $c_{\rm L}\propto(\gamma/\mu)^{1/2}$, so the ratio is $[(5/3)/(7/5)\times28.9647/4.002602]^{1/2} = 2.9351$, which agrees. The molecular weight of helium is the standard atomic weight, an input not checked against a source here. No measured speed in helium is compared.
K2. A red giant has $M = 1.2\,M_\odot$, $R = 10\,R_\odot$ and $T_{\rm eff} = 4800$ K. Taking $\Gamma_1 = 5/3$ and the neutral $\mu = 1.2250$, compute $g$, $c$, $H$ and $\nu_{\rm ac}$ at $T_{\rm eff}$. Check the ratio to the solar value against the scaling of Problem D2, and estimate $\nu_{\max}$ by assuming it is the same fraction of the computed cutoff (4.8) as in the Sun.
Solution
With the table's $R_\odot = 6.9598\times10^{10}$ cm, $g = 1.2\,GM_\odot/(10R_\odot)^2 = 328.8\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}$. At $4800$ K, $c = 7.369\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, $H = 9910$ km, and $\nu_{\rm ac} = c/(4\pi H) = 59.2\ \mu$Hz. The solar value from (4.8) is $4497\ \mu$Hz, so the ratio is $0.01316$. The scaling $\nu_{\rm ac}\propto gT^{-1/2}$ gives $(328.8/27398)\times(5772/4800)^{1/2} = 0.01316$, which agrees. Taking $\nu_{\max}$ as the same fraction of the computed cutoff as in the Sun, $\nu_{\max} = 3090\times0.01316 = 40.7\ \mu$Hz, a period of $6.83$ hours. The stellar parameters are representative round values, not those of a named star. The assumption that $\nu_{\max}$ is a fixed fraction of $\nu_{\rm ac}$ is the scaling $\nu_{\max}\propto\nu_{\rm ac}$ that Mosser et al. (2013) cite to Belkacem et al. (2011); with the measured fraction $3090/5000$ instead, the estimate would be $36.6\ \mu$Hz. It is used here as an input.
K3. For the isothermal atmosphere of Fig. 1, compute the decay rate $\kappa$ and the energy e-folding length $1/2\kappa$ for modes at $3090$ and $2000\ \mu$Hz, and express each length in units of $H$. Which mode is trapped more tightly, and why does the answer approach a limit as $\nu\to0$?
Solution
From (4.5) with $\omega_{\rm ac} = 2\pi\times4497\ \mu$Hz and $c = 8.081\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$: at $3090\ \mu$Hz, $\kappa = 2.540\ \mathrm{Mm^{-1}}$ and $1/2\kappa = 196.8$ km $= 1.376\,H$; at $2000\ \mu$Hz, $\kappa = 3.132\ \mathrm{Mm^{-1}}$ and $1/2\kappa = 159.7$ km $= 1.117\,H$. The lower-frequency mode is trapped more tightly, because it lies further below the cutoff. As $\nu\to0$, $\kappa\to\omega_{\rm ac}/c = 1/2H = 3.497\ \mathrm{Mm^{-1}}$, so $1/2\kappa\to H$. The energy of the most deeply evanescent wave falls off over one scale height, the same length over which the equilibrium pressure falls.
