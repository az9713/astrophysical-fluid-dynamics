1. From a kinetic distribution to five fluid fields

Module 1 established when collisions maintain a nearly Maxwellian local velocity distribution. We now derive the continuum equations by taking velocity moments of the Boltzmann equation. This procedure yields exact conservation laws, together with a hierarchy that must be closed by additional physical assumptions.

The starting point is the object Module 1 introduced but never solved for. A gas of identical particles of mass $m$ is described completely by its distribution function $f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)$, which obeys the Boltzmann equation, equation (2.1) of Module 1:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} \;+\; \mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} f \;+\; \mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}} f \;=\; \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\right)_{\!\mathrm{coll}}. \tag{1.1}$$

Here $\mathbf{x}$ is position, $\mathbf{v}$ is particle velocity, $t$ is time, and $\mathbf{a}$ is the acceleration produced by any smooth external force, gravity in most of this book. The right-hand side is the collision term, which we will write $C[f]$ from now on.

The distribution function depends on three position coordinates, three velocity coordinates and time. Its collision term also contains high-dimensional velocity integrals. Solving this kinetic equation directly is unnecessary for many macroscopic flows, but reducing it to fluid variables requires a controlled approximation.

A fluid description uses five local scalar fields: mass density, three components of bulk velocity and temperature. These fields depend only on position and time. The reduction from a seven-variable distribution function is substantial, but the resulting moment equations do not close automatically.

The procedure that performs it is called taking moments. Multiply (1.1) by a function of velocity and integrate over all velocities; what survives is an equation among the reduced fields. Doing this with the three quantities that a collision cannot change produces exactly the four equations this module is named after. The cost appears immediately and never goes away: each moment equation contains the next moment, so the set of equations is never closed by itself. Closing it requires an extra assumption, and the quality of that assumption is what separates the Euler equations from the Navier–Stokes equations.

Sections 2–4 define velocity moments, identify the five collision invariants and derive the general moment equation. Sections 5–8 obtain the conservation laws for mass, momentum and energy. Section 9 introduces the closures leading to the Euler and Navier–Stokes equations. Section 10 then tests mass conservation against solar-wind measurements.

2. Moments of the distribution function

Every fluid variable in this book is an average over the local velocity distribution. This section defines them once, with their units, so that no later equation contains an undefined symbol.

Definition 1 (distribution function). $f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)\,d^3x\,d^3v$ is the number of particles at time $t$ occupying the volume $d^3x$ about $\mathbf{x}$ and the velocity-space volume $d^3v$ about $\mathbf{v}$. Its units are $\mathrm{cm^{-6}\,s^{3}}$, and it is non-negative.
Definition 2 (velocity average). For any function $\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)$, the local average is $$ \langle \psi\rangle(\mathbf{x},t) \;\equiv\; \frac{1}{n(\mathbf{x},t)}\int \psi\, f\, d^3v, \qquad n(\mathbf{x},t) \;\equiv\; \int f\, d^3v, \tag{2.1}$$ in which $n$ is the number density in $\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and the integral runs over all of velocity space. The mass density is $\rho \equiv m n$, in $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.

The first moment of the velocity itself defines the flow field.

Definition 3 (bulk velocity and peculiar velocity). The bulk velocity is $\mathbf{u} \equiv \langle\mathbf{v}\rangle$, in $\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$. The peculiar velocity of a particle is its departure from the local bulk motion, $$ \mathbf{w} \;\equiv\; \mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t), \qquad\text{so that}\qquad \langle\mathbf{w}\rangle \;=\; \mathbf{0} \tag{2.2}$$ identically, by the definition of $\mathbf{u}$.

The distinction between $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$ is the single most important bookkeeping step in this module. A particle's velocity contains the motion of the gas as a whole plus a random part. Bulk motion carries momentum and kinetic energy from place to place; random motion is what we call pressure and heat. Every fluid equation below is what appears after those two contributions have been separated.

Definition 4 (pressure tensor and scalar pressure). The pressure tensor is the flux of peculiar momentum carried by peculiar motion, $$ P_{ij} \;\equiv\; \rho\,\langle w_i w_j\rangle \;=\; m\int w_i w_j\, f\, d^3v, \tag{2.3}$$ in $\mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$. It is symmetric, $P_{ij}=P_{ji}$, because $w_iw_j = w_jw_i$. The scalar pressure is one third of its trace, $$ p \;\equiv\; \tfrac{1}{3}P_{ii} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{3}\rho\,\langle |\mathbf{w}|^2\rangle, \tag{2.4}$$ with the repeated index summed, and the kinetic temperature is defined by $p \equiv n k_B T$.
Definition 5 (heat flux). The heat flux is the flux of peculiar kinetic energy carried by peculiar motion, $$ \mathbf{q} \;\equiv\; \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,\langle |\mathbf{w}|^2\,\mathbf{w}\rangle \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}m\int |\mathbf{w}|^2\,\mathbf{w}\, f\, d^3v, \tag{2.5}$$ in $\mathrm{erg\,cm^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$.

Two remarks make the later algebra shorter. First, $P_{ij}$ and $\mathbf{q}$ are defined for any $f$, not only a Maxwellian; nothing so far assumes the gas is near equilibrium. Second, if $f$ is the local Maxwellian $f_M$ of Module 1, equation (2.2), then $P_{ij}=p\,\delta_{ij}$ and $\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}$ exactly. Both follow from $f_M$ depending on $\mathbf{w}$ only through $|\mathbf{w}|$, but they need two different steps and it is worth separating them. The off-diagonal components of (2.3) vanish because for $i\neq j$ the integrand $w_iw_jf_M(|\mathbf{w}|)$ changes sign under $w_i\to-w_i$ while nothing else in the integral does, so the integral equals its own negative. The heat flux vanishes for the same reason, its integrand in (2.5) changing sign under $\mathbf{w}\to-\mathbf{w}$. The three diagonal components need a different argument, because $w_i^2$ is even and oddness says nothing about it. There the relevant fact is that relabelling the coordinate axes leaves an integral over $f_M(|\mathbf{w}|)$ unchanged, so $\langle w_1^2\rangle=\langle w_2^2\rangle=\langle w_3^2\rangle$; each is therefore $\tfrac{1}{3}\langle|\mathbf{w}|^2\rangle$, which is $p/\rho$ by (2.4). The two steps together give $P_{ij}=p\,\delta_{ij}$. This is the fact §9 will lean on.

One identity is used repeatedly and is worth recording now.

Proposition 1 (equipartition identity). For any distribution function whatsoever, $$ \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,\langle|\mathbf{w}|^2\rangle \;=\; \tfrac{3}{2}p, \tag{2.6}$$ so the specific internal energy $e \equiv \tfrac{1}{2}\langle|\mathbf{w}|^2\rangle$ satisfies $\rho e = \tfrac{3}{2}p$.
By (2.4), $p=\tfrac{1}{3}\rho\langle|\mathbf{w}|^2\rangle$, so $\rho\langle|\mathbf{w}|^2\rangle = 3p$ and half of it is $\tfrac{3}{2}p$. The result is an identity, not a thermodynamic statement: it follows from the definition of $p$ as one third of the trace of $P_{ij}$ and holds however far $f$ is from equilibrium. What is a physical assumption is the identification $p = nk_BT$ with a thermodynamic temperature, which requires the gas to be close enough to a Maxwellian for the concept to mean anything. Module 1 quantified "close enough" as $\mathrm{Kn}\ll 1$. ∎

For a monatomic gas, (2.6) is the statement that the internal energy per particle is $\tfrac{3}{2}k_BT$, one half of $k_BT$ for each of the three translational degrees of freedom. Writing it as $\rho e = p/(\gamma-1)$ with $\gamma=5/3$ is the same statement; §8 uses that form.

3. The five collision invariants

The moment procedure can be applied with any function $\psi(\mathbf{v})$. It produces an exact equation — one with no residual collision term to model — only for the special functions that a collision leaves unchanged. There are exactly five of them, and that is why there are exactly five conservation laws.

Definition 6 (collision invariant). A function $\psi(\mathbf{v})$ is a collision invariant if, for every binary elastic collision taking a pair of velocities $(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}_1)$ to $(\mathbf{v}',\mathbf{v}_1')$, $$ \psi(\mathbf{v}) + \psi(\mathbf{v}_1) \;=\; \psi(\mathbf{v}') + \psi(\mathbf{v}_1'). \tag{3.1}$$
Proposition 2 (the collision term is blind to its invariants). If $\psi$ satisfies (3.1), then for every $f$ $$ \int \psi(\mathbf{v})\, C[f]\, d^3v \;=\; 0. \tag{3.2}$$ The collision invariants of a simple gas of structureless particles are exactly the five functions $$ \psi \;\in\; \{\,1,\;\; m v_x,\;\; m v_y,\;\; m v_z,\;\; \tfrac{1}{2}m|\mathbf{v}|^2\,\}, \tag{3.3}$$ and every other invariant is a linear combination of these.
Write the Boltzmann collision term in its standard bilinear form, $$ C[f] \;=\; \int\!\!\int \left(f'f_1' - f f_1\right) g\, \sigma(g,\Omega)\, d\Omega\, d^3v_1, $$ in which $f\equiv f(\mathbf{v})$, $f_1\equiv f(\mathbf{v}_1)$, $f'\equiv f(\mathbf{v}')$, $f_1'\equiv f(\mathbf{v}_1')$, $g=|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_1|$ is the relative speed, and $\sigma$ is the differential cross-section. Multiply by $\psi(\mathbf{v})$ and integrate over $\mathbf{v}$. Two transformations of the integration variables leave the measure $g\,\sigma\,d\Omega\,d^3v\,d^3v_1$ unchanged. Call the first $A$: swap the labels of the two incoming particles, $\mathbf{v}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{v}_1$, and correspondingly $\mathbf{v}'\leftrightarrow\mathbf{v}_1'$. It is a relabelling of a symmetric double integral and needs no physical input. Call the second $B$: replace each collision by its time reverse, $(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}_1)\leftrightarrow(\mathbf{v}',\mathbf{v}_1')$. This one does need physical input. It leaves the measure unchanged because elastic collisions are reversible and the Jacobian of the transformation is unity, the property called microscopic reversibility, which is an assumption about the interaction and not a mathematical identity. Each of $A$ and $B$ is its own inverse, so the two generate a group of exactly four transformations: the identity, $A$, $B$, and $AB$. The integral is unchanged by every one of them, so it equals the average of its four images. That average is where the factor $\tfrac{1}{4}$ comes from: $$ \int \psi\, C[f]\, d^3v \;=\; \tfrac{1}{4}\int\!\!\int\!\!\int \left(\psi+\psi_1-\psi'-\psi_1'\right)\left(f'f_1'-ff_1\right) g\,\sigma\, d\Omega\, d^3v_1\, d^3v. $$ The first bracket vanishes identically when $\psi$ obeys (3.1), so the whole integral vanishes. That proves (3.2). For the converse — that (3.3) exhausts the invariants — note that an elastic collision between structureless particles of equal mass conserves exactly the number of particles, the total momentum and the total kinetic energy, and nothing else. Any $\psi$ obeying (3.1) for all collision geometries is therefore a linear combination of $1$, the three components of $m\mathbf{v}$, and $\tfrac{1}{2}m|\mathbf{v}|^2$. The full proof of this last step is Boltzmann's, and is a functional-equation argument. We take it as given. ∎

The consequence is worth stating plainly, because it is the structural reason this book has four equations and not five or three. The number of exact fluid conservation laws equals the number of collision invariants. The five independent collision invariants yield five scalar conservation equations: one for mass, three for momentum and one for energy. Had the particles possessed internal degrees of freedom that collisions could excite, the energy invariant would change form and the adiabatic index would change with it. Had collisions been able to destroy particles, there would be no mass equation.

Choosing any other $\psi$ — the pressure tensor's $\psi = m w_iw_j$, for example — produces an equation with a surviving collision term on the right. Such an equation is not wrong, and Chapman–Enskog theory uses exactly that kind of equation to compute the viscosity. But it is not a conservation law, and it cannot be written down without modelling the collisions.

4. The moment hierarchy and the closure problem

We now perform the moment operation once, in general, and read off the structure of the result. Everything in §5 to §8 is a special case.

Proposition 3 (Maxwell's equation of transfer). Let $\psi(\mathbf{v})$ depend on velocity alone and be a collision invariant. Suppose the acceleration field satisfies $\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{a}=0$ and $f\to 0$ faster than any power of $|\mathbf{v}|$ as $|\mathbf{v}|\to\infty$. Then $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big(n\langle\psi\rangle\Big) \;+\; \nabla\cdot\Big(n\langle\mathbf{v}\,\psi\rangle\Big) \;=\; n\,\big\langle \mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\psi\big\rangle. \tag{4.1}$$
Multiply the Boltzmann equation (1.1) by $\psi(\mathbf{v})$ and integrate over all velocities, taking the three terms on the left in turn. The first term. Because $\psi$ does not depend on $t$, the time derivative passes outside the velocity integral: $$ \int \psi\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\, d^3v \;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int \psi f\, d^3v \;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(n\langle\psi\rangle\big). $$ The second term. Because $\psi$ and $\mathbf{v}$ do not depend on $\mathbf{x}$, and because $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are independent coordinates of phase space, the spatial divergence passes outside as well: $$ \int \psi\,\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f\, d^3v \;=\; \nabla\cdot\int \psi\,\mathbf{v} f\, d^3v \;=\; \nabla\cdot\big(n\langle\mathbf{v}\psi\rangle\big). $$ The third term is the one that does work. Integrate by parts in velocity space: $$ \int \psi\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}f\, d^3v \;=\; \int \nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\big(\psi\,\mathbf{a}\,f\big)\, d^3v \;-\; \int f\,\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\big(\psi\,\mathbf{a}\big)\, d^3v. $$ The first integral on the right is the divergence of a vector field over the whole of velocity space, and by the divergence theorem equals a surface integral at $|\mathbf{v}|\to\infty$, which vanishes because $f$ decays faster than any power. In the second integral, $\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot(\psi\mathbf{a}) = \psi\,\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\psi$, and the first piece vanishes by hypothesis. So the third term equals $-n\langle\mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\psi\rangle$, and moving it to the right-hand side gives the sign in (4.1). The right-hand side of (1.1) contributes $\int\psi\,C[f]\,d^3v$, which is zero by Proposition 2. ∎

The hypothesis $\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{a}=0$ deserves a note, because it is the reason this derivation covers the two forces that matter in this book. For gravity, $\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})$ does not depend on $\mathbf{v}$ at all, so its velocity divergence is trivially zero. For the Lorentz force on a charge, $\mathbf{a} = (q/mc)\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ does depend on $\mathbf{v}$, but $\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}) = 0$ because the $i$th component of $\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ contains no $v_i$. Magnetohydrodynamics, in Module 12, therefore inherits (4.1) unchanged.

Equation (4.1) reveals the moment hierarchy. Its storage term contains $\langle\psi\rangle$, while its flux contains $\langle\mathbf v\psi\rangle$. Choosing $\psi=m$ introduces the bulk velocity. Choosing $\psi=m\mathbf v$ introduces the pressure tensor, and choosing kinetic energy introduces the heat flux. Each equation therefore depends on a higher velocity moment, as Fig. 1 illustrates.

5. Conservation of mass

Take $\psi = m$, a constant. It is the first entry of (3.3), so Proposition 3 applies.

Proposition 4 (continuity). For any gas obeying (1.1), whether or not it is close to equilibrium, $$ \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} \;+\; \nabla\cdot\big(\rho\mathbf{u}\big) \;=\; 0. \tag{5.1}$$
With $\psi=m$: the storage term is $n\langle m\rangle = mn = \rho$. The flux term is $n\langle m\mathbf{v}\rangle = mn\langle\mathbf{v}\rangle = \rho\mathbf{u}$ by Definition 3; and the source term is $n\langle\mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}m\rangle=0$ because $m$ is a constant, so its velocity gradient vanishes. Substituting into (4.1) gives (5.1). ∎

Three features of this result are worth naming, because they do not survive into the later equations.

First, no approximation was made. Equation (5.1) contains no closure assumption, no expansion in the Knudsen number, and no appeal to a Maxwellian. It is a consequence of counting particles and of the fact that collisions do not create or destroy them. A collisionless plasma obeys it; so does a galaxy, with "density" meaning the mass density of stars. This is why §10 can use it as a sharp test: a measured departure from (5.1) would mean the measurement is wrong, or that mass is entering the flow from outside it, and nothing else.

Second, the equation is exact but not closed. It relates two unknowns, $\rho$ and $\mathbf{u}$, and supplies one equation. The momentum equation is needed to determine $\mathbf{u}$.

Third, its structure is the standard form of every conservation law: a rate of change of a stored density, plus the divergence of its flux, equals zero. Integrating (5.1) over a fixed volume $V$ with surface $S$ and applying the divergence theorem gives the statement in its original form,

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\int_V \rho\, d^3x \;=\; -\oint_S \rho\,\mathbf{u}\cdot d\mathbf{S}, \tag{5.2}$$

the mass inside a fixed region changes only by flowing through its boundary.

6. The material derivative

Equation (5.1) is written in the frame of a fixed observer: $\partial\rho/\partial t$ is the rate of change of density at a fixed point in space. That is the Eulerian description. There is a second, equally natural choice — follow a particular parcel of fluid and ask how its properties change as it moves. That is the Lagrangian description, and the equations become substantially easier to interpret in it.

Definition 7 (Lagrangian derivative). The Lagrangian, material, or convective derivative of a field $\phi(\mathbf{x},t)$ is $$ \frac{D\phi}{Dt} \;\equiv\; \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} \;+\; \big(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\big)\phi. \tag{6.1}$$ It is the rate of change of $\phi$ measured by an observer carried along with the fluid at velocity $\mathbf{u}$.

The justification is the chain rule. Let $\mathbf{X}(t)$ be the path of a parcel, so $d\mathbf{X}/dt = \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}(t),t)$. Then the value of $\phi$ experienced by that parcel is $\phi(\mathbf{X}(t),t)$, and

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\,\phi\big(\mathbf{X}(t),t\big) \;=\; \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} \;+\; \frac{d\mathbf{X}}{dt}\cdot\nabla\phi \;=\; \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} \;+\; \big(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\big)\phi, \tag{6.2}$$

which is (6.1). The two terms have different physical meanings and it is worth keeping them apart. The first is genuine local change — the gas at this point is getting denser. The second is advection: a moving parcel experiences change as it crosses a spatial density gradient, even if the density at a fixed point is steady. Fig. 2 shows the distinction with a single flow.

Rewriting continuity in this language exposes its meaning.

Proposition 5 (continuity in Lagrangian form). Equation (5.1) is equivalent to $$ \frac{D\rho}{Dt} \;=\; -\rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}. \tag{6.3}$$ A flow is called incompressible when $D\rho/Dt=0$, which by (6.3) holds if and only if $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0$.
Expand the divergence in (5.1) by the product rule: $\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\rho + \rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}$. Then $$ 0 \;=\; \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\rho + \rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} \;=\; \frac{D\rho}{Dt} + \rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} $$ by Definition 7, which rearranges to (6.3). Since $\rho\gt 0$ for any real gas, $D\rho/Dt=0$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0$ imply one another. ∎

Equation (6.3) says that a parcel's density changes only when the flow converges or diverges around it, at a fractional rate equal to $-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}$. This is the correct reading of $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}$: it is the fractional rate at which a comoving volume element expands,

$$ \frac{1}{\delta V}\frac{D(\delta V)}{Dt} \;=\; \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}, \tag{6.4}$$

which follows from (6.3) and the constancy of the parcel's mass $\rho\,\delta V$.

Incompressible does not mean uniform. The condition is $D\rho/Dt = 0$, not $\nabla\rho = 0$. A stratified atmosphere in which every parcel keeps its own density while the density varies strongly with height is incompressible in this sense and not uniform in any sense. The confusion matters in Module 6, where convection is driven by exactly such a stratification. Note also that incompressibility is a statement about the flow, not about the substance: the same gas is compressible in a shock and effectively incompressible in a slow subsonic eddy, and the dividing line is the Mach number, which Module 4 derives.

7. Momentum conservation

Take $\psi = m v_i$, the second entry of (3.3). This is where the separation of bulk from peculiar velocity does its work.

Proposition 6 (the momentum equation, in conservative and Lagrangian form). For any gas obeying (1.1) under a gravitational acceleration $\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},t)$, $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(\rho u_i\big) \;+\; \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\Big(\rho u_i u_j + P_{ij}\Big) \;=\; \rho g_i, \tag{7.1}$$ and, after using continuity, equivalently $$ \rho\,\frac{D u_i}{Dt} \;=\; -\frac{\partial P_{ij}}{\partial x_j} \;+\; \rho g_i. \tag{7.2}$$ Equation (7.2) is the Cauchy momentum equation. Like continuity, it is exact: no closure has yet been assumed.
With $\psi = mv_i$, the storage term of (4.1) is $n\langle mv_i\rangle = \rho u_i$. The flux term is $n\langle m v_i v_j\rangle = \rho\langle v_iv_j\rangle$. Substituting $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}$ and expanding, $$ \langle v_iv_j\rangle \;=\; \langle (u_i+w_i)(u_j+w_j)\rangle \;=\; u_iu_j \;+\; u_i\langle w_j\rangle \;+\; u_j\langle w_i\rangle \;+\; \langle w_iw_j\rangle \;=\; u_iu_j + \langle w_iw_j\rangle, $$ because $\mathbf{u}$ is not a random variable and may be taken outside the average, and because $\langle\mathbf{w}\rangle=\mathbf{0}$ by (2.2) kills the two cross terms. Multiplying by $\rho$ and using Definition 4 gives $\rho\langle v_iv_j\rangle = \rho u_iu_j + P_{ij}$, which is the flux in (7.1). The source term is $n\langle \mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}(mv_i)\rangle$. Since $\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}(mv_i)$ is $m$ times the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_i$, this equals $n m\langle a_i\rangle = \rho g_i$ for an acceleration independent of velocity. That establishes (7.1). For (7.2), expand the left-hand side of (7.1) and subtract $u_i$ times continuity (5.1): $$ \rho\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t} + u_i\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \rho u_j\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + u_i\frac{\partial(\rho u_j)}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial P_{ij}}{\partial x_j} \;=\; \rho g_i. $$ The second and fourth terms are $u_i$ times the left side of (5.1) and therefore cancel, leaving $\rho(\partial_t u_i + u_j\partial_j u_i) = \rho\,Du_i/Dt$ on the left. ∎

Equation (7.2) is Newton’s second law per unit volume. The material acceleration $\rho Du_i/Dt$ is balanced by the surface force $-\partial_jP_{ij}$ and body force $\rho g_i$. The material derivative from §6 converts the motion of a fluid parcel into an Eulerian field equation.

The equation is still not usable, because $P_{ij}$ is six unknown functions and we have no equation for them. This is the closure gap of Fig. 1, arrived at concretely. Two closures are standard.

7.1 The Euler equation

If the distribution is exactly the local Maxwellian, $P_{ij}=p\,\delta_{ij}$, as noted after Definition 5. Module 1 showed that the departure from the local Maxwellian is of order $\mathrm{Kn}=\lambda/L$; so setting $P_{ij}=p\,\delta_{ij}$ is the leading term of an expansion in $\mathrm{Kn}$, correct up to a fractional error of that size.

Proposition 7 (Euler's equation). With the zeroth-order closure $P_{ij}=p\,\delta_{ij}$, equation (7.2) becomes $$ \boxed{\;\rho\,\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} \;=\; \rho\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\right] \;=\; -\nabla p \;+\; \rho\,\mathbf{g}\;} \tag{7.3}$$ with a fractional error of order $\mathrm{Kn}$.
Substitute $P_{ij}=p\,\delta_{ij}$ into (7.2). The divergence of the pressure tensor becomes $$ \frac{\partial P_{ij}}{\partial x_j} \;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\big(p\,\delta_{ij}\big) \;=\; \frac{\partial p}{\partial x_i}, $$ since $\delta_{ij}$ is constant and the sum over $j$ selects $j=i$. Writing the result in vector form gives (7.3), and expanding $D/Dt$ by Definition 7 gives the bracketed form. The error statement rests on a result already in hand, not on anything below. Module 1's Proposition 1 established that the departure of $f$ from the local Maxwellian is first order in $\mathrm{Kn}$. Since $P_{ij}$ is linear in $f$ by (2.3), and since the Maxwellian part of $f$ contributes exactly $p\,\delta_{ij}$ by the argument following Definition 5, what has been discarded is of order $\mathrm{Kn}$ relative to $p$. §7.2 identifies the discarded part as the viscous stress, and §9 puts numbers on it. ∎

Equation (7.3) is the single most used equation in this book. Modules 3 to 11 are, with few exceptions, applications of it under different geometries and different force terms.

7.2 The Navier–Stokes equation

Retaining the first-order term produces a viscous stress. Module 1 computed the transport coefficient in its Proposition 4; here we give the tensor it multiplies.

Proposition 8 (Navier–Stokes equation). At first order in $\mathrm{Kn}$ the pressure tensor takes the form $$ P_{ij} \;=\; p\,\delta_{ij} \;-\; \sigma_{ij}, \qquad \sigma_{ij} \;=\; \mu\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i} - \frac{2}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}\right) \;+\; \zeta\,\delta_{ij}\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}, \tag{7.4}$$ in which $\mu$ is the dynamic viscosity of Module 1, equation (6.2), and $\zeta$ is the bulk viscosity, which vanishes for a monatomic gas. Equation (7.2) then becomes $$ \rho\,\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} \;=\; -\nabla p \;+\; \mu\nabla^2\mathbf{u} \;+\; \left(\zeta+\tfrac{1}{3}\mu\right)\nabla\big(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}\big) \;+\; \rho\,\mathbf{g}, \tag{7.5}$$ for spatially uniform $\mu$ and $\zeta$.
The form of (7.4) is fixed by three requirements rather than derived here from the collision integral. The stress must be symmetric, because $P_{ij}$ is. It must be a linear function of the velocity gradient, because a first-order expansion in $\mathrm{Kn}$ is by construction linear in gradients. And it must be isotropic, because a gas has no preferred direction. The most general isotropic linear relation between two symmetric tensors has exactly two independent coefficients. Splitting the velocity gradient into its trace-free and trace parts assigns one coefficient to each: $\mu$ multiplies the trace-free rate of strain, $\zeta$ multiplies the isotropic expansion. The value of $\mu$ is what Chapman–Enskog theory computes, and Module 1's Proposition 4 estimated it as $\mu\simeq\tfrac{1}{3}\rho v_{\rm th}\lambda$. That $\zeta=0$ for a monatomic gas follows from the fact that isotropic compression changes only the translational energy, which is already in $p$; a gas with internal degrees of freedom, which relax more slowly, has $\zeta\neq 0$. To obtain (7.5), take the divergence of (7.4) with $\mu,\zeta$ constant: $$ \frac{\partial \sigma_{ij}}{\partial x_j} \;=\; \mu\left(\nabla^2 u_i + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} - \frac{2}{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}\right) + \zeta\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}, $$ using $\partial_j\partial_j u_i = \nabla^2u_i$ and $\partial_j\partial_iu_j = \partial_i(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})$. Collecting the two gradient-of-divergence terms gives the coefficient $\zeta+\tfrac{1}{3}\mu$, and substituting $-\partial_jP_{ij} = -\partial_ip + \partial_j\sigma_{ij}$ into (7.2) gives (7.5). ∎

For incompressible flow, Proposition 5 gives $\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf u=0$, so the third term in equation (7.5) vanishes. The remaining viscous term may still be negligible. Section 9 uses the Reynolds number to make that comparison.

8. Conservation of energy

Take $\psi = \tfrac{1}{2}m|\mathbf{v}|^2$, the last entry of (3.3). The same decomposition into bulk and peculiar parts now separates kinetic energy from internal energy.

Proposition 9 (total energy equation). Let $e \equiv \tfrac{1}{2}\langle|\mathbf{w}|^2\rangle$ be the specific internal energy, so that $\rho e = \tfrac{3}{2}p$ by Proposition 1. Then $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\rho\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathbf{u}|^2 + e\right)\right] \;+\; \nabla\cdot\left[\rho\mathbf{u}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathbf{u}|^2 + e\right) + \mathsf{P}\cdot\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{q}\right] \;=\; \rho\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{g}, \tag{8.1}$$ in which $(\mathsf{P}\cdot\mathbf{u})_j = P_{jk}u_k$.
With $\psi=\tfrac{1}{2}m|\mathbf{v}|^2$, the storage term is $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\langle|\mathbf{v}|^2\rangle$. Substituting $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}$, $$ \langle|\mathbf{v}|^2\rangle \;=\; |\mathbf{u}|^2 + 2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\langle\mathbf{w}\rangle + \langle|\mathbf{w}|^2\rangle \;=\; |\mathbf{u}|^2 + 2e, $$ again because $\langle\mathbf{w}\rangle=\mathbf{0}$, so the storage term is $\rho(\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathbf{u}|^2+e)$. The flux term is $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\langle |\mathbf{v}|^2 v_j\rangle$. Expanding the same way and dropping every term containing a single factor of $\mathbf{w}$ inside an average, $$ \tfrac{1}{2}\langle|\mathbf{v}|^2v_j\rangle \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}|\mathbf{u}|^2u_j \;+\; e\,u_j \;+\; u_k\langle w_jw_k\rangle \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\langle|\mathbf{w}|^2w_j\rangle. $$ Multiplying by $\rho$ identifies the four pieces in order: the flux of bulk kinetic energy, the advection of internal energy, the work done by the pressure tensor $P_{jk}u_k$, and the heat flux $q_j$ of Definition 5. The source term is $n\langle\mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}(\tfrac{1}{2}m|\mathbf{v}|^2)\rangle = n\langle m\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{v}\rangle = \rho\,\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{u}$ for a velocity-independent $\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{g}$. Substituting all three into (4.1) gives (8.1). ∎

Equation (8.1) conserves total energy, but it mixes two physically different things, and for most purposes the internal energy alone is wanted. Subtracting the bulk kinetic energy gives the second form.

Proposition 10 (internal energy equation). Equation (8.1), combined with continuity and the momentum equation, is equivalent to $$ \rho\,\frac{De}{Dt} \;=\; -p\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} \;+\; \sigma_{ij}\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} \;-\; \nabla\cdot\mathbf{q}. \tag{8.2}$$
Form the kinetic-energy equation by taking the dot product of the Cauchy equation (7.2) with $\mathbf{u}$: $$ \rho\,\frac{D}{Dt}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathbf{u}|^2\right) \;=\; -u_i\frac{\partial P_{ij}}{\partial x_j} \;+\; \rho\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{g}. $$ Rewrite (8.1) in Lagrangian form using continuity, which converts $\partial_t(\rho\Phi)+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u}\Phi)$ into $\rho\,D\Phi/Dt$ for any $\Phi$; with $\Phi=\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathbf{u}|^2+e$ this gives $$ \rho\,\frac{D}{Dt}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathbf{u}|^2+e\right) \;=\; -\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\big(P_{jk}u_k\big) \;-\; \nabla\cdot\mathbf{q} \;+\; \rho\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{g}. $$ Subtract the kinetic-energy equation from this. The gravitational terms cancel. The two stress terms combine as $$ -\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\big(P_{jk}u_k\big) + u_k\frac{\partial P_{kj}}{\partial x_j} \;=\; -P_{jk}\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_j}, $$ using the symmetry of $P$. Finally substitute $P_{jk}=p\,\delta_{jk}-\sigma_{jk}$ from (7.4): the isotropic part gives $-p\,\partial_ku_k = -p\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}$ and the viscous part gives $+\sigma_{jk}\partial_ku_j$, which is the second term of (8.2) after relabelling. ∎

Each term of (8.2) has a name and a sign that can be checked. The first is $p\,dV$ work: a parcel that expands, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}\gt 0$, loses internal energy. The second is viscous dissipation, which is non-negative and converts bulk kinetic energy irreversibly into heat. The third is conduction, which redistributes heat without creating it. Setting the last two to zero — the Euler closure of $\sigma_{ij}=0$ and $\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}$ — leaves a parcel whose internal energy changes only by doing work, which is the definition of adiabatic. That gives the third and most used form.

Proposition 11 (adiabatic form). For an ideal monatomic gas with $\rho e = p/(\gamma-1)$ and $\gamma=5/3$, under the Euler closure $\sigma_{ij}=0$ and $\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}$, equation (8.2) is equivalent to $$ \boxed{\;\frac{D}{Dt}\left(\frac{p}{\rho^{\gamma}}\right) \;=\; 0\;} \tag{8.3}$$ — each fluid parcel conserves its own entropy, though different parcels may carry different values.
With $\sigma_{ij}=0$ and $\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}$, equation (8.2) is $\rho\,De/Dt = -p\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}$. Use Proposition 5 to replace the divergence, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} = -\rho^{-1}D\rho/Dt$, giving $$ \rho\,\frac{De}{Dt} \;=\; \frac{p}{\rho}\frac{D\rho}{Dt}. $$ Now substitute $e = p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$ and differentiate the quotient: $$ \frac{De}{Dt} \;=\; \frac{1}{\gamma-1}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{Dp}{Dt} - \frac{p}{\rho^2}\frac{D\rho}{Dt}\right). $$ Multiplying by $\rho$ and equating to the right-hand side above, $$ \frac{1}{\gamma-1}\left(\frac{Dp}{Dt} - \frac{p}{\rho}\frac{D\rho}{Dt}\right) \;=\; \frac{p}{\rho}\frac{D\rho}{Dt} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{Dp}{Dt} \;=\; \frac{\gamma p}{\rho}\frac{D\rho}{Dt}. $$ Finally, apply $D/Dt$ to $p\rho^{-\gamma}$ directly: $$ \frac{D}{Dt}\left(\frac{p}{\rho^\gamma}\right) \;=\; \frac{1}{\rho^\gamma}\frac{Dp}{Dt} - \frac{\gamma p}{\rho^{\gamma+1}}\frac{D\rho}{Dt} \;=\; \frac{1}{\rho^\gamma}\left(\frac{Dp}{Dt} - \frac{\gamma p}{\rho}\frac{D\rho}{Dt}\right) \;=\; 0 $$ by the relation just obtained. ∎

Equation (8.3) is not the statement that the entropy is the same everywhere. It is the statement that each parcel carries its entropy with it unchanged. A flow in which every parcel happens to carry the same value is called isentropic, and then $p\rho^{-\gamma}$ is a single constant for the whole flow. That stronger condition is assumed in Modules 3 and 4 and must be justified separately each time. §10 shows a real flow in which (8.3) fails outright while (5.1) holds.

With Propositions 4, 7 and 11 we have the closed set this book uses:

$$ \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u}) = 0, \qquad \rho\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = -\nabla p + \rho\mathbf{g}, \qquad \frac{D}{Dt}\left(\frac{p}{\rho^\gamma}\right) = 0, \qquad p = \frac{\rho k_B T}{\mu_{\rm m} m_p}. \tag{8.4}$$

Five equations — one scalar, one vector, one scalar — for five unknowns $\rho$, $\mathbf{u}$, $p$, with the equation of state supplying $T$ afterwards. Here $\mu_{\rm m}$ is the mean molecular weight, the mean mass per particle in units of the proton mass, equal to $0.6$ for a fully ionised gas of cosmic composition. Here $\mu$ denotes dynamic viscosity and $\mu_{\rm m}$ denotes mean molecular weight. The bare $\mu$ is the dynamic viscosity of (7.4), and the two quantities share a letter by historical accident across this whole field. This book writes $\mu$ for viscosity and $\mu_{\rm m}$ for mean molecular weight everywhere, and the Notation table carries both.

9. Closure and the Reynolds number

The system (8.4) was obtained by cutting the hierarchy of Fig. 1 at the first rung, setting $P_{ij}=p\,\delta_{ij}$ and $\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}$. That cut is an assumption, and Module 1 supplies the number that says when it is safe.

The Chapman–Enskog expansion writes $f = f_M(1 + \phi_1 + \phi_2 + \dots)$ with $\phi_k = O(\mathrm{Kn}^k)$. At zeroth order $P_{ij}=p\,\delta_{ij}$ and $\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}$ exactly, and the fluid equations are Euler's. At first order the corrections are the viscous stress and Fourier conduction, and the equations are Navier–Stokes. So the fractional error committed by using Euler rather than Navier–Stokes is of order $\mathrm{Kn}$, the same number Module 1 tabulated for every astrophysical gas.

There is a second, equivalent way to say this, in terms of a number an engineer would recognise.

Proposition 12 (the Reynolds number is fixed by the Knudsen and Mach numbers). Define the Reynolds number $\mathrm{Re}\equiv \rho u L/\mu$, the thermal Mach number $\mathrm{Ma}_{\rm th}\equiv u/v_{\rm th}$ with $v_{\rm th}=\sqrt{2k_BT/m}$, and the Knudsen number $\mathrm{Kn}\equiv\lambda/L$. Then, with Module 1's estimate $\mu\simeq\tfrac{1}{3}\rho v_{\rm th}\lambda$, $$ \mathrm{Re} \;\simeq\; \frac{3\,\mathrm{Ma}_{\rm th}}{\mathrm{Kn}}. \tag{9.1}$$ The explicit density factor cancels, but density still enters through the mean free path.

The explicit factor of $\rho$ cancels on substituting the kinetic estimate of viscosity, giving $\mathrm{Re}\simeq3\mathrm{Ma}_{\rm th}/\mathrm{Kn}$. The density still enters through $\lambda$. For neutral hard-sphere collisions, $\lambda=(\sqrt{2}n\sigma)^{-1}$, so $\mathrm{Re}\propto n$ when $u$, $L$, $T$ and $\sigma$ are fixed. The formula relates three dimensionless measures of the same gas. It does not make the Reynolds number independent of density.

Substitute Module 1's equation (6.2), $\mu = \tfrac{1}{3}\rho v_{\rm th}\lambda$, into the definition: $$ \mathrm{Re} \;=\; \frac{\rho u L}{\tfrac{1}{3}\rho v_{\rm th}\lambda} \;=\; 3\,\frac{u}{v_{\rm th}}\,\frac{L}{\lambda} \;=\; \frac{3\,\mathrm{Ma}_{\rm th}}{\mathrm{Kn}}, $$ the explicit factor $\rho$ cancelling between numerator and denominator. This algebra does not remove the density dependence contained in $\lambda$; for neutral hard spheres, $\lambda\propto1/n$ and hence $\mathrm{Re}\propto n$ at fixed $u$, $L$, $T$ and cross-section. ∎

The Reynolds number measures the term discarded by the Euler closure. On a length scale $L$, inertia has magnitude $\rho u^2/L$ and viscosity has magnitude $\mu u/L^2$. Their ratio is $$ \frac{\big|\mu\nabla^2\mathbf{u}\big|}{\big|\rho(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\big|} \;\sim\; \frac{\mu u/L^2}{\rho u^2/L} \;=\; \frac{\mu}{\rho u L} \;=\; \frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}}. \tag{9.2}$$ Equation (9.1) therefore converts the Knudsen census from Module 1 into a test of whether viscosity may be neglected. 10. Comparison with the solar wind

The equations are written. The question this section asks is whether a real astronomical flow obeys them, and it is worth being careful about what would count as an answer. Testing a closure is hard, because a closure is an approximation and disagreement can always be blamed on the approximation. Testing a conservation law is clean, because it has no free parameter: it predicts an exact number.

The solar wind supplies the test. It is the only astrophysical flow whose density and speed have been measured in situ at many heliocentric distances by instruments flying through it, so the radial dependence is a measurement rather than an inference.

10.1 The prediction from steady spherical continuity

Proposition 13 (steady spherical outflow). Let a flow be steady, $\partial/\partial t = 0$, and spherically symmetric with radial velocity $v(r)$ and density $\rho(r)$. Then continuity (5.1) requires $$ 4\pi r^2 \rho(r)\, v(r) \;=\; \dot M \;=\; \text{constant}, \tag{10.1}$$ and if, over some range of radius, $n\propto r^{-\alpha}$ and $v\propto r^{+\beta}$ with constant exponents, then $$ \boxed{\;\alpha - \beta \;=\; 2\;} \tag{10.2}$$ exactly.
In spherical coordinates the divergence of a purely radial vector field $\mathbf{A}=A(r)\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ is $r^{-2}\,d(r^2A)/dr$. With $\partial\rho/\partial t=0$, equation (5.1) reduces to $$ \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\Big(r^2\rho v\Big) \;=\; 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r^2\rho v \;=\; \text{constant}, $$ and multiplying by $4\pi$ names the constant $\dot M$, the mass crossing every sphere per unit time. For (10.2), substitute the power laws. With $\rho = mn \propto r^{-\alpha}$ and $v\propto r^{\beta}$, $$ r^2\rho v \;\propto\; r^{\,2-\alpha+\beta}, $$ which is independent of $r$ if and only if the exponent vanishes, that is $\alpha-\beta=2$. ∎

Equation (10.2) is the ideal test object. It contains no coefficient to be fitted, no cross-section, no efficiency factor and no calibration. Two exponents are measured independently and their difference is predicted to be an integer.

Proposition 13 applies to a single steady stream, whereas each radial bin contains a mixture of fast and slow wind. Density and speed are anticorrelated within that mixture. Continuity constrains $\langle nv\rangle$, but the published fits describe $\langle n\rangle$ and $\langle v\rangle$ separately. The fitted exponents obey $\alpha-\beta=2$ if the mixture is radially self-similar. This requires individual streams to share the same exponents and their relative weights to remain constant with radius.

The solar-wind mixture is not exactly self-similar because fast streams overtake slow streams and exchange mass and momentum. This evolution plausibly produces the 4–5 per cent residual in Fig. 3. The residual measures departure from the single-stream assumptions rather than failure of continuity. Density–velocity anticorrelation mainly changes the flux amplitude when its covariance has the same radial scaling as the product. The mass-loss comparison in §10.4 should therefore be treated as a calibration.

10.3 Comparison of prediction and measurement

Taking the mean fits, $\alpha = 2.010$ and $\beta=0.049$, so $\alpha-\beta = 1.961$. Taking the median fits, $\alpha=2.093$ and $\beta=0.058$, so $\alpha-\beta = 2.035$. Fig. 3 shows both against the prediction.

Continuity agrees with the Helios radial scalings. Equation (8.4) requires $\alpha-\beta=2$. The mean fits give $1.961$, and the median fits give $2.035$; their ratios to the prediction are $0.980$ and $1.018$. Formal fit errors place the two values $0.99\sigma$ and $0.73\sigma$ from 2. Year-to-year scatter gives the more representative values $0.53\sigma$ and $0.48\sigma$. The steady spherical continuity relation therefore holds to about two per cent over a factor of $3.4$ in radius.

The mean-fit and median-fit constructions give different radial trends. Neither is automatically a bound on the physical mass flux. In particular, $\langle\rho u\rangle=\langle\rho\rangle\langle u\rangle+\operatorname{Cov}(\rho,u)$, so the density–speed covariance is needed to infer the mean flux. The comparison is a consistency check on these fitted summaries, not a proof that the true mass flux lies between them.

10.4 Solar mass-loss rate

The same fits give the constant in (10.1). Using the median 1 AU values, $n = 5.61\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $v=410.7\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, and counting protons only,

$$ \dot M \;=\; 4\pi r^2 \rho v \;=\; 4\pi\big(1.496\times10^{13}\big)^2\big(5.61\times1.673\times10^{-24}\big)\big(4.107\times10^{7}\big) \;=\; 1.08\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}. \tag{10.3}$$
Check against a published value. Verscharen, Bale & Velli (2021), MNRAS 506, 4993, quote $4\pi r^2 U_r N M \approx 10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$ for the ecliptic solar wind at solar minimum. Our (10.3) gives $1.08\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$, a ratio of $1.08$. Including helium at four per cent by number raises the figure to $1.26\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$. The mean rather than median fits would give $1.55\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$.

The mass-loss comparison is weaker than the exponent test. Equation (10.2) compares a measured exponent with an exact prediction that contains no fitted normalization. Equation (10.3) compares two estimates derived from overlapping data and can accommodate any constant $\dot M$. Agreement in equation (10.3) checks normalization and arithmetic; equation (10.2) tests the radial scaling.

In more familiar units, (10.3) is $1.72\times10^{-14}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$. Sustained for the Sun's present age of $4.6$ Gyr, that removes $7.9\times10^{-5}\,M_\odot$, eight parts in $10^{5}$ of the Sun's mass. The solar wind is dynamically important for the heliosphere and irrelevant to the Sun's own evolution.

10.5 Failure of the adiabatic energy closure

The temperature exponent supplies an independent test of the adiabatic closure. It uses the same Helios data and fitting method as the continuity comparison, but the closure makes a different quantitative prediction.

Proposition 11 says that under the Euler closure each parcel conserves $p\rho^{-\gamma}$. For an ideal gas, $T\propto p/\rho$, so adiabatic expansion gives $T\propto\rho^{\gamma-1}$. With $\rho\propto r^{-\alpha}$, the temperature must then fall as

$$ T \;\propto\; r^{-\alpha(\gamma-1)} \;=\; r^{-2\alpha/3} \quad\text{for}\quad \gamma=\tfrac{5}{3}. \tag{10.4}$$

The mean fits have $\alpha = 2.010$, so (10.4) predicts a temperature exponent of $-1.340$. The measured mean exponent is $-0.792\pm0.028$.

The adiabatic closure fails here, and by a wide margin. Measured $-0.792$ against predicted $-1.340$: the ratio is $0.591$, so $40.9$ per cent of the adiabatic cooling is not happening. The formal significance is $19.6\sigma$. The median fits give $-0.913$ against a predicted $-1.395$, a ratio of $0.654$ and a departure of $12.4\sigma$. The solar wind is heated as it expands, at a rate sufficient to cancel two fifths of its adiabatic cooling; What does the heating is a separate question, and this module does not answer it: the measurement above establishes that the cooling is slower than adiabatic, not why. The explanation usually given is the dissipation of a turbulent cascade. That attribution is quoted from the field at large and is not checked here — no source in this book's ledger has been read for it, and it should be treated as unverified until Module 10 takes it up. Whatever the mechanism, it lies outside the equations of this module, because (8.2) was closed by setting every heating term to zero.

The failure of the adiabatic closure is consistent with the kinetic ordering. Section 9 found $\mathrm{Kn}\approx1.9$, where the Chapman–Enskog expansion does not converge. Neither the Euler nor Navier–Stokes closure is then controlled. The measured temperature profile demonstrates the resulting failure rather than introducing a separate inconsistency.

That also limits what the measurement can be said to show. Equation (8.2) was reduced to the adiabatic form (8.3) by dropping three separate things: the viscous heating $\sigma_{ij}\partial_ju_i$, the conduction term $-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{q}$, and any external heating source, which (8.2) does not contain at all. A single measured temperature exponent constrains their sum and cannot separate them. At $\mathrm{Kn}\approx2$ the heat flux is certainly not small, so conduction alone could in principle account for part of the shortfall. The attribution of the remainder to in-situ turbulent heating is the field's, not this module's, and as noted above it is unverified here. What is established by these data is narrower and still worth having: the adiabatic closure is excluded, at $19.6\sigma$ for the mean fits.

The observations distinguish exact conservation from an approximate closure. Continuity, which follows from particle-number conservation, agrees with the fitted exponents to about two per cent. The adiabatic energy equation assumes no heating or conduction and misses the temperature exponent by roughly forty per cent. The solar wind therefore conserves mass while violating the adopted thermodynamic closure.

All Helios fit coefficients are inputs quoted from Venzmer & Bothmer (2018), Table 3, not results of this book. Two caveats attach to the anchor result. First, the fits describe the mean and median of a highly variable flow, not any single stream. The solar wind at a given radius contains slow and fast components with different densities and speeds, and the $\pm 5$ per cent residual in Fig. 3 is dominated by that structure rather than by measurement error. Second, the Helios orbits sampled a narrow band of heliographic latitude near the ecliptic, so (10.2) is tested for the ecliptic wind only; Ulysses found the polar wind faster and less dense, and the test has not been repeated here for those data. The mass loss rate (10.3) additionally assumes the ecliptic values hold over the whole sphere, which they do not.

Conceptual ↑ contents

C1. A student proposes to obtain a sixth exact fluid equation by taking the moment of the Boltzmann equation with $\psi = m|\mathbf{v}|^4$. Explain precisely what goes wrong.
Solution
Nothing goes wrong with the integration; equation (4.1) can be formed with any $\psi$. What fails is the step that removed the collision term. Proposition 2 guarantees $\int\psi\,C[f]\,d^3v=0$ only when $\psi$ satisfies (3.1), and $m|\mathbf{v}|^4$ does not: a collision conserves $|\mathbf{v}|^2$ summed over the pair, but not $|\mathbf{v}|^4$. The resulting equation therefore carries a collision integral on its right-hand side which cannot be evaluated without modelling the collision operator. It is a valid equation and not a conservation law, and it introduces a new unknown — the fourth moment — while requiring a model for the collision term, so it makes the closure problem worse rather than better.
C2. A gas flows along a channel. Its density varies by a factor of ten from one end to the other, and the flow is steady. Can this flow be incompressible?
Solution
Yes. Incompressibility is $D\rho/Dt=0$, which by Proposition 5 is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0$, and it concerns what happens to a parcel, not how density is distributed in space. If each parcel retains its own density while parcels of different density occupy different parts of the channel, the flow is incompressible and strongly stratified at the same time. What the condition does forbid, in a steady flow, is a parcel's density changing as it travels: with $\partial\rho/\partial t=0$, $D\rho/Dt=(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\rho$, so incompressibility requires $\rho$ to be constant along each streamline. The tenfold variation must therefore be across streamlines, not along them.
C3. The intracluster medium has $\mathrm{Re}\approx600$ by the table in §9. Observations of cluster cores indicate turbulent motions. State the tension and name the two ways it might be resolved.
Solution
A Reynolds number of order $10^{2}$ to $10^{3}$ is marginal for sustained turbulence; laboratory pipe flow becomes turbulent near $\mathrm{Re}\approx2\times10^{3}$, a textbook figure quoted here as a familiar yardstick and neither derived nor checked anywhere in this book. If the Spitzer viscosity of Module 1's Proposition 4 acted unsuppressed, viscous damping would remove small-scale motions in the intracluster medium and it would be close to laminar. Two resolutions are available. The first is that the viscosity is suppressed: a tangled magnetic field prevents momentum transport across field lines, which raises the effective Reynolds number, and the same mechanism is invoked for thermal conduction in Module 1 §6. The second is that the estimate of $u$ or $L$ in the table is wrong; $\mathrm{Re}$ is linear in both, so a factor of a few in either changes the conclusion. The first is the accepted answer and Module 12 develops it, but the second is the reason the table entry is quoted to one significant figure.
C4. Continuity (5.1) was derived without assuming the gas is collisional, yet Module 1 spent its length establishing that astrophysical gases are collisional. If continuity does not need collisions, what does?
Solution
Continuity needs only that particles are conserved and that $\mathbf{u}$ is defined as the mean velocity; both hold for any $f$, so it applies to a collisionless system such as a galaxy. Collisions are needed for everything that follows from a closure. Setting $P_{ij}=p\,\delta_{ij}$ requires the distribution to be nearly isotropic in the peculiar velocity, and collisions are what make it so; a collisionless system retains whatever anisotropy it was born with, which is why a galaxy has a velocity-dispersion tensor with unequal eigenvalues and no scalar pressure. The same applies to $\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}$. So continuity survives without collisions, and the momentum and energy equations survive only in their exact Cauchy forms (7.2) and (8.2), which contain unknown tensors and are not a closed system.

Derivational ↑ contents

D1. Show that for any field $\Phi$, continuity implies $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}(\rho\Phi) + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u}\Phi) = \rho\dfrac{D\Phi}{Dt}$. This identity was used twice in §8.
Solution
Expand both terms by the product rule: $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\rho\Phi) + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u}\Phi) = \rho\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t} + \Phi\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \rho\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\Phi + \Phi\,\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u}). $$ The second and fourth terms are $\Phi$ times the left-hand side of continuity (5.1) and therefore vanish together. The first and third combine into $\rho(\partial_t\Phi + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\Phi) = \rho\,D\Phi/Dt$ by Definition 7.
D2. Derive equation (6.4), $\dfrac{1}{\delta V}\dfrac{D(\delta V)}{Dt} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}$, from Proposition 5.
Solution
A fluid parcel is a material volume, so the mass it contains, $\delta m = \rho\,\delta V$, does not change: $D(\rho\,\delta V)/Dt = 0$. Expanding the product, $$ \delta V\frac{D\rho}{Dt} + \rho\frac{D(\delta V)}{Dt} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{\delta V}\frac{D(\delta V)}{Dt} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{D\rho}{Dt}. $$ Substituting Proposition 5, $D\rho/Dt = -\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}$, gives $-\rho^{-1}(-\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}) = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}$. So $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}$ is the fractional rate of expansion of a comoving volume, and Proposition 5 is the statement that a parcel's density falls exactly as fast as its volume grows.
D3. Show that the isothermal analogue of Proposition 11 is $p = c_T^2\rho$ with $c_T^2$ constant, and identify what physical assumption replaces the adiabatic one.
Solution
If $T$ is held fixed at $T_0$ throughout the flow, the equation of state in (8.4) gives $p = \rho k_BT_0/(\mu_{\rm m}m_p) \equiv c_T^2\rho$ with $c_T^2=k_BT_0/(\mu_{\rm m}m_p)$ constant. This replaces (8.3) and closes the system with one fewer equation, since $T$ is no longer an unknown. The physical assumption is the opposite extreme from adiabatic: instead of assuming that a parcel exchanges no heat with its surroundings, it assumes that heating and cooling are so efficient that the parcel is held at the ambient temperature however fast it is compressed. Formally, the term $-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{q}$ in (8.2), together with any radiative source, is taken to be large enough to cancel $-p\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}$ at all times. It is the standard closure for interstellar clouds, where line cooling is fast, and Module 5 uses it.
D4. A steady spherical flow has $n\propto r^{-\alpha}$ and $v\propto r^{\beta}$. Show that the adiabatic closure fixes a second relation between exponents, and give it for $\gamma=5/3$.
Solution
Adiabatic flow conserves $p\rho^{-\gamma}$ along a streamline, and in a steady spherical flow the streamlines are radial, so $p\propto\rho^{\gamma}\propto r^{-\alpha\gamma}$. With $p = nk_BT$ and $n\propto r^{-\alpha}$, $$ T \;\propto\; \frac{p}{n} \;\propto\; r^{-\alpha\gamma+\alpha} \;=\; r^{-\alpha(\gamma-1)}. $$ Writing $T\propto r^{-\tau}$, the relation is $\tau = \alpha(\gamma-1)$, which for $\gamma=5/3$ is $\tau = 2\alpha/3$. This is equation (10.4). Note that it involves $\alpha$ and $\tau$ only, and is independent of $\beta$: continuity constrains the density–speed pair, the energy equation constrains the density–temperature pair, and the two tests in §10 are therefore independent of one another.

Computational ↑ contents

K1. Using the Helios mean fits, $\alpha=2.010$ and $\beta=0.049$, compute the factor by which the inferred mass flux $4\pi r^2\rho v$ changes between $0.29$ and $0.98$ AU. Express it as a percentage.
Solution
From the proof of Proposition 13, $r^2\rho v\propto r^{2-\alpha+\beta}$. The residual exponent is $2-2.010+0.049 = 0.039$. The radial span is $0.98/0.29 = 3.379$, so the flux changes by the factor $3.379^{0.039}$. Taking logarithms, $0.039\times\ln 3.379 = 0.039\times1.2176 = 0.04749$, and $e^{0.04749} = 1.0486$. The mass flux therefore rises by $4.9$ per cent across the range. For the median fits the residual exponent is $2-2.093+0.058 = -0.035$, giving $3.379^{-0.035} = 0.958$, a fall of $4.2$ per cent.
K2. Compute $\dot M$ from the Helios median 1 AU values, $n=5.61\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $v = 410.7\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, counting protons only. Give the answer in $\mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$, in $M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$, and as the fraction of the Sun's mass lost in $4.6$ Gyr.
Solution
The mass density is $\rho = n m_p = 5.61\times1.6726\times10^{-24} = 9.383\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$. With $r = 1\ \mathrm{AU} = 1.496\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm}$, the sphere area is $4\pi r^2 = 2.812\times10^{27}\ \mathrm{cm^2}$, and $v = 4.107\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$. Then $$ \dot M = 2.812\times10^{27}\times9.383\times10^{-24}\times4.107\times10^{7} = 1.084\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}. $$ Converting, $1.084\times10^{12}\times3.156\times10^{7}/1.989\times10^{33} = 1.72\times10^{-14}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$. Over $4.6\times10^{9}$ yr this removes $1.72\times10^{-14}\times4.6\times10^{9} = 7.9\times10^{-5}\,M_\odot$, which is $0.0079$ per cent of the Sun's mass.
K3. The intracluster medium of a cluster core has $n=10^{-2}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $T=3\times10^{7}$ K, a Coulomb mean free path of $\lambda = 0.22$ kpc from Module 1, a characteristic length $L=100$ kpc and a characteristic speed $u = 300\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Compute $\mathrm{Kn}$, $\mathrm{Ma}_{\rm th}$ and $\mathrm{Re}$.
Solution
The Knudsen number is $\mathrm{Kn} = \lambda/L = 0.22/100 = 2.2\times10^{-3}$. The proton thermal speed is $$ v_{\rm th} = \sqrt{\frac{2k_BT}{m_p}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\times1.381\times10^{-16}\times3\times10^{7}}{1.673\times10^{-24}}} = 7.04\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}, $$ so $\mathrm{Ma}_{\rm th} = 3\times10^{7}/7.04\times10^{7} = 0.426$. By Proposition 12, $\mathrm{Re} = 3\times0.426/2.2\times10^{-3} = 5.8\times10^{2}$. The viscous term is therefore smaller than the inertial term by a factor of about $600$, which is a large ratio by everyday standards and a small one by astrophysical standards — compare $10^{13}$ for the solar convection zone.
K4. Test the adiabatic closure against the Helios median fits: $\alpha=2.093$ and a measured temperature exponent $-0.913\pm0.039$. By what factor and by how many standard deviations does it fail?
Solution
Problem D4 gives the adiabatic prediction $\tau=2\alpha/3=1.395$, or $T\propto r^{-1.395}$. The measured exponent is $-0.913$, only $0.654$ of the predicted magnitude. The discrepancy is $12.4\sigma$ for the median fits and $19.6\sigma$ for the mean fits. Thus the same data satisfy steady continuity within one standard deviation but exclude adiabatic cooling.
