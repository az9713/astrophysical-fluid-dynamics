Radiation changes a fluid in two ways. It carries heat down a temperature gradient and transfers momentum when it is absorbed or scattered. The first effect modifies stellar oscillations; the second can oppose gravity near a luminous source. We begin with the angular distribution of light, then derive the transfer and diffusion laws needed to quantify both effects.

The Eddington limit in §9 follows from a simple force balance. Its opacity and flux must first be defined carefully. The solar-atmosphere example in §6 then shows why electron scattering cannot be assumed to describe every gas.

The photospheric opacity used below comes from an ATLAS9 model atmosphere. We do not derive the atomic opacity of H⁻ here. That distinction matters: a model opacity is a physical input, whereas the column-mean calculation in §6 is an inference from it.

1. Photons carry momentum

A photon of energy $E$ carries momentum $E/c$. A beam with energy flux $F$ therefore delivers momentum flux $F/c$ to a perfectly absorbing surface. In a star, radiation arrives from many directions, so a single beam is insufficient. We describe its directional distribution with the specific intensity.

Definition 1 (specific intensity and its moments). The specific intensity $I_\nu(\mathbf r,\mathbf n,t)$ gives radiation energy per unit time, area normal to direction $\mathbf n$, solid angle, and frequency. Its units are $\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}\,cm^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,Hz^{-1}}$. Let $\theta$ be the angle from a chosen $z$ axis and $\mathrm d\Omega$ an element of solid angle. Three angular moments are $$ u_\nu = \frac{1}{c}\oint I_\nu\,\mathrm{d}\Omega, \qquad F_\nu = \oint I_\nu\cos\theta\,\mathrm{d}\Omega, \qquad P_\nu = \frac{1}{c}\oint I_\nu\cos^{2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\Omega, \tag{1.1}$$ They are energy density, axial energy flux, and axial momentum flux per unit frequency. The last is radiation pressure. Mean intensity is $J_\nu=(1/4\pi)\oint I_\nu\,\mathrm d\Omega=c u_\nu/(4\pi)$. Frequency-integrated quantities omit the subscript: $u_{\rm rad}=\int u_\nu\,\mathrm d\nu$, $F=\int F_\nu\,\mathrm d\nu$, and $P_{\rm rad}=\int P_\nu\,\mathrm d\nu$.

We denote the second angular moment by $P_\nu$, emphasizing its interpretation as radiation pressure. The notation also avoids confusion with the polytropic constant $K$ used in Module 3.

Proposition 1 (the radiation pressure of an isotropic field). If $I_\nu$ does not depend on direction, then $P_\nu = u_\nu/3$ and $F_\nu = 0$. In thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$ the integrated energy density is $u_{\rm rad} = a_{\rm rad}T^{4}$, so $$ P_{\rm rad} \;=\; \frac{u_{\rm rad}}{3} \;=\; \frac{a_{\rm rad}T^{4}}{3}, \qquad a_{\rm rad} \;=\; \frac{4\sigma_{\rm SB}}{c}. \tag{1.2}$$ The factor $1/3$ is derived; the law $u_{\rm rad} = a_{\rm rad}T^4$ is stated.
Write $\mathrm{d}\Omega = \sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi = -\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\phi$ with $\mu = \cos\theta$. For isotropic $I_\nu$ the three integrals in (1.1) are $\oint\mathrm{d}\Omega = 2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mu = 4\pi$, $\oint\mu\,\mathrm{d}\Omega = 2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\mu = 0$ and $\oint\mu^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega = 2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}\mu^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu = 4\pi/3$. So $u_\nu = 4\pi I_\nu/c$, $F_\nu = 0$ and $P_\nu = 4\pi I_\nu/3c = u_\nu/3$. Integrating over $\nu$ gives $P_{\rm rad} = u_{\rm rad}/3$. We take the Stefan–Boltzmann law for black-body radiation, $\int B_\nu(T)\,\mathrm{d}\nu = \sigma_{\rm SB}T^{4}/\pi$, where $B_\nu(T)$ is the Planck function; its statistical-mechanical derivation lies outside the present fluid treatment. Given it, the energy crossing a plane from one side of an isotropic black-body field is $\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1 B_\nu\,\mu\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\phi = \pi B_\nu$ per unit frequency, which integrates to $\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4$, while $u_{\rm rad} = (4\pi/c)\int B_\nu\,\mathrm{d}\nu = 4\sigma_{\rm SB}T^{4}/c$. That is $u_{\rm rad} = a_{\rm rad}T^4$ with $a_{\rm rad} = 4\sigma_{\rm SB}/c$. ∎

The radiation constant is $a_{\rm rad}=7.565733\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{erg\,cm^{-3}\,K^{-4}}$. The factor $1/3$ in isotropic radiation pressure is geometric. A beam directed along $z$, by contrast, has $P_\nu=u_\nu$ because every photon carries its full axial momentum across the surface. A uniformly illuminated hemisphere still gives $P_\nu=u_\nu/3$, although its net flux is nonzero. These limits show why pressure and flux are distinct moments.

2. The transfer equation

Here $\nu$ denotes photon frequency, measured in hertz. Earlier modules also use $\nu$ for kinematic viscosity, but viscosity does not enter this chapter. Subscripts such as $I_\nu$ and $\kappa_\nu$ always refer to frequency.
Definition 2 (opacity, emissivity and the source function). A ray crossing a path length $\mathrm{d}s$ of gas of density $\rho$ loses the fraction $\kappa_\nu\rho\,\mathrm{d}s$ of its intensity to absorption and scattering together; $\kappa_\nu$ is the mass extinction coefficient, or opacity, in $\mathrm{cm^{2}\,g^{-1}}$. The same gas adds $j_\nu\rho\,\mathrm{d}s$, where $j_\nu$ is the emissivity, in $\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}\,g^{-1}\,sr^{-1}\,Hz^{-1}}$. Their ratio $S_\nu \equiv j_\nu/\kappa_\nu$ is the source function. In thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$, $S_\nu = B_\nu(T)$.

We write $\kappa_\nu$ for monochromatic opacity, $\kappa_{\rm R}$ for its Rosseland mean, $\kappa_{\rm es}$ for electron scattering, and $\kappa_{\rm F}$ for a flux mean. Keeping these quantities distinct prevents a column-averaged opacity from being mistaken for a local scattering coefficient.

Proposition 2 (the transfer equation and its formal solution). In a static medium, along a ray with path length $s$, $$ \frac{\mathrm{d}I_\nu}{\mathrm{d}s} \;=\; \rho\left(j_\nu - \kappa_\nu I_\nu\right), \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}I_\nu}{\mathrm{d}\tau_\nu} \;=\; S_\nu - I_\nu, \tag{2.1}$$ where $\mathrm{d}\tau_\nu = \kappa_\nu\rho\,\mathrm{d}s$ is accumulated in the direction of travel from the point where the ray enters, $\tau_\nu = 0$. Its solution is $$ I_\nu(\tau_\nu) \;=\; I_\nu(0)\,e^{-\tau_\nu} \;+\; \int_0^{\tau_\nu} S_\nu(t)\,e^{-(\tau_\nu - t)}\,\mathrm{d}t. \tag{2.2}$$ The first form of (2.1) is stated; (2.2) is derived.
What is stated. The first form of (2.1) is energy conservation for the photons in a thin cylinder along the ray: what leaves one end equals what entered the other, less what the gas removed, plus what it emitted. It omits the term $(1/c)\partial I_\nu/\partial t$, which is negligible whenever the light-crossing time is short against the time on which the medium changes, and it assumes the gas is at rest. What is derived. Dividing the first form by $\kappa_\nu\rho$ and using $\mathrm{d}\tau_\nu = \kappa_\nu\rho\,\mathrm{d}s$ and $S_\nu = j_\nu/\kappa_\nu$ gives the second. Multiply it by $e^{\tau_\nu}$: the left side becomes $e^{\tau_\nu}(\mathrm{d}I_\nu/\mathrm{d}\tau_\nu + I_\nu) = \mathrm{d}(I_\nu e^{\tau_\nu})/\mathrm{d}\tau_\nu$, so $\mathrm{d}(I_\nu e^{\tau_\nu})/\mathrm{d}\tau_\nu = S_\nu e^{\tau_\nu}$. Integrating from $0$ to $\tau_\nu$ gives $I_\nu(\tau_\nu)e^{\tau_\nu} - I_\nu(0) = \int_0^{\tau_\nu}S_\nu(t)e^{t}\,\mathrm{d}t$, and multiplying by $e^{-\tau_\nu}$ gives (2.2). ∎

For a constant source function, equation (2.2) becomes $I_\nu=I_\nu(0)e^{-\tau_\nu}+S_\nu(1-e^{-\tau_\nu})$. An optically thin slab barely changes the incident ray. An optically thick slab instead emits with its own source function, losing memory of the incident intensity. When scattering contributes to $S_\nu$, the source depends on the radiation field itself, and the formal solution becomes an integral equation.

3. Optical depth

Definition 3 (optical depth and the photon mean free path). The optical depth is $\mathrm{d}\tau_\nu = \kappa_\nu\rho\,\mathrm{d}s$. In an atmosphere it is measured inward from the observer, with $\tau_\nu = 0$ at the top; along a ray in Proposition 2 the same increment is accumulated in the direction of travel. The photon mean free path is $\lambda_{\rm ph} = 1/(\kappa_\nu\rho)$ at one frequency, or $1/(\kappa_{\rm R}\rho)$ for the Rosseland mean of §4.

Optical depth measures the expected number of interactions along a path. With no emission, equation (2.2) gives an unscattered survival fraction $e^{-\tau}$. The mean distance to the next interaction is the local mean free path $\lambda_{\rm ph}$. In the ATLAS9 solar atmosphere, that path is about $84.2$ km at Rosseland optical depth $2/3$.

Proposition 3 (the random walk). A photon that is scattered, or absorbed and re-emitted, in a random direction at every step, and that must cross a slab of optical depth $\tau \gg 1$, takes of order $$ N \;\approx\; \tau^{2} \tag{3.1}$$ steps to do it. This is an order of magnitude and is printed as one.
Let the photon take $N$ steps $\boldsymbol{\ell}_i$, each of length $\lambda_{\rm ph}$ on average and in an independent random direction. The net displacement is $\mathbf{d} = \sum_i\boldsymbol{\ell}_i$, and $\langle|\mathbf{d}|^{2}\rangle = \sum_i\langle|\boldsymbol{\ell}_i|^{2}\rangle + \sum_{i\ne j}\langle\boldsymbol{\ell}_i\cdot\boldsymbol{\ell}_j\rangle = N\lambda_{\rm ph}^{2}$, because independent random directions make every cross term average to zero. The slab has thickness $L = \tau\lambda_{\rm ph}$. Setting the root-mean-square displacement $\lambda_{\rm ph}\sqrt{N}$ equal to $L$ gives $N = \tau^{2}$. The factors of order unity that this sets aside — the spread of step lengths, whether the displacement is measured in one dimension or three, and the absorbing boundary — are what §4 fixes. ∎

A photon must take many random steps to cross an optically thick layer. For thickness $L=\tau\lambda_{\rm ph}$, the typical number is $N\sim\tau^2$. Its total path is then $N\lambda_{\rm ph}\sim\tau L$, much longer than a straight crossing. This delay leads naturally to a diffusion description in the stellar interior.

4. The diffusion limit

Deep inside a star, the radiation field is nearly isotropic. A temperature gradient introduces a small anisotropy, allowing energy to flow from hotter layers to cooler ones. This is the diffusion limit of the transfer equation. It requires a photon mean free path short compared with the temperature scale length.

Definition 4 (the Rosseland mean opacity). $$ \frac{1}{\kappa_{\rm R}} \;\equiv\; \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{\kappa_\nu}\,\frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T}\,\mathrm{d}\nu}{\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T}\,\mathrm{d}\nu}. \tag{4.1}$$ It is defined so that Proposition 4 holds with a single opacity. It is a harmonic mean, so frequencies where the gas is transparent dominate it, and it is weighted by $\partial B_\nu/\partial T$, which is where the diffusing flux is carried.
Proposition 4 (radiative diffusion). Consider a static, plane-parallel medium whose optical depth is large across a temperature scale height. Its frequency-integrated flux along $z$ is $$ F \;=\; -\,\frac{c}{3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\!\left(a_{\rm rad}T^{4}\right) \;=\; -\,\frac{16\sigma_{\rm SB}T^{3}}{3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}z}. \tag{4.2}$$ The derivation uses the Eddington closure $P_\nu=u_\nu/3$. Proposition 1 proved that relation for an isotropic field; here it is an approximation for a nearly isotropic field.
With $\mu = \cos\theta$ and $z$ the coordinate normal to the layers, $\mathrm{d}s = \mathrm{d}z/\mu$, so the first form of (2.1) is $\mu\,\partial I_\nu/\partial z = \rho(j_\nu - \kappa_\nu I_\nu)$. Multiply by $\mu$ and integrate over solid angle. On the left, $\oint\mu^{2}\,\partial I_\nu/\partial z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega = c\,\mathrm{d}P_\nu/\mathrm{d}z$ by (1.1). On the right, the emission of a gas at rest is isotropic, so $\oint j_\nu\mu\,\mathrm{d}\Omega = 0$, and $\oint\kappa_\nu I_\nu\mu\,\mathrm{d}\Omega = \kappa_\nu F_\nu$. The first moment of the transfer equation is therefore exact: $$ c\,\frac{\mathrm{d}P_\nu}{\mathrm{d}z} \;=\; -\,\kappa_\nu\rho\,F_\nu. $$ Now apply the closure $P_\nu = u_\nu/3$, and use that at $\tau\gg1$ the field is black-body to leading order, $u_\nu = 4\pi B_\nu(T)/c$, so that $\mathrm{d}P_\nu/\mathrm{d}z = (4\pi/3c)(\partial B_\nu/\partial T)\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}z$. Then $$ F_\nu \;=\; -\,\frac{4\pi}{3\kappa_\nu\rho}\,\frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}z}. $$ The hinge is the order of the two operations. Integrating over frequency, $\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}z$ and $\rho$ come outside, and what is left inside is $\int(1/\kappa_\nu)(\partial B_\nu/\partial T)\,\mathrm{d}\nu$, which by (4.1) is $(1/\kappa_{\rm R})\int(\partial B_\nu/\partial T)\,\mathrm{d}\nu$. The remaining integral is $\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}T$ of $\int B_\nu\,\mathrm{d}\nu = \sigma_{\rm SB}T^4/\pi$, that is $4\sigma_{\rm SB}T^{3}/\pi$. Hence $F = -(4\pi/3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho)(4\sigma_{\rm SB}T^{3}/\pi)\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}z = -(16\sigma_{\rm SB}T^3/3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho)\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}z$, the second form of (4.2). Since $a_{\rm rad} = 4\sigma_{\rm SB}/c$, $\mathrm{d}(a_{\rm rad}T^4)/\mathrm{d}z = (16\sigma_{\rm SB}T^{3}/c)\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}z$, which turns the second form into the first. ∎

An isothermal region gives $F=0$ in equation (4.2), whatever its opacity. A temperature gradient alone does not guarantee that diffusion applies, however. Near a free surface the radiation field becomes strongly one-sided, and the optically thick closure fails. Section 5 treats that boundary with a grey-atmosphere approximation.

Proposition 5 (the two diffusivities). Written as $F = -D\,\mathrm{d}u_{\rm rad}/\mathrm{d}z$, (4.2) has photon diffusivity $$ D \;=\; \frac{c\,\lambda_{\rm ph}}{3}, \qquad \lambda_{\rm ph} = \frac{1}{\kappa_{\rm R}\rho}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad t_{\rm diff} \;\sim\; \frac{R^{2}}{D} \;=\; \frac{3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho R^{2}}{c}. \tag{4.3}$$ Written as a heat flux in a gas of specific heat $c_P$ at constant pressure, the same law is a diffusion of temperature with diffusivity $$ \chi \;=\; \frac{16\sigma_{\rm SB}T^{3}}{3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho^{2}c_P}. \tag{4.4}$$ The time $R^2/D$ is an order of magnitude and is printed as one.
With $u_{\rm rad} = a_{\rm rad}T^4$, the first form of (4.2) reads $F = -(c/3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho)\,\mathrm{d}u_{\rm rad}/\mathrm{d}z$, so $D = c/(3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho) = c\lambda_{\rm ph}/3$. For a diffusion equation $\partial u/\partial t = D\,\partial^{2}u/\partial z^{2}$, a disturbance of size $R$ decays on a time of order $R^{2}/D$; the exact coefficient depends on the shape of the disturbance, which is why this is an order of magnitude. Substituting $D$ gives $3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho R^{2}/c$. For (4.4), write the second form of (4.2) as $F = -K_{\rm rad}\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}z$ with conductivity $K_{\rm rad} = 16\sigma_{\rm SB}T^3/(3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho)$. Energy conservation in a gas heated only by the divergence of this flux at constant pressure is $\rho c_P\,\partial T/\partial t = -\partial F/\partial z = \partial(K_{\rm rad}\,\partial T/\partial z)/\partial z$, which for locally uniform $K_{\rm rad}$ is a diffusion equation for $T$ with $\chi = K_{\rm rad}/(\rho c_P)$, which is (4.4). ∎

The factor $1/3$ in the photon diffusivity comes from averaging directions in an almost isotropic field. The gas thermal diffusivity $\chi$ is different: it divides radiative conductivity by the gas heat capacity per unit volume. We therefore use $D$ for photon energy diffusion and $\chi$ for the rate at which gas-temperature perturbations relax.

The gas diffusivity $\chi$ enters the adiabatic-wave criterion introduced in Module 4. Section 7 evaluates that criterion for two layers of a solar model atmosphere.

5. The grey atmosphere

Radiative diffusion cannot describe the stellar surface, where photons escape and the field is no longer nearly isotropic. A grey atmosphere connects the deep diffusive region to the surface by taking opacity to be independent of frequency. Its familiar temperature law also requires radiative equilibrium and a boundary closure.

Proposition 6 (Eddington's grey atmosphere). A plane-parallel grey atmosphere in radiative equilibrium and local thermal equilibrium, under the Eddington closure $P_{\rm rad} = u_{\rm rad}/3$ and with no radiation incident from outside, carrying the flux $F = \sigma_{\rm SB}T_{\rm eff}^{4}$, has $$ T^{4}(\tau) \;=\; \tfrac34\,T_{\rm eff}^{4}\left(\tau + \tfrac23\right), \tag{5.1}$$ so that $T = T_{\rm eff}$ at $\tau = 2/3$. The exact grey solution, without the closure, replaces $2/3$ by the Hopf function $q_{\rm H}(\tau)$; that replacement is stated and not derived.
Grey means that $\kappa_\nu$ is the same at every $\nu$, so it equals its own Rosseland mean, $\kappa_\nu = \kappa_{\rm R}$. Let $\tau$ increase inward, $\mathrm{d}\tau = -\kappa_{\rm R}\rho\,\mathrm{d}z$ with $z$ outward, and $\mu = \cos\theta$ from the outward normal. The transfer equation (2.1) reads $\mu\,\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}\tau = I - S$, frequency-integrated because $\kappa_\nu$ does not depend on $\nu$. Integrate over solid angle: $\mathrm{d}F/\mathrm{d}\tau = 4\pi(J - S)$. Radiative equilibrium says $F$ is constant, so $J = S$ at every depth. Multiply by $\mu$ and integrate: $c\,\mathrm{d}P_{\rm rad}/\mathrm{d}\tau = F$, so $P_{\rm rad} = F\tau/c + C$. The closure $P_{\rm rad} = u_{\rm rad}/3 = 4\pi J/3c$ converts this into $J(\tau) = (3F/4\pi)\tau + 3cC/4\pi$. The surface condition fixes the integration constant $C$. At $\tau = 0$ nothing comes in, and the Eddington approximation takes the outgoing intensity as uniform over the outward hemisphere, $I = I_0$ for $\mu \gt 0$ and $I = 0$ for $\mu \lt 0$. Then $J(0) = I_0/2$ and $F = 2\pi\int_0^1 I_0\mu\,\mathrm{d}\mu = \pi I_0$, so $J(0) = F/2\pi$. (Such a hemispheric field also has $P_{\rm rad} = u_{\rm rad}/3$, by Problem C1, so the surface condition does not contradict the closure.) Hence $J(\tau) = (3F/4\pi)(\tau + 2/3)$. In local thermal equilibrium $S = \int B_\nu\,\mathrm{d}\nu = \sigma_{\rm SB}T^{4}/\pi$, and $J = S$ gives $\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4/\pi = (3\sigma_{\rm SB}T_{\rm eff}^4/4\pi)(\tau + 2/3)$, which is (5.1). At $\tau = 2/3$ the bracket is $4/3$ and $T = T_{\rm eff}$. What is stated: the exact solution of $J = \Lambda[J]$, the integral equation that (2.2) becomes when $S = J$, is $J = (3F/4\pi)(\tau + q_{\rm H}(\tau))$; $q_{\rm H}$ is evaluated numerically below. ∎

Far below the surface, the grey temperature law approaches the diffusion result. Differentiating equation (5.1) gives the same constant flux as equation (4.2). At the surface, the constant $2/3$ comes from the Eddington boundary approximation, not from local diffusion. The two regimes meet only within the assumptions of the grey model.

A more accurate grey-transfer solution replaces the constant $2/3$ with a depth-dependent Hopf function. Its values are $0.57730$ at the surface and $0.68876$ at $\tau_{\rm R}=2/3$ in the calculation used here. The resulting temperature change is small for this comparison, but it exposes the boundary approximation in equation (5.1).

The ATLAS9 comparison uses $T_{\rm eff}=5777$ K because that is the effective temperature of the tabulated atmosphere. Elsewhere this book uses the IAU nominal value $5772$ K. Keeping the model's own temperature avoids introducing a mismatch unrelated to the grey approximation.

6. The anchor: the opacity of the solar photosphere

A photospheric scale-height estimate can constrain the mean opacity of a column. Module 6 used $H=142.9$ km and $\rho=3.0631\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ at a representative solar layer. Combined with optical depth $2/3$, these numbers imply a column-averaged Rosseland opacity. They do not determine the opacity at the layer's base.

Let $m$ be the mass per unit area above a layer. The optical depth is $\tau_{\rm R}=\int_0^m\kappa_{\rm R}\,\mathrm{d}m'$. Thus $\tau_{\rm R}/m$ is a column mean even when local opacity changes with depth. Approximating $m$ by $\rho H$ yields equation (6.1), whose meaning remains a column average.

$$ \bar\kappa_{\rm R} \;=\; \frac{2/3}{\rho H} \;=\; \frac{(2/3)\,g}{P} \;=\; 0.15231\ \mathrm{cm^{2}\,g^{-1}}. \tag{6.1}$$

The estimate gives $\bar\kappa_{\rm R}=0.15231\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$ from the rounded $\rho H$ values. Using hydrostatic $m=P/g$ gives $0.15233\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$. Their small difference is rounding. Neither calculation measures the local $\kappa_{\rm R}$ at optical depth $2/3$.

Castelli's ATLAS9 solar atmosphere tabulates pressure, column mass, and local Rosseland opacity as functions of depth. Integrating the model opacity yields a column mean at each comparison layer. We compare like with like: the value inferred from $\rho H$ against that integrated mean, and separately against the local opacity to show why the distinction matters.

CHECK 2 — a column-mean opacity comparison. Equation (6.1) gives $\bar\kappa_{\rm R}=0.15231\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$. The ATLAS9 column means are $0.15470$ at the layer with $T=5772$ K and $0.17487$ at $\tau_{\rm R}=2/3$. The model-to-estimate ratios are $1.0157$ and $1.1482$. No acceptance tolerance was fixed in advance, so these numbers describe agreement rather than validate a prediction. As a local opacity, equation (6.1) is low by factors of $2.36$–$2.92$. It must not be used as the coefficient at the base of the column.

No tolerance was specified before this opacity comparison. The ratios in CHECK 2 therefore describe agreement with the ATLAS9 column means; they are not a pass or fail criterion. Most of the difference at $\tau_{\rm R}=2/3$ follows from the pressure adopted in Module 6, which exceeds the ATLAS9 pressure at that layer.

Equation (6.1) averages opacity over the material above the chosen layer. In ATLAS9, local $\kappa_{\rm R}$ rises from $0.0424$ at $\tau_{\rm R}=0.01$ to $0.4447\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$ at $2/3$. A mean over that column cannot be substituted for the local coefficient at its base. Figure 2 shows the difference across the atmosphere.

ATLAS9 supplies a model atmosphere with specified abundances, opacities, and convection. Its output is useful for testing a simplified calculation, but it is not a direct measurement of the Sun's opacity. The comparison should be read at the level of those model assumptions.

Why the photosphere is not electron-scattering

Free electrons scatter low-energy photons with the Thomson cross-section $\sigma_{\rm T}$. For fully ionised hydrogen–helium gas, this gives the electron-scattering opacity in equation (6.2). The approximation requires free, non-relativistic electrons and photon energies $h\nu\ll m_{\rm e}c^2$. At higher photon energies the Klein–Nishina cross-section depends on energy and falls below $\sigma_{\rm T}$.

$$ \kappa_{\rm es} \;=\; \frac{\sigma_{\rm T}n_{\rm e}}{\rho} \;=\; \frac{\sigma_{\rm T}}{2m_{\rm p}}(1+X). \tag{6.2}$$

For pure ionised hydrogen, equation (6.2) gives $\kappa_{\rm es}=0.39773\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$. With the adopted present solar-surface hydrogen fraction $X=0.7583$, it gives $0.34966\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$. These values describe fully ionised gas in the Thomson regime, not the largely neutral photosphere.

Solar photospheric hydrogen is mostly neutral at these temperatures, so scattering by free electrons is weak. The ATLAS9 electron density at $\tau_{\rm R}=2/3$ is far below the density implied by complete ionisation. Its Rosseland opacity is instead controlled largely by atomic and ionic processes, including H⁻. Comparing the local Rosseland value with the fully ionised electron-scattering formula would mix different physical states.

The lower bound $\kappa_{\rm R}\ge\kappa_{\rm es}$ applies only if the gas is fully ionised and the scattering opacity stays in the Thomson limit throughout the column. Neither condition holds throughout the solar photosphere, so that bound must not be applied to the ATLAS9 column.

7. Heat that leaks out of a sound wave

Module 4 used an adiabatic approximation for solar acoustic waves. It identified $\omega\chi/c_{\rm s}^2\ll1$ as the condition for negligible thermal leakage, but did not evaluate the gas diffusivity $\chi$. We now estimate it from an ATLAS9 atmosphere. The estimate is informative near the photosphere, where radiative escape is important, yet the diffusion closure becomes unreliable at the free surface.

Proposition 7 (the criterion is a ratio of two times). For a sound wave of cyclic frequency $\nu$, angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi\nu$, speed $c_{\rm s}$ and wavelength $c_{\rm s}/\nu$, in a gas of thermal diffusivity $\chi$, $$ \frac{\omega\chi}{c_{\rm s}^{2}} \;=\; \frac{2\pi}{t_{\rm diff}/\mathcal{P}}, \tag{7.1}$$ where $t_{\rm diff} = (c_{\rm s}/\nu)^2/\chi$ is the time for heat to diffuse across one wavelength and $\mathcal{P} = 1/\nu$ is the period. Adiabatic means $t_{\rm diff}/\mathcal{P} \gg 2\pi$.
$t_{\rm diff}/\mathcal{P} = [(c_{\rm s}/\nu)^2/\chi]\,\nu = c_{\rm s}^{2}/(\nu\chi)$. Then $2\pi/(t_{\rm diff}/\mathcal{P}) = 2\pi\nu\chi/c_{\rm s}^{2} = \omega\chi/c_{\rm s}^2$. ∎

The dimensionless ratio compares thermal diffusion with wave motion. If $\omega\chi/c_{\rm s}^2\ll1$, a wave retains most of its compressional heat during an oscillation and behaves approximately adiabatically. A ratio near unity signals potentially important leakage. It does not by itself give a damping rate, because that requires a wave calculation with the radiative boundary condition.

Local temperature, density, and Rosseland opacity come from two ATLAS9 layers. Using local rather than column-mean opacity is essential because $\chi$ is a local transport coefficient.

8. A gas that contains radiation

Radiation also alters the equation of state. A gas–radiation mixture has pressure from particles and photons, and both components respond to compression. Its adiabatic index therefore depends on the local fraction of total pressure supplied by gas.

Write $P=P_{\rm gas}+P_{\rm rad}$, with $P_{\rm gas}=\rho k_BT/(\mu m_u)$ and $P_{\rm rad}=a_{\rm rad}T^4/3$. Define $\beta=P_{\rm gas}/P$. This is the gas-pressure fraction used in Module 3, not the plasma beta of Module 12. The next proposition computes the first adiabatic index at fixed entropy.

Proposition 8 (the first adiabatic index of a gas-plus-radiation mixture). With $e = 3P_{\rm gas}/(2\rho) + a_{\rm rad}T^{4}/\rho$, the adiabatic index $\Gamma_1 \equiv (\partial\ln P/\partial\ln\rho)_{\rm ad}$ is $$ \Gamma_1 \;=\; \beta \;+\; \frac{(4-3\beta)^{2}(\gamma-1)}{\beta + 12(\gamma-1)(1-\beta)}, \qquad \gamma = \tfrac53, \tag{8.1}$$ which is $5/3$ at $\beta = 1$ and $4/3$ at $\beta = 0$.
Work per unit mass with $v = 1/\rho$ and write $\mathcal{R} = k_B/(\mu m_u)$, so $P_{\rm gas} = \mathcal{R}T/v$ and $e = \tfrac32\mathcal{R}T + a_{\rm rad}T^{4}v$. The first law for an adiabatic change is $\mathrm{d}e + P\,\mathrm{d}v = 0$. Differentiating $e$, $$ \left(\tfrac32\mathcal{R} + 4a_{\rm rad}T^{3}v\right)\mathrm{d}T \;+\; \left(a_{\rm rad}T^{4} + \frac{\mathcal{R}T}{v} + \frac{a_{\rm rad}T^{4}}{3}\right)\mathrm{d}v \;=\; 0. $$ Express every term through $P$ and $\beta$: $\mathcal{R}T/v = \beta P$ and $a_{\rm rad}T^4 = 3(1-\beta)P$. The first bracket times $\mathrm{d}T$ is $Pv\,(\mathrm{d}T/T)\left[\tfrac32\beta + 12(1-\beta)\right]$, and the second bracket is $P\left[3(1-\beta) + \beta + (1-\beta)\right] = P(4 - 3\beta)$. With $\mathrm{d}v/v = -\mathrm{d}\ln\rho$, $$ \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln T}{\mathrm{d}\ln\rho}\right|_{\rm ad} \;=\; \frac{4-3\beta}{\tfrac32\beta + 12(1-\beta)} \;=\; \frac{(4-3\beta)(\gamma-1)}{\beta + 12(\gamma-1)(1-\beta)}, $$ the second form multiplying top and bottom by $\gamma - 1 = 2/3$. Now differentiate the pressure itself: $\mathrm{d}P = P_{\rm gas}(\mathrm{d}\ln\rho + \mathrm{d}\ln T) + 4P_{\rm rad}\,\mathrm{d}\ln T$, so $$ \mathrm{d}\ln P \;=\; \beta\,\mathrm{d}\ln\rho \;+\; (4 - 3\beta)\,\mathrm{d}\ln T. $$ Dividing by $\mathrm{d}\ln\rho$ along the adiabat and substituting the previous line gives (8.1). Both limits. At $\beta = 1$ the fraction is $1\cdot(\gamma-1)/1$, so $\Gamma_1 = 1 + (\gamma - 1) = \gamma = 5/3$. At $\beta = 0$ it is $16(\gamma-1)/[12(\gamma-1)] = 4/3$, and $\Gamma_1 = 4/3$. ∎

Equation (8.1) is Chandrasekhar's gas–radiation expression for $\Gamma_1$. It approaches $5/3$ when gas pressure dominates and $4/3$ when radiation pressure dominates. Those limits provide checks on the algebra. Between them, the local value depends on $\beta$ and cannot be replaced by a universal constant.

Two statements about $\Gamma_1$ must be kept separate. Equation (8.1) gives the local response of a gas–radiation mixture. A stellar model may also use an effective polytropic exponent to describe an extended structure. That structural parameter need not equal the local thermodynamic derivative. A claim about one cannot be transferred to the other without the model assumptions.

At the solar centre, the tabulated model used in Module 3 gives $1-\beta_c=6.1940\times10^{-4}$. Substitution into equation (8.1) therefore leaves $\Gamma_1$ close to the gas-dominated value $5/3$. Radiation pressure is present but is a small correction to the local compressional response in this example.

Proposition 9 (where radiation pressure equals gas pressure). At temperature $T$, $P_{\rm rad} = P_{\rm gas}$ at the density $$ \rho_{\rm eq}(T) \;=\; \frac{a_{\rm rad}\,\mu m_u\,T^{3}}{3k_B}, \tag{8.2}$$ and radiation dominates the pressure where $\rho \lt \rho_{\rm eq}(T)$. At any point, $\rho/\rho_{\rm eq}(T) = \beta/(1-\beta)$.
Setting $\rho k_BT/(\mu m_u) = a_{\rm rad}T^4/3$ and solving for $\rho$ gives (8.2). At fixed $T$, $P_{\rm gas}$ is proportional to $\rho$ and $P_{\rm rad}$ does not depend on it, so $P_{\rm gas} \lt P_{\rm rad}$ exactly when $\rho \lt \rho_{\rm eq}$. Finally $P_{\rm gas}/P_{\rm rad} = \rho/\rho_{\rm eq}$ by the same proportionality, and $P_{\rm gas}/P_{\rm rad} = \beta/(1-\beta)$ by the definition of $\beta$. ∎

For $\mu=0.832$, equation (8.2) gives $\rho_{\rm eq}=2.5236\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ at $10^6$ K and $25.236\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ at $10^8$ K. The crossover density rises as $T^3$. Above it gas pressure dominates; below it radiation pressure dominates, within the assumed ideal-gas and equilibrium-radiation model.

9. The Eddington limit

The radiative force on surrounding gas competes with gravity. For a source of mass $M$ and isotropic luminosity $L$, both forces per unit mass decrease as $r^{-2}$. Their ratio is therefore independent of radius if the flux-mean opacity stays fixed. Setting that ratio to one defines the Eddington luminosity.

Proposition 10 (the Eddington luminosity). Let gas at radius $r$ have flux-mean opacity $\kappa_{\rm F}\equiv(1/F)\int\kappa_\nu F_\nu\,\mathrm d\nu$. Suppose a central mass $M$ radiates luminosity $L$ isotropically. Outward radiative acceleration is smaller than inward gravitational acceleration when $$ L \;\lt\; L_{\rm Edd} \;\equiv\; \frac{4\pi GMc}{\kappa_{\rm F}}, \tag{9.1}$$ provided the stated opacity describes momentum transfer by the radiation field. For fully ionised hydrogen–helium gas in the low-energy Thomson limit, $\kappa_{\rm es}=\sigma_{\rm T}(1+X)/(2m_{\rm p})$. Taking electron scattering as the relevant flux-mean opacity gives, for pure hydrogen $(X=1)$, $$ L_{\rm Edd} \;=\; \frac{4\pi GMm_{\rm p}c}{\sigma_{\rm T}}, \tag{9.2}$$ the form used in Module 9. Additional opacity lowers this force-balance luminosity. At photon energies approaching $m_ec^2$, the scattering cross-section decreases and the Thomson value cannot serve as a spectrum-independent floor.
At radius $r$, isotropic luminosity produces flux $F=L/(4\pi r^2)$. Gas with flux-mean momentum opacity $\kappa_{\rm F}$ experiences outward acceleration $\kappa_{\rm F}F/c$. Gravity supplies inward acceleration $GM/r^2$. The first is smaller when $$ \frac{\kappa_{\rm F}L}{4\pi r^{2}c} \;\lt\; \frac{GM}{r^{2}}, $$ and cancelling $r^2$ gives equation (9.1). In the Thomson regime, scattering is effectively independent of photon frequency for free, non-relativistic electrons. If it dominates momentum coupling, $\kappa_{\rm F}=\kappa_{\rm es}$ and equation (6.2) gives the composition dependence. For $X=1$, equation (9.2) follows. Electrostatic coupling makes the electrons and ions respond together, so the force balance is written per unit mass of plasma. ∎

If opacity tends to zero, radiation transfers negligible momentum and the force-balance luminosity tends to infinity. Conversely, increasing opacity lowers the luminosity at which the outward radiative acceleration matches gravity. This is a local force balance, not a guarantee that gas above the limit escapes or that gas below it accretes steadily.

Eddington's own form

Eddington's original argument set an upper bound on opacity in a hydrostatic star with an inward-increasing gas pressure. It did not derive the electron-scattering luminosity used in modern accretion examples. The following proposition states his result in the notation used here, so its historical scope remains clear.

Proposition 11 (Eddington's opacity bound). Consider a hydrostatic star of total luminosity $L$ and mass $M$. Suppose gas pressure $p_{\rm G}$ increases inward. Also suppose the interior ratio $L_r/M_r$ is at least its surface value $L/M$. The flux-mean opacity $k$ then obeys $$ k \;\lt\; \frac{4\pi cGM}{L}. \tag{9.3}$$ For specified $L$ and $M$, this is a bound on opacity under the stated conditions.
Hydrostatic balance for an inward step $\mathrm{d}r \lt 0$ gives the increase of total pressure $\mathrm{d}P = -\rho(GM_r/r^{2})\,\mathrm{d}r$. The frequency-integrated first moment from the proof of Proposition 4, $c\,\mathrm{d}P_{\rm rad} = -k\rho F\,\mathrm{d}r$ with $F = L_r/4\pi r^2$, gives the increase of radiation pressure. Their ratio is $\mathrm{d}P_{\rm rad}/\mathrm{d}P = kL_r/(4\pi cGM_r)$. Since $\mathrm{d}P = \mathrm{d}p_{\rm G} + \mathrm{d}P_{\rm rad}$ and $\mathrm{d}p_{\rm G} \gt 0$, $\mathrm{d}P_{\rm rad} \lt \mathrm{d}P$, so $kL_r/(4\pi cGM_r) \lt 1$. With $L_r/M_r \ge L/M$, $kL/(4\pi cGM) \le kL_r/(4\pi cGM_r) \lt 1$, which is (9.3). At the surface, $L_r = L$ and $M_r = M$, and (9.3) is (9.1) with $k$ for $\kappa_{\rm F}$: the same inequality. ∎

The historical bound depends on the radial distributions of luminosity and enclosed mass. It applies where gas pressure rises inward and the assumptions of hydrostatic balance hold. The simple Eddington luminosity in equation (9.1) instead compares gravity with radiative acceleration for a specified flux-mean opacity. The two arguments are related but not identical.

Equation (9.1) assumes spherical, isotropic radiation and a specified opacity. A disc or beam has a direction-dependent flux. Dust, bound–free absorption, and spectral changes can alter $\kappa_{\rm F}$; high-energy photons leave the Thomson regime. Gas dynamics, geometry, and time dependence can also permit inflow or outflow in situations where a spherical static balance would suggest otherwise.

10. Accretion at the limit

For accretion at mass rate $\dot M$, define radiative efficiency by $L=\eta_{\rm rad}\dot Mc^2$. The Eddington accretion rate is then $\dot M_{\rm Edd}=L_{\rm Edd}/(\eta_{\rm rad}c^2)$. This convention depends on the chosen efficiency as well as the opacity. We retain $\eta_{\rm rad}=0.1$ for numerical comparisons with Module 9.

Proposition 12 (growth at the Eddington rate). A hole that accretes at $\dot M_{\rm Edd}$ and radiates $\eta_{\rm rad}\dot Mc^2$ keeps $(1-\eta_{\rm rad})\dot M$, and its mass grows as $M \propto e^{t/t_{\rm e}}$ with $$ t_{\rm e} \;=\; \frac{\eta_{\rm rad}\,\kappa_{\rm es}\,c}{4\pi G\,(1-\eta_{\rm rad})}, \tag{10.1}$$ which does not depend on $M$.
The energy radiated, $\eta_{\rm rad}\dot Mc^2$ per unit time, leaves as photons and with it the rest mass $\eta_{\rm rad}\dot M$, so the hole gains $\mathrm{d}M/\mathrm{d}t = (1-\eta_{\rm rad})\dot M$. At $\dot M = \dot M_{\rm Edd} = L_{\rm Edd}/(\eta_{\rm rad}c^{2})$ with (9.1), $$ \frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}t} \;=\; (1-\eta_{\rm rad})\,\frac{4\pi GMc}{\kappa_{\rm es}\,\eta_{\rm rad}c^{2}} \;=\; \frac{M}{t_{\rm e}}, $$ with $t_{\rm e}$ as in (10.1). The right side is linear in $M$, so $M = M_0e^{t/t_{\rm e}}$; $M$ cancelled out of $t_{\rm e}$. ∎

With $\eta_{\rm rad}=0.1$ and pure-hydrogen Thomson opacity, $M/\dot M_{\rm Edd}=4.5049\times10^7$ yr. The black-hole mass grows on a longer e-folding time because a fraction $\eta_{\rm rad}$ of the supplied rest mass leaves as radiation. Proposition 12 includes that factor explicitly.

A mass divided by a supply rate is a characteristic supply time, not automatically a black-hole doubling time. The Bondi example in Module 9 gives $M/\dot M=1.2297\times10^4$ yr for its outer supply estimate. Interpreting this as growth requires knowing what fraction reaches the horizon and what fraction of that mass-energy is radiated.

11. Where radiation matters, and where it does not

The equality $P_{\rm rad}=P_{\rm gas}$ defines a line $\rho_{\rm eq}\propto T^3$ in the density–temperature plane. Figure 5 places the examples considered here relative to that line. It is a local pressure comparison, separate from the luminosity-based Eddington ratio.

The solar centre lies well above the equal-pressure density at its temperature, so gas pressure dominates there. Hotter or more dilute environments can approach radiation-pressure dominance. The plotted values depend on the adopted mean molecular weight and on whether local thermal equilibrium is justified.

For the Sun, the global ratio $L_\odot/L_{\rm Edd}$ is $2.6772\times10^{-5}$ under the stated electron-scattering convention. This does not mean that radiative force is equally small at every layer: local flux, opacity, and enclosed mass matter. In the solar photosphere, electron scattering is not the dominant opacity. Use the ratio as an illustrative global scale, not a local force measurement.

Radiation can also remove heat behind a shock. The molecular-gas example in Module 8 reaches a much larger compression when cooling is efficient than an adiabatic jump permits. That effect involves radiative energy loss, whereas the Eddington balance concerns radiative momentum transfer. A full radiation-hydrodynamics calculation couples both.

K1. A $10\,M_\odot$ black hole accretes at exactly the Eddington rate, with $\eta_{\rm rad} = 0.1$ and $\kappa_{\rm es} = \sigma_{\rm T}/m_{\rm p}$. The hole keeps the fraction $(1-\eta_{\rm rad})$ of what falls in. How long does it take to reach $10^{9}\,M_\odot$?
Solution
By (10.1), $t_{\rm e} = 5.0054\times10^{7}$ yr. The growth factor is $10^{8}$, which is $\ln 10^{8} = 18.420681$ e-folds, so the time is $9.2204\times10^{8}$ yr. Dropping the $(1-\eta_{\rm rad})$ — using the Salpeter time $4.5049\times10^{7}$ yr as the e-folding time — gives $8.2983\times10^{8}$ yr, 10 per cent short. The time does not depend on the seed's mass except through the logarithm of the growth factor.
K2. Module 9 §10's Bondi hole has $M = 10\,M_\odot$, with $M_\odot = 1.9884\times10^{33}$ g, and accretes $\dot M = 5.124\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$ (module09.html:913). $\dot M_{\rm Edd}$ is defined at $\eta_{\rm rad} = 0.1$ and $L_{\rm Edd}$ by (9.2). What luminosity would the hole radiate at $\eta_{\rm rad} = 0.1$, how many times $L_{\rm Edd}$ is it, and at what $\eta_{\rm rad}$ would it sit exactly at $L_{\rm Edd}$?
Solution
$L = 0.1\,\dot Mc^2 = 4.6052\times10^{42}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$, which is $3663.5$ times $L_{\rm Edd} = 1.257065\times10^{39}$ — the same number as $\dot M/\dot M_{\rm Edd}$, because $\dot M_{\rm Edd}$ is defined at the same $0.1$. At $L = L_{\rm Edd}$ the efficiency is $L_{\rm Edd}/\dot Mc^2 = 2.7297\times10^{-5}$. At that assumed efficiency, the luminosity equals the Thomson force-balance scale. This alone does not establish a steady inflow at the Bondi rate: the supplied gas may be lost before reaching the radiating region, and flow geometry and opacity also matter.
K3. Evaluate the adiabaticity parameter $\omega\chi/c_{\rm s}^2$ at the solar acoustic cutoff, $\nu_{\rm ac} = 4497\ \mu$Hz, with ATLAS9's values at $\tau_{\rm R} = 2/3$: $\kappa_{\rm R} = 0.4447\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$, $\rho = 2.6721\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $T = 5910.1$ K, $c_P = 1.6968\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ and $c_{\rm s} = 8.081\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$.
Solution
By (4.4), $\chi = 1.1588\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ (rounded inputs), and with $\omega = 2\pi\nu_{\rm ac}$ the parameter is $0.5014$. By (7.1) the diffusion time across a wavelength is $12.532$ periods. The parameter is linear in $\omega$, so at fixed $\chi$ it is $4497/3090 = 1.4553$ times its value at $\nu_{\rm max}$; against §7's $0.3445$ the ratio is $1.4554$, because the five rounded inputs above give $\chi = 1.1588\times10^{13}$ where §7's full-precision ATLAS9 values give $1.1587\times10^{13}$. The local diffusion estimate is therefore not asymptotically adiabatic at this frequency. Because the layer lies near a free surface, the number is not a computed damping rate.
