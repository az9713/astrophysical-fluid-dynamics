# Twenty-rule textbook prose standard

This edition is a complete prose rewrite of the twelve revised modules. It preserves verified mathematics, physical arguments, numerical results, equations, figures, citations, anchors, and valid HTML while rebuilding the prose from first principles.

1. Write in clear, natural academic English suitable for an advanced undergraduate or beginning graduate textbook.
2. Each paragraph should develop one identifiable idea. Begin with the idea, explain it, and state its physical consequence.
3. Prefer sentences of 15–25 words. Break sentences longer than about 35 words unless their structure is exceptionally clear.
4. Do not join multiple independent claims with commas, semicolons, or long chains of dashes.
5. Remove AI-style rhetoric, dramatic verdicts, slogans, fake suspense, and self-conscious commentary.
6. Avoid expressions such as “the answer lives here,” “the theory pays a debt,” “this module exists to,” “hold that number,” “the premise is spent,” and similar personification.
7. Do not discuss the writing process in the textbook body. Phrases such as “the paper was read,” “this project has no source,” “the generator produced,” “the first draft got this wrong,” and “a shipped module” belong in editorial notes or provenance records.
8. Separate physical statements from source criticism. State the physics in the main text. Put provenance, source limitations, and audit observations in concise source notes.
9. Define every symbol before using it. State assumptions before drawing conclusions from them.
10. Distinguish clearly among derivation, approximation, empirical comparison, and interpretation.
11. Use cautious language only when uncertainty is real. Explain the specific limitation instead of adding generic disclaimers.
12. Preserve useful qualifications, but place each qualification next to the claim it limits.
13. Avoid repetitive announcements such as “this is the point,” “this is the result,” and “this is what the figure shows.” State the result directly.
14. Do not repeatedly describe whether a result is “confirmed” or “refuted.” Explain the numerical comparison and let the evidence support the conclusion.
15. Use transitions based on the scientific argument, not on the module-writing process.
16. Keep paragraphs reasonably short, but do not produce choppy sequences of one-sentence paragraphs.
17. Read each completed section aloud in effect. Revise any passage that sounds bureaucratic, mechanical, theatrical, or unlike something a physicist would naturally write.
18. Check adjacent modules for consistent notation, terminology, and scientific conclusions.
19. Do not preserve an original sentence merely because it is grammatically correct. Preserve it only if it already meets the required textbook standard.
20. Do not modify the existing module files. Write the new edition to a separate folder.

Where appropriate, a paragraph should state the physical question, present the governing relation, explain its terms and assumptions, derive the implication, compare it with observation or simulation, and state what the comparison establishes and what it does not.
