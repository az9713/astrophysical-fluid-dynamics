130 [h2]: 1. Photons carry momentum
132 [p]: A photon of energy $E$ carries momentum $E/c$. A beam that delivers energy at a rate $F$ per unit area therefore delivers momentum at a rate $F/c$ per unit area, and a body that absorbs the beam feels a pressure $F/c$. For sunlight on the Earth that pressure is negligible. Inside a star the same accounting is not, and near a luminous compact object it decides whether gas can fall in at all. To do the accounting one needs to describe radiation that travels in every direction at once.
138 [p]: The second moment is written $P_\nu$ and never $K_\nu$ , which is what most of the transfer literature writes, because $K$ is Module 3 's polytropic constant. That $P_\nu$ is a pressure is the whole point of the first proposition.
146 [p]: The run evaluates $a_{\rm rad} = 7.565733\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{erg\,cm^{-3}\,K^{-4}}$ from the exact SI value of $\sigma_{\rm SB}$. No earlier module fixed a symbol for the Stefan–Boltzmann constant: Module 3 's notation table fixes $a_{\rm rad}$ only, and Module 11 §8 writes the constant as bare $\sigma$ ( module11.html:360 ). The factor $1/3$ is a statement about geometry and not about photons. Its trivial limit is a parallel beam , $I_\nu \propto \delta(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{n}_0)$ along $z$: then $\cos\theta = 1$ wherever the intensity is non-zero, the three integrals in (1.1) are equal, and $P_\nu = u_\nu$. A beam pushes three times harder than an isotropic field of the same energy density. Problem C1 asks for both cases and for a third, a field that fills one hemisphere uniformly and is empty in the other, which also has $P_\nu = u_\nu/3$ — a fact that §5 uses at the surface of a star.
148 [h2]: 2. The transfer equation
154 [p]: Opacity is never written bare $\kappa$ here. The bare glyph already has three meanings in this book — Module 1 's thermal conductivity, Module 4 's evanescent decay rate, and Module 12 's $\kappa_\parallel$, $\kappa_\perp$ — so this module writes $\kappa_\nu$ at one frequency, $\kappa_{\rm R}$ for the Rosseland mean of §4 , $\kappa_{\rm es}$ for electron scattering and $\kappa_{\rm F}$ for the flux mean of §9 . And the emissivity is $j_\nu$ and not the $\eta_\nu$ of many texts, because module12.html:927 refuses a new bare meaning of $\eta$.
164 [p]: The trivial limit. For a uniform source function $S_\nu$, (2.2) integrates to $I_\nu = I_\nu(0)e^{-\tau_\nu} + S_\nu(1 - e^{-\tau_\nu})$. At $\tau_\nu \ll 1$ this is $I_\nu(0) + (S_\nu - I_\nu(0))\tau_\nu$: the gas barely changes the ray. At $\tau_\nu \gg 1$ it is $S_\nu$, whatever came in: a thick enough slab forgets the light that entered it and shows only its own source function, which is $B_\nu(T)$ in thermal equilibrium. That is why a star looks like a black body of roughly its surface temperature. When the extinction includes scattering, $S_\nu$ contains the scattered light and depends on $I_\nu$ itself, so (2.2) is then an integral equation and not a solution; §5 meets exactly that.
166 [h2]: 3. Optical depth
170 [p]: The glyph $\tau$ is already the optical depth in this book: Module 6 's notation table claims it "in $\tau = 2/3$ only", and this module takes it over in full. By (2.2) with no source, a ray survives an optical depth $\tau$ with probability $e^{-\tau}$, so the mean optical depth a photon travels before an interaction is $\int_0^\infty\tau e^{-\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau = 1$ and the mean distance is $\lambda_{\rm ph}$. In the ATLAS9 solar model of §6 , at $\tau_{\rm R} = 2/3$, the photon mean free path is $84.2$ km.
178 [p]: The consequence is that a star is opaque in time as well as in intensity. The photon travels a total path $N\lambda_{\rm ph} = \tau^{2}\lambda_{\rm ph} = \tau L$, which is $\tau$ times longer than the straight line across. §4 turns this into a diffusion coefficient with its factor fixed.
180 [h2]: 4. The diffusion limit
182 [p]: Deep inside a star, where $\tau \gg 1$ over any distance on which the temperature changes, the radiation field is almost isotropic and almost black-body. The flux is then the small anisotropy that a temperature gradient forces on it, and it obeys a diffusion law. The one freedom is which average of $\kappa_\nu$ over frequency appears in it, and that is settled by a definition.
198 [p]: The trivial limit is an isothermal slab. With $\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}z = 0$, (4.2) gives $F = 0$ at any opacity: a region at one temperature is filled with isotropic black-body radiation, and by Proposition 1 an isotropic field carries no flux. The other check is on the weighting. Had one averaged $\kappa_\nu$ directly instead of $1/\kappa_\nu$, a single opaque line would dominate the mean and choke the flux, which is the opposite of what happens: radiation leaks out through the transparent windows, and the harmonic mean (4.1) says so.
208 [p]: The factor $3$ in $t_{\rm diff}$ is the $1/3$ of Proposition 4, and it is the same $1/3$ as in Module 1 §6 's kinetic-theory transport coefficients, $\mu \simeq \tfrac13\rho v\lambda$ ( module01.html:363 ): a flux carried by carriers of speed $v$ and free path $\lambda$ has diffusivity $\tfrac13 v\lambda$, and for photons $v = c$. Proposition 3's random walk had no such factor, because it did not fix the geometry; this is the factor it set aside. The step-1 version of this module's generator carried the $3$ as "the standard three-dimensional correction", with no source; Gate D ruled that it be derived rather than sourced, and it is derived here.
210 [p]: $\chi$ is the diffusivity that Module 4 §7 named "the radiative diffusivity of the stellar gas" and did not evaluate. §7 evaluates it.
212 [h2]: 5. The grey atmosphere
214 [p]: At the surface of a star the diffusion law must fail, because $\tau$ goes to zero and the radiation field becomes one-sided. The simplest model that carries the radiation from the diffusion regime out to the surface is a grey atmosphere, in which $\kappa_\nu$ does not depend on frequency, in radiative equilibrium , in which every layer emits exactly the energy it absorbs so that the flux is constant with depth. That model has a closed-form temperature profile under the Eddington closure.
222 [p]: The trivial limit is $\tau \to \infty$ , and it closes a loop. Deep in the atmosphere (5.1) gives $\mathrm{d}T^4/\mathrm{d}\tau = \tfrac34T_{\rm eff}^4$. Put that into Proposition 4 in its optical-depth form, $F = (c/3)\,\mathrm{d}(a_{\rm rad}T^4)/\mathrm{d}\tau$: the flux is $(ca_{\rm rad}/3)(\tfrac34T_{\rm eff}^4) = (ca_{\rm rad}/4)T_{\rm eff}^4 = \sigma_{\rm SB}T_{\rm eff}^4$ exactly, because $ca_{\rm rad} = 4\sigma_{\rm SB}$. The grey atmosphere at depth is the diffusion law carrying its own flux. The run evaluates the ratio as $1.000000$ (Problem D2). This is a consistency check and cannot fail: the same moment equation and closure derived (5.1).
224 [p]: The Hopf function differs from $2/3$ by little. The run solves the exact grey problem and gets $q_{\rm H}(0) = 0.57730$ against the known $1/\sqrt3 = 0.57735$, and $q_{\rm H}(2/3) = 0.68876$. The closure therefore moves $T$ by $0.412$ per cent at $\tau = 2/3$, and by $3.0$ per cent at $\tau = 0.01$ ; the paragraph after CHECK 4 below uses both.
226 [p]: Which $T_{\rm eff}$. The check below uses $5777$ K, because the model atmosphere it is compared against was built at that value and says so in its header. Everywhere else this book uses the IAU nominal $5772$ K, which is $0.087$ per cent lower. Comparing a model to (5.1) at a $T_{\rm eff}$ it was not built for would put a spurious $0.087$ per cent into every row.

311 [h2]: 6. The anchor: the opacity of the solar photosphere
313 [p]: Module 6 §6 computed the photospheric scale height $H = 142.9$ km and density $\rho = 3.0631\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ ( module06.html:496 – :497 ) from four inputs: $T = 5772$ K, $\mu = 1.2250$, the IAU surface gravity $g = 2.7420\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}$, and a gas pressure $P = 1.2\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$ at $\tau = 2/3$, which module06.html:490 says "comes from a solar atmosphere calculation". It never wrote down an opacity. Yet the photosphere is defined by one: it is the layer at optical depth $2/3$. Those inputs therefore imply an opacity, and the implication can be checked against a published model atmosphere.
315 [p]: The inference, and what it measures. Let $m$ be the mass per unit area above a level, the column mass . The optical depth of that column is $\tau = \int_0^m\kappa_{\rm R}\,\mathrm{d}m'$ whether or not the opacity varies, so $\tau/m$ is exactly the mass-weighted mean opacity of the column, $\bar\kappa_{\rm R}$. Hydrostatic balance, $\mathrm{d}P = g\,\mathrm{d}m$ with $P = 0$ at the top, gives $m = P/g$, and for an ideal gas $P/g = \rho H$ at any level. Demanding $\tau = 2/3$ at Module 6's level therefore gives
318 [p]: The number is from Module 6's printed $\rho$ and $H$; its inputs $P$ and $g$ give $0.15233$, and the difference is the rounding of the printed $\rho H = 4.3772$ against $P/g = 4.3764\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-2}}$. $\mu$ and $T$ cancel: (6.1) is a statement about the pressure at $\tau = 2/3$, and nothing in it comes from convection. It is a mean over the column, and not the opacity at the bottom of it. The two agree only if $\kappa_{\rm R}$ is the same all the way up. By construction the photon mean free path at that mean opacity is $H/\tau = 1.5000\,H$.

373 [h3]: Why the photosphere is not electron-scattering
375 [p]: Electron scattering is the one opacity with no atomic physics in it: every free electron scatters with the Thomson cross-section $\sigma_{\rm T}$, whatever its energy (for $kT \ll m_{\rm e}c^2$). A fully ionised gas of hydrogen mass fraction $X$, the rest helium, has $n_{\rm e} = (\rho/2m_{\rm p})(1+X)$ electrons per unit volume — one per hydrogen nucleus, two per helium nucleus of mass $4m_{\rm p}$ — so
379 [p]: At $X = 1$ this is $\sigma_{\rm T}/m_{\rm p} = 0.39773\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$, and at the Sun's present surface composition, $X = 0.7583$, it is $0.34966$. (The round formula $0.2(1+X)$ gives $0.3517$, which differs by $0.572$ per cent.) Heavier elements, fully ionised, contribute about one electron per two nucleon masses, like helium, so (6.2) holds for them too.
381 [p]: The photosphere is not fully ionised , and that, not the size of its opacity, is why electron scattering does not set it. At $5910$ K hydrogen is almost entirely neutral, and ATLAS9's own electron density at $\tau_{\rm R} = 2/3$ is $2.4815\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, supplied mostly by easily ionised metals. With those electrons, $\sigma_{\rm T}n_{\rm e}/\rho = 6.1780\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$ — $5659.8$ times below the fully ionised value, and $7198.5$ times below ATLAS9's $\kappa_{\rm R}$. The opacity that fills that gap is, by the standard attribution this module does not derive, bound–free and free–free absorption by the negative hydrogen ion, H⁻, a hydrogen atom that has captured one of those scarce electrons. This book does no atomic physics, and H⁻ is refused here : its cross-sections are what ATLAS9 contains and what this module borrows.
385 [h2]: 7. Heat that leaks out of a sound wave
387 [p]: Module 4 §7 derived the Sun's acoustic modes on the assumption that every compression is adiabatic, and then wrote: "By (3.4) it requires $\omega\chi/c^2\ll1$, with $\chi$ now the radiative diffusivity of the stellar gas. That criterion is not evaluated for the Sun in this module. It is expected to hold deep in the star, where photons diffuse slowly, and to fail near the photosphere, where they escape; neither statement is computed here, and the size of the failure is a subject of Module 13" ( module04.html:429 ). It is the book's one explicitly deferred computation, and it comes with a prediction this section can test at one end. In Module 4's criterion $c$ is the sound speed ; in this module $c$ is the speed of light, so the sound speed is written $c_{\rm s}$ and the criterion is $\omega\chi/c_{\rm s}^2 \ll 1$.
395 [p]: So the criterion is not about a scale height at all. It asks whether heat has time to diffuse across a wavelength during one oscillation. If it does not, the compressions keep their heat and the wave is adiabatic; if it does, temperature differences smooth out within a cycle and the wave approaches isothermal. At order unity it is neither, and the wave loses energy to the radiation every cycle. The run checks (7.1) numerically to $10^{-9}$.
397 [p]: The inputs are $\nu_{\rm max} = 3090\ \mu$Hz, so $\omega = 1.941504\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$; Module 4 's photospheric sound speed $c_{\rm s} = 8.0810\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$; Module 6 's $c_P = 1.6968\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$; and $\kappa_{\rm R}$, $\rho$ and $T$ from ATLAS9 at two levels. By (4.4), $\chi = 1.5027\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ at $T = 5772$ K and $1.1587\times10^{13}$ at $\tau_{\rm R} = 2/3$.

487 [h2]: 9. The Eddington limit
489 [p]: Now the destination. A source of luminosity $L$ and mass $M$ sits at the centre, and ionised gas surrounds it. Gravity pulls each gram inward. The radiation streaming outward pushes each gram outward, by the momentum it deposits. Both forces fall as $1/r^2$.
501 [p]: The trivial limit is $\kappa_{\rm F} \to 0$ , and (9.1) gives $L_{\rm Edd}\to\infty$: a gas that does not interact with the radiation feels no force from it, and there is no limit. The cancellation of $r$ is the content of the proposition. Without it there would be a critical radius for a given luminosity; with it there is a critical luminosity for a given mass, the same at every radius.

The three compositions are sourced: $X = 1$ is what the formula means; $X = 0.7583$ is the present solar surface of the BS2005-AGS,OP model, which Module 4 §4 already uses; and $X = 0.7261$ is the same model's initial composition, $1 - Y_i - Z_i$ from Bahcall, Serenelli and Basu's Table 1, which is the apt value for gas a black hole accretes because it has not had the Sun's helium settling. The 1.1 per cent against Eddington's own bound is not traced : his 1926 solar luminosity and mass were not read.
518 [h3]: Eddington's own form
520 [p]: Eddington did not state a limiting luminosity, and he did not use electron scattering. In his Sect. 82, p. 115, of The Internal Constitution of the Stars he derives "an upper limit to the opacity $k$ in any star (perfect gas or not) for which $L$ and $M$ are known by observation", and gives its physical reading on the same page: "the radiation observed to be emitted must work its way through the star, and if there were too much obstruction it would blow up the star".
528 [p]: Eddington writes the ratio $(L_r/M_r)/(L/M)$ as a single symbol and argues that it exceeds one because energy generation is concentrated toward the centre; the symbol is not used here, because module12.html:927 refuses a new bare meaning of the glyph he chose. His "If both temperature and density increase the material pressure $p_G$ must increase" is the condition $\mathrm{d}p_{\rm G} \gt 0$. The name "Eddington limit" is kept because it is what the literature calls (9.1), with the qualification that Eddington stated it as a bound on $k$ and not with $\kappa_{\rm es}$. For the Sun (9.3) gives $k \lt 13061\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$ with this book's constants; he gives $13{,}200$.
530 [p]: The limit's assumptions, each of which can fail. It assumes spherical symmetry, so that the flux at $r$ is $L/4\pi r^2$; a disc or a beam puts the flux where the gas is not. It assumes the opacity is electron scattering; any additional absorption lowers $L_{\rm Edd}$. And it assumes a steady state; a transient can exceed it while it blows the gas away. This module names no observed violation, because none was read.
532 [h2]: 10. Accretion at the limit
534 [p]: An accreting black hole radiates $L = \eta_{\rm rad}\dot Mc^{2}$, where the radiative efficiency $\eta_{\rm rad}$ is Module 9 's notation. Module 9 defines the Eddington accretion rate as $\dot M_{\rm Edd} = L_{\rm Edd}/(\eta_{\rm rad}c^2)$ at $\eta_{\rm rad} = 0.1$ ( module09.html:944 ).
544 [p]: At $\eta_{\rm rad} = 0.1$ and $\kappa_{\rm es} = \sigma_{\rm T}/m_{\rm p}$ the run gives $M/\dot M_{\rm Edd} = 4.5049\times10^{7}$ yr — the Salpeter time , $\eta_{\rm rad}\kappa_{\rm es}c/4\pi G$, the same at $10\,M_\odot$ and at $10^{8}\,M_\odot$ to $3.33\times10^{-16}$ — and $t_{\rm e} = 5.0054\times10^{7}$ yr. The difference is the $(1-\eta_{\rm rad})$: the first number is the e-folding time if the hole kept everything that falls in, and it does not. Problem K1 asks how long a $10\,M_\odot$ seed takes to reach $10^{9}\,M_\odot$.
546 [p]: A sentence Module 9 §10 leaves unsaid. module09.html:913 prints that the Bondi-fed hole "would double its mass in $1.23\times10^{4}$ yr"; the run recomputes $M/\dot M = 1.2297\times10^{4}$ yr. That is exact at constant $\dot M$, and the page states no growth law. Under Bondi's $\dot M \propto M^{2}$ the doubling time halves, to $6.1484\times10^{3}$ yr; at an Eddington-limited $\dot M \propto M$ it is $\ln 2$ times $M/\dot M$, $8.5235\times10^{3}$ yr. This is not a defect in Module 9, which is not edited.

611 [h2]: 11. Where radiation matters, and where it does not
613 [p]: Proposition 9 draws the boundary between gas-pressure and radiation-pressure support as a line $\rho_{\rm eq}\propto T^{3}$, which is a straight line of slope $3$ in the logarithms. Fig. 5 draws it with two points from this book.
657 [figcaption]: The $(\rho, T)$ plane on logarithmic axes. The line is $P_{\rm rad} = P_{\rm gas}$, (8.2), at $\mu = 0.832$; below it, at lower density for the temperature, radiation dominates the pressure. The Sun's tabulated centre ($\rho_c = 150.50\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $T_c = 1.5480\times10^{7}$ K, Module 3's BS2005-AGS,OP row) lies $1614.5$ times above the line's density at the centre's own $\mu = 0.82851$. The photosphere point is ATLAS9 at $\tau_{\rm R} = 2/3$, where $P_{\rm rad}/P = 2.9454\times10^{-5}$.
660 [p]: At the Sun's centre the line lies at $9.3219\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ for $\mu = 0.82851$, against the actual $150.50$: the centre is $1614.5$ times denser than the crossover, and by Proposition 9 that ratio is $\beta/(1-\beta)$, the same statement as $1 - \beta = 6.190\times10^{-4}$ computed from $\rho_c$ and $T_c$ at that $\mu$, which is $0.999382$ of Module 3's $6.1940\times10^{-4}$. Why radiation matters more in more massive stars follows from the slope. Along a sequence of stars of the same structure and composition, $\rho_c \propto M/R^3$, and the hydrostatic central pressure $P_c \propto GM^2/R^4$ with $P_c \propto \rho_cT_c$ gives $T_c \propto M/R$. Then $\rho_c/\rho_{\rm eq}(T_c) \propto (M/R^3)/(M/R)^3 = M^{-2}$: the radius cancels, and each factor of ten in mass moves the centre two decades toward the line. This is the scaling inside Eddington's quartic, whose $(1-\beta)/\beta^4 \propto M^2$ Module 3 solves in its Problem K3.
662 [p]: The Sun as a whole sits at $L_\odot/L_{\rm Edd} = 2.6772\times10^{-5}$, with $\kappa_{\rm es}$ at $X = 0.7583$ (Problem D3). Read it for what it is. By the proof of Proposition 11, at any radius the ratio of the radiative force to gravity is $\mathrm{d}P_{\rm rad}/\mathrm{d}P = \kappa_{\rm F}L_r/(4\pi cGM_r)$, and $L_\odot/L_{\rm Edd}$ is that ratio at the surface for an opacity of $\kappa_{\rm es}$. The photosphere's opacity is not $\kappa_{\rm es}$: its $\kappa_{\rm R} = 0.4447$ is $1.2719$ times larger, and its own electron scattering is $6.1780\times10^{-5}$ ( §6 ). So $2.6772\times10^{-5}$ is the ratio for a fully ionised, electron-scattering surface. With $\kappa_{\rm R}$ in place of $\kappa_{\rm es}$ the ratio would be $3.405\times10^{-5}$, the same order; that is an estimate only, because $\kappa_{\rm F}$ is not $\kappa_{\rm R}$. What Module 3 §11 dropped when it wrote "Ideal gas (3.2), with radiation only in Proposition 6" ( module03.html:795 ) is radiation pressure in the equation of state, and its size is $P_{\rm rad}/P = 1-\beta$: $6.1940\times10^{-4}$ at the centre, Module 3 §9 's value ( module03.html:628 ), and $2.9454\times10^{-5}$ at the photosphere. This module evaluates $1-\beta$ at those two points only. Radiation transport , which sets the temperature gradient through (4.2), is not a correction at all.
664 [p]: The radiating shock. Module 8 §3 ( module08.html:336 ) computed that a $10\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ shock in molecular gas at $10$ K that radiates away its heat compresses the gas by a factor of $2802$, against $3.993$ for an adiabatic shock at the same speed. That is radiation's other role in fluid dynamics — as an energy sink rather than a force — and it is Module 8's result, cited here and not re-derived.
666 [h2]: 12. What this module captures, and what it misses
668 [p]: The module captures radiation as a momentum carrier and as a heat carrier, in the two limits where each has a closed form: the optically thick diffusion law and the grey atmosphere, and the force balance on optically thin gas. What it misses are frequency dependence beyond the Rosseland mean, the time-dependent and moving-medium terms of the transfer equation, and all the atomic physics that sets real opacities.
