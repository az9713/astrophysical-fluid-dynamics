92: Module 7 — Rayleigh–Taylor and Kelvin–Helmholtz
93: Two fluids meet at a surface. If the heavy one is on top, the surface overturns; if they slide past each other, the surface rolls up. Both come out of one dispersion relation with four terms, and every section of this module switches one term on or off. The laboratory cases first — an upside-down glass of water, a jet stream — then the astrophysical ones, where the surface tension is gone and the gravity points the wrong way. Two checks, and they are of different kinds: the non-linear mixing law against a laser experiment at the National Ignition Facility, which has a standard deviation, and the linear theory against Kelvin–Helmholtz vortices on the flank of a coronal mass ejection, which has none and yields a bound on a magnetic field instead of a confirmation.
115: 1. One relation, two instabilities
117: Modules 5 and 6 asked whether a smoothly stratified fluid would stay where it was put. Gravity against pressure gave the Jeans criterion; buoyancy against an adiabatic gradient gave Schwarzschild's, and cooling against heating gave Field's. In all three the fluid varied continuously, and the question was about a gradient.
119: This module asks the same question about a surface . Two fluids of different densities meet across a plane, and the properties jump rather than slide. That is not a special case of the earlier work: a jump is where a gradient's length scale goes to zero, and the earlier criteria, which compare one length against another, have nothing left to compare. A separate calculation is needed, and it produces something the earlier ones did not — a growth rate that depends on the wavelength of the disturbance, so the interface picks a scale.
121: Two mechanisms live on such a surface, and they turn out to be two terms of one formula.
123: Rayleigh–Taylor. Put the heavy fluid on top. Every parcel of it that sinks and every parcel of the light fluid that rises lowers the potential energy of the pair, so the arrangement is unstable and the interface breaks into descending spikes and ascending bubbles. Nothing is flowing; only gravity and the density difference matter. §4 derives the terrestrial case, where surface tension cuts the instability off below a centimetre or two, and §5 removes the surface tension, which is what happens between two plasmas.
125: Kelvin–Helmholtz. Now let the two fluids slide past each other. A ripple on the interface forces the streaming fluid to go faster over its crest, which by Bernoulli lowers the pressure there, which lifts the crest further. Gravity is not needed and may be absent; what is needed is a velocity jump. §6 derives it, and finds that gravity, when it does act across the interface, kills the long waves rather than the short ones — the opposite end of the spectrum from surface tension.
127: The reason to treat them together is that one calculation gives both. §3 derives a single dispersion relation whose four terms are the Kelvin–Helmholtz drive, the Rayleigh–Taylor drive, the capillary restoring force and the magnetic tension. Setting the shear to zero leaves Rayleigh–Taylor; setting gravity to zero leaves Kelvin–Helmholtz; keeping both gives the mixed problem that a real astrophysical interface presents. The five limits of §3 check the relation before it is used on any case that has no closed form, which is the discipline Module 3 applied to its Lane–Emden integrator.
129: The two halves of the module are tested differently, and the difference is worth stating at the start. The Rayleigh–Taylor half is checked against a laboratory measurement — a laser-driven experiment at the National Ignition Facility — and that is the only place in this module where a standard deviation exists. The Kelvin–Helmholtz half is checked against a solar observation which quotes every input to one significant figure and gives no uncertainty at all. §9 says so in its first paragraph, and then shows what such an observation can still be made to yield: not a confirmation, but a bound on a magnetic field that nobody measured.
131: 2. The interface, and what is perturbed
133: Everything in this module is a statement about the following configuration, and every hypothesis it carries is listed here once so that the propositions can name them rather than repeat them.
137: The hypotheses that will later be paid for are worth naming now. Incompressible deletes the sound speed, so nothing derived here can contain a compressible effect — §7 quotes one from the literature precisely because this relation cannot produce it. Inviscid deletes the only dissipation, so the theory has no way to stop short wavelengths growing; §4 estimates where viscosity would take over and says plainly that it does not solve the viscous problem. Semi-infinite deletes every length except the wavelength itself, which is what makes the answer scale-free. Sharp is the assumption §9 finds broken in the observation it uses.
147: Two things follow from Definition 3 alone and are worth having before any algebra. First, $\sigma$ has dimensions of inverse time, so $1/\sigma$ is an e-folding time and a growth is only meaningful against some other clock — the age of a supernova remnant, the duration of an observation. Second, because $\sigma$ will turn out to depend on $k$, the interface does not simply "go unstable": it goes unstable at some wavelengths and not at others, and the one that grows fastest is what an experiment or an image shows.

209: 4. Rayleigh–Taylor in a glass of water
211: Switch off the shear and the field. Equation (3.1) becomes
$$ \sigma^2 \;=\; Agk \;-\; \frac{T_sk^3}{\rho_t+\rho_b}, \tag{4.1}$$
which is the whole of terrestrial Rayleigh–Taylor. Two terms with different powers of $k$ must cross, and where they cross is the answer to a question anyone can ask in a kitchen: fill a glass with water, put a card over it, turn it upside down and take the card away. The water falls out. Put the same water in a drinking straw and it does not.
225: Water over air at $20\ ^\circ$C, with $\rho_t = 0.998207\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $\rho_b = 1.2041\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $T_s = 72.75\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-1}}$, gives $A = 0.997590$ — as close to the maximum $1$ as makes no difference, which is why the air's inertia can be ignored in every hand calculation of this problem.
229: The capillary length is $\sqrt{T_s/(g\Delta\rho)} = 2.728$ mm, and
233: That is the straw. A tube much narrower than $\lambda_c = 1.71$ cm admits no unstable mode, because the longest wave that fits across it is already inside the capillary cutoff, so the water stays in. The comparison is an order-of-magnitude one and is not exact: Definition 1's interface is unbounded, and a tube imposes a boundary condition at its wall whose lowest admissible wavelength is $\lambda_c$ only up to a geometrical factor of order unity that this module does not compute. Fig. 1 draws both curves, with and without the capillary term. What is exact is that the cutoff is a length , fixed by three material constants, and that it is of order a centimetre for water — which is why the demonstration works with a drinking straw and fails with a tumbler.
305: The two cutoffs sit at opposite ends of the spectrum. Upper panel: (4.1) for water over air, drawn twice. Without surface tension, $\sigma = \sqrt{Agk}$ of Proposition 4 rises without limit as the wavelength shortens and there is no fastest mode; with it, the curve turns over at $\lambda_{\max} = 2.9686$ cm and falls to zero at $\lambda_c = 1.7139$ cm, and the ratio of those two wavelengths is $\sqrt3$ exactly by Proposition 2. Lower panel, which belongs to §9.7 and is drawn here for the contrast: the Kelvin–Helmholtz rate for the solar interface of the anchor observation, with and without gravity acting across it. Gravity cuts off the long waves, at $5124$ km, and the observed mode at $7000$ km lies a factor $1.37$ beyond that cutoff. Its axis annotations name three quantities this section has not met: $\Delta U = 20\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ is the shear measured on the flank of a coronal mass ejection, $2.24$ the ratio of the densities on its two sides, and $n_e = 3\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ the coronal electron density, which is an input carried from Module 1 rather than a measurement. All three are set out in §9.1 . The capillary cutoff above and the gravitational cutoff below are the same algebra at opposite ends of $k$.
308: 4.1 Where viscosity would take over, and why it does not here
310: Equation (4.1) has no dissipation in it, so it is silent about whether the fastest mode it predicts is the one a real fluid shows. A crude but sufficient test compares the inviscid growth rate against the rate at which viscosity damps a surface wave.
320: For water, $\nu = 1.0034\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ gives $\lambda_\nu = 0.467$ mm against $\lambda_{\max} = 29.7$ mm, a factor $63.5$. Viscosity is irrelevant to the kitchen experiment, and capillarity alone chooses the pattern. Problem K1 does the same calculation for glycerol, where $\nu$ is a thousand times larger, $\lambda_\nu = 5.207$ cm exceeds $\lambda_{\max} = 2.458$ cm by $2.12$, and the ordering reverses: the same instability, with the opposite cut-off in charge.
322: 4.2 Two fluids that mix: no capillary cutoff at all
324: Proposition 2 assumed $T_s \gt 0$. Interfacial tension exists only between fluids that do not mix. Pour salt water gently on top of fresh and there is a density difference but no skin: the two are miscible, molecules cross the boundary freely, and $T_s$ is essentially zero.
326: Three per cent NaCl by mass at $20\ ^\circ$C has $\rho = 1.0210\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ against fresh water's $0.998207$, giving $A = 0.011288$, some $88$ times smaller than water over air. With $T_s = 0$ equation (4.1) reduces to $\sigma = \sqrt{Agk}$, which has no cutoff and no maximum: every wavelength grows, and shorter ones grow faster.

336: The miscible case is the one that matters for the rest of the module, because two plasmas are always miscible.
338: 5. Rayleigh–Taylor with no skin: the astrophysical case
340: Between two regions of ionised gas there is no interfacial tension. Setting $T_s = 0$ and $B = 0$ in (3.1) leaves the barest possible statement.
350: Proposition 4 is a short statement with a long consequence, and it is worth spelling out because it is a standing hazard in this subject rather than an elegant result.
352: A simulation of this problem has no converged answer. The growth rate rises as $\sqrt{k}$, so whatever the smallest resolved scale is, that is the scale that goes unstable first, and refining the grid changes the answer rather than improving it. The cut-off must come from somewhere the ideal equations do not contain: from viscosity, as in (4.4); from a finite interface thickness, which §9 shows is the realistic case for an astrophysical boundary; or from the magnetic tension term of (3.1), which Module 12 takes up. What Proposition 4 establishes is that something must supply it, and that the ideal fluid model of Modules 2–11 does not.
354: 5.1 A decelerating shell: where $g$ comes from when there is no star
356: Equation (5.1) needs a $g$. For a supernova remnant there is no external gravity worth the name at the relevant radius; the acceleration that matters is the deceleration of the ejecta by the material they sweep up. The ejecta are dense and the ambient medium ahead of them is thin, so in the frame of the decelerating shell the effective gravity points from the dense fluid towards the light one — which is the unstable ordering.
366: Two standard cases bracket the range. The Sedov–Taylor blast wave of Module 8 has $m = 2/5$; a young remnant still driven by its own ejecta has $m \simeq 0.7$. Both are evaluated below, and the point of putting them side by side is that the answer barely moves.

382: 6. Kelvin–Helmholtz, and the two ways gravity stops it
384: Now switch the shear on. With $T_s = 0$ and $B = 0$, (3.1) reads
$$ \sigma^2 \;=\; \frac{k^2\rho_t\rho_b\,\Delta U^2}{(\rho_t+\rho_b)^2} \;+\; A\,g\,k. \tag{6.1}$$
If the light fluid is on top, $A \lt 0$ and the two terms compete. The first rises as $k^2$ and the second only as $k$, so the shear wins at short wavelength and gravity wins at long: the cutoff is at the opposite end of the spectrum from the capillary one.
398: The physical reading is worth keeping, because the sign of the effect is counter-intuitive the first time. A long wave displaces a large volume of fluid; a short wave displaces a small one. Gravity charges by volume and the shear pays by area, so the longer the wave, the worse the bargain. Long waves have too much heavy fluid to lift.

455: 8. After the linear theory: the mixing law, and the laboratory check
457: §5.1 ended with a count: a young supernova remnant is three to twelve e-folding times old at every wavelength from a hundredth of its radius to a tenth of it. Linear theory has therefore been exhausted long before anything is observable, and the quantity an image can measure — how far the two fluids have interpenetrated — is not something (5.1) predicts. A separate claim is needed for the non-linear stage, and it is a claim of a different kind: not a growth rate for each wavelength, but a single width for the whole mixing layer.
477: Hypothesis (iv) is the whole content of the proposition, and it is not a technical convenience. It is the assertion that a mixing layer eventually stops remembering the ripple it grew from — that after enough bubble mergers, the width alone sets the scale. Shimony et al. state it in those words: "We assumed that at the self-similar stage, the initial conditions are forgotten." Everything that follows tests it.
479: When $g$ varies in time, (8.1) generalises by replacing $Agt^2$ with the Read integral $$ x_{\rm Read} \;\equiv\; A\left(\int\!\sqrt{g(t)}\,dt\right)^{2}, \tag{8.4}$$
which for constant $g$ is $Agt^2$ exactly — a consistency the numbers script verifies to ten decimal places. Plotting a mix width against $x_{\rm Read}$ rather than against time is what allows experiments driven by different acceleration histories to be compared on one axis.

497: 8.2 Confirmed: the form of the law
499: What is confirmed is a functional form, not a number, and it is worth being exact about how far the evidence goes. The experiment fielded four initial conditions: a two-dimensional seed at $\langle\lambda\rangle = 30\ \mu$m, the same with a thinner ablator, a three-dimensional seed at $30\ \mu$m, and a two-dimensional seed at $15\ \mu$m. Each gives a mix-width history in time. Plotted against the Read integral (8.3), each becomes linear at late times once the Richtmyer–Meshkov and decompression contributions are removed, and the slope of that line is $\alpha_S + \alpha_B$. Linearity in $x_{\rm Read}$ is exactly (8.1); a layer still governed by its seed would not be linear in it at all.

615: 9. The anchor: Kelvin–Helmholtz on the flank of a CME
617: On 8 April 2010, beginning at 02:34 UT, SDO/AIA observed a flare and coronal mass ejection from an active region at $16^\circ$E, $29^\circ$N. The flare peaked at 03:25 UT at GOES class B3.7 and STEREO/SECCHI measured the ejection at about $500\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Along the boundary of one of the dimming regions left behind by the evacuated plasma, Ofman & Thompson (2011) identified a chain of vortex-shaped features, several to ten arcseconds across, travelling at $6$–$14\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ — the first reported observation of the Kelvin–Helmholtz instability in the solar corona in the extreme ultraviolet.
621: What such an observation can still be made to yield is the subject of this section, and the answer is stated in advance so that no check below has to carry more weight than it can.

829: 9.6 Refuted: the vortex-sheet assumption
831: Their p. 7 justifies treating the velocity jump as a discontinuity because the layer is "much smaller than the wavelength": $a/\lambda = 800/7000 = 0.1143$, a factor $8.75$. But the parameter that decides whether a layer may be treated as a sheet is not $a/\lambda$; it is $ka$, because $k$ is the inverse depth over which the perturbation decays and $a$ is the depth over which the shear is spread. Those are the two lengths that must be compared.

848: 9.7 A counterfactual, not a refutation: the geometry is load-bearing
850: Their p. 8 says the model is run "without gravity (since it is perpendicular to the plane)". That is correct and it is also the most consequential sentence in the setup. The CME flank is radial, so solar gravity lies along the interface and exerts no restoring force across it; Proposition 6, which needs gravity across the interface, does not apply.
852: Suppose it did. The dense corona would be below and the evacuated side above, which is the stable ordering Proposition 6 needs — the other way up is heavy fluid on top, which is Rayleigh–Taylor unstable outright and has no Kelvin–Helmholtz cutoff at all, as problem D2 makes a student notice.
