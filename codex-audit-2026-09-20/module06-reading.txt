116: 1. Two fluids that will not sit still
118: Module 3 solved the hydrostatic equation and built stars out of it. Nothing in that module asked whether the solutions it found would survive being disturbed. A stratified fluid in hydrostatic balance has every force balanced at every point, and that is a statement about the fluid at rest; it says nothing about what happens to a parcel of it that is nudged. Balance is not stability.
120: This module asks the stability question twice, of two different restoring mechanisms, and gets two different instabilities.
122: The first is buoyancy. Displace a parcel upward a little and it expands to the lower ambient pressure. If it arrives denser than its new surroundings it sinks back and the layer is stable. If it arrives lighter it keeps rising, and the layer convects. §2 turns that sentence into an inequality between two logarithmic derivatives, one belonging to the star and one to the gas, and §3 turns the inequality into a frequency. §4 asks what a composition gradient does to it and §5 what partial ionisation does. §6 then asks the quantitative question the criterion cannot answer — how much heat a convecting layer carries — and §7 takes the answer to two measurements of the solar surface.
124: The second is cooling. Compress a parcel of interstellar gas a little, at the pressure of its surroundings. If the compression makes it radiate away energy faster than it gains it, the parcel cools, and at fixed pressure a cooler parcel is denser, so it contracts further. No gravity is involved at all. §8 derives Field's criterion for that runaway, §9 shows that it carves the interstellar medium into two stable phases separated by a narrow band of pressures, and §10 compares the predicted band with 89 measured sight lines. That comparison is the anchor of this module, and it confirms one prediction and refutes another from the same measurement.
126: A terrestrial case sets the difficulty of the first question immediately. The International Standard Atmosphere fixes the tropospheric lapse rate at $6.50\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$, against a dry adiabatic value $g/c_p = 9.761\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$ for dry air. The ratio is $0.6659$, which makes the standard troposphere a polytrope of index $n = 4.2558$, with $\nabla = 1/(n+1) = 0.19026$ against the diatomic adiabatic value $2/7 = 0.28571$. By the criterion of §2 the troposphere is stable, with $33$ per cent to spare. It nevertheless convects every afternoon. The resolution is that air is not dry: a saturated parcel releases about $2.5\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}}$ of latent heat as it rises and condenses, which warms it on the way up and lowers its effective lapse rate to $5$–$6\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$, below the ambient $6.5$. Moist convection is unstable where dry convection is not. Problem C1 works this through.
128: The Sun has no latent-heat term of that kind, so the solar criterion is the clean one, and it is derived first.
130: 2. The displaced parcel and the Schwarzschild criterion
132: The whole of this section follows from the Euler equation of Module 2 and one assumption about the parcel. No transport theory is needed, and none is used.
134: Definition 1 (the two logarithmic gradients). For a stratified fluid in hydrostatic balance, the stratification gradient is the logarithmic temperature gradient of the background, taken along the radius,
$$ \nabla \;\equiv\; \frac{d\ln T}{d\ln P}, \tag{2.1}$$
which is Module 3, Definition 5. The adiabatic gradient is the corresponding derivative for a parcel of the gas at fixed specific entropy $s$,
$$ \nabla_{\rm ad} \;\equiv\; \left(\frac{\partial\ln T}{\partial\ln P}\right)_{\!s}. \tag{2.2}$$
The first is a property of the star; the second is a property of the gas. Fig. 1 sets out the comparison the criterion will make. Notation. In this module the symbol $\nabla$ with no argument always means (2.1). The vector operator is written only with an argument attached, as in $\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{u}$ or $\nabla\Phi$.
172: Setup for Proposition 1. A parcel at the level where the ambient pressure is $P_0$ is displaced upward by $\delta r$ to the level $P_1$. It stays in pressure balance with its new surroundings throughout, and changes state adiabatically, so it arrives at $P_1$ with its own temperature $T_{\rm p}$. Only the comparison of $T_{\rm p}$ with the ambient $T$ at the same pressure decides the sign of the buoyancy force, because at equal pressure a hotter ideal gas of the same composition is the lighter one.
174: Proposition 1 (the Schwarzschild criterion). Consider a plane-parallel stratification of an ideal gas in hydrostatic balance, with (i) uniform chemical composition, so that the mean molecular weight $\mu$ is the same in the parcel and its surroundings, and (ii) $\rho = \mu m_u P/(k_BT)$. Displace a parcel from the level $r$ by $\delta r$ with $|\delta r| \ll H$, where $H \equiv P/(\rho g)$ is the pressure scale height, subject to (iii) instantaneous pressure balance with the surroundings, and (iv) adiabatic change of state. Then to first order in $\delta r$ the density contrast the parcel acquires is
$$ \frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho} \;=\; -\,(\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad})\,\frac{\delta r}{H}, \tag{2.3}$$
and the buoyancy force per unit mass on it is $+g\,(\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad})\,\delta r/H$, directed along $\delta r$. The force therefore restores the parcel to its starting level if and only if
$$ \nabla \;\lt \; \nabla_{\rm ad}. \tag{2.4}$$
180: Write $\Delta$ for the difference between the parcel and its surroundings at the same height, and $\delta$ for the change along the displacement. Both the parcel and the surroundings are ideal gases of the same $\mu$ by (i) and (ii), so $\ln\rho = \ln P - \ln T + \mathrm{const}$ for each, and
182: By (iii) the parcel's pressure is the ambient pressure at every instant, so $\delta\ln P$ is the same number for both. By (iv) the parcel's temperature follows its own adiabat, $\delta\ln T_{\rm p} = \nabla_{\rm ad}\,\delta\ln P$, while the surroundings supply $\delta\ln T = \nabla\,\delta\ln P$ by (2.1). Subtracting,
184: Hydrostatic balance gives $dP/dr = -\rho g$, so $d\ln P/dr = -\rho g/P = -1/H$ and $\delta\ln P = -\delta r/H$ to first order. Substituting gives (2.3).
185: Archimedes' principle in the Euler equation is the statement that a parcel of density $\rho+\Delta\rho$ in surroundings of density $\rho$ feels a net force per unit mass $-g\,\Delta\rho/\rho$ in the direction of increasing $r$. With (2.3) that force is $+g(\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad})\delta r/H$. Since $g$, $H$ and $\rho$ are positive, the force has the sign of $(\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad})\,\delta r$: it points along $\delta r$, and so amplifies the displacement, when $\nabla \gt \nabla_{\rm ad}$, and against $\delta r$, restoring it, when $\nabla \lt \nabla_{\rm ad}$. ∎
188: Four premises were used and each will be paid for later. Premise (i), uniform composition, is lifted in §4 . Premise (iii), instantaneous pressure balance, requires the parcel to move slowly compared with the sound speed, and §6 finds $v_c/c_s = 0.4271$ at the solar photosphere, where the premise is therefore spent. Premise (iv), adiabatic change, requires the parcel to lose no appreciable heat while it travels, and §6 and §7 find that at the photosphere radiation carries $98.7$ per cent of the flux, so it is spent there too. Only premise (ii) survives everywhere this module uses the criterion.

Everything later in the module either changes the left-hand side of (2.4), which §3 does by reading $\nabla$ off a solar model, or the right-hand side, which §5 does by breaking the constancy of $\gamma$.
196: 3. The buoyancy frequency, and where the Sun's convection zone starts
198: Proposition 1 gives a sign. Its magnitude is a frequency, and the frequency is a measurable property of a star.
200: Proposition 2 (the Brunt–Väisälä frequency). Under the hypotheses of Proposition 1, and neglecting any drag on the parcel, the displacement obeys
$$ \ddot{\delta r} \;=\; -N^2\,\delta r, \qquad N^2 \;\equiv\; \frac{g}{H}\,(\nabla_{\rm ad}-\nabla). \tag{3.1}$$
The layer is stable if and only if $N^2\gt 0$, and a displaced parcel then oscillates about its starting level with angular frequency $N$.
205: The parcel has unit mass and the force per unit mass computed in Proposition 1 is $+g(\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad})\delta r/H$. Newton's second law gives $\ddot{\delta r} = g(\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad})\delta r/H = -(g/H)(\nabla_{\rm ad}-\nabla)\,\delta r$, which is (3.1). When $N^2\gt 0$ this is the equation of a harmonic oscillator of angular frequency $N$; when $N^2\lt 0$ its general solution is $A e^{|N|t}+Be^{-|N|t}$ and the growing branch is convection. The coefficients $g$, $H$, $\nabla$ and $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ are evaluated at the starting level and held fixed, which is the same first-order approximation used in Proposition 1. ∎
208: Equation (3.1) is the Schwarzschild criterion again, with a rate attached. It also identifies what the criterion costs when it is close: $N^2$ is a small difference of two numbers of order $0.4$, so a model table has to resolve $\nabla$ well to give $N^2$ at all inside a convection zone. That limit is stated below and respected.

245: Read the $\nabla$ column of Table 1 outward, from the centre to the surface. It falls through $0.2116$ at $0.3016\,R$ to a shallow minimum — $0.1968$ at $0.4002\,R$ and $0.1962$ at $0.5018\,R$ — and then rises : $0.2074$ at $0.5979\,R$, $0.2616$ at $0.6804\,R$, $0.3259$ at $0.7099\,R$, and $0.3963$ at $0.7500\,R$. Across the whole interval $0.4002\,R$ to $0.7500\,R$, $\nabla$ rises by a factor $2.014$. The interval is not monotone, and the sampling matters: the rows printed above dip to $0.1962$ at $0.5018\,R$, while the full profile reaches its minimum of $0.1937$ at $0.4531\,R$, a radius Table 1 does not sample. Measured from that minimum the factor is $2.046$. Either way the rise carries $\nabla$ straight across the flat adiabatic plateau $0.3957$. The base of the convection zone is where the crossing happens, at $0.7269\,R$, which is $T = 2.02\times10^{6}$ K in the same table.
247: It is worth being exact about which side moves. In this star the convection zone opens because $\nabla$ rises to meet $\nabla_{\rm ad}$, not because $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ falls to meet $\nabla$: over the whole radiative interior $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ is the flat plateau value. A radiative layer carries its flux with $\nabla = \nabla_{\rm rad}\propto \kappa L P/(m\,T^4)$, so a rising opacity $\kappa$ and a falling temperature both push $\nabla$ up, and both are happening here. This module computes neither. It reads the rise off the model and does not apportion it between opacity and temperature; doing so would need the opacity tables the model was built with, which are not part of this module. §5 shows the other mechanism, the one that moves $\nabla_{\rm ad}$, and shows that it operates somewhere else entirely.

348: 4. Ledoux: what a composition gradient does
350: Premise (i) of Proposition 1 was uniform composition. The Sun has burned hydrogen in its core for $4.6$ Gyr, so its $\mu$ is not uniform, and the premise has to be paid for.
352: Proposition 3 (the Ledoux criterion). Keep every hypothesis of Proposition 1 except (i), and let the composition of the surroundings vary with depth, with $\nabla_\mu \equiv d\ln\mu/d\ln P$, while the displaced parcel carries its own composition unchanged. Then (2.3) becomes
$$ \frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho} \;=\; -\left[\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad}-\nabla_\mu\right]\frac{\Delta r}{H}, $$
the buoyancy frequency becomes
$$ N^2 \;=\; \frac{g}{H}\left[\nabla_{\rm ad}-\nabla+\nabla_\mu\right], \tag{4.1}$$
and the layer is stable if and only if $\nabla \lt \nabla_{\rm ad} + \nabla_\mu$. Here $\Delta r$ is the displacement written $\delta r$ in Proposition 1; it is renamed for this proof only, so that no symbol has to serve both as a variation and as a coefficient.
359: For an ideal gas $\rho = \mu m_uP/(k_BT)$, so along the displacement $\Delta\ln\rho = \Delta\ln P - \Delta\ln T + \Delta\ln\mu$ for the surroundings, and the same without the last term for the parcel, which carries its composition unchanged. The parcel and its surroundings share $\Delta\ln P$ by premise (iii), so those terms cancel in the difference exactly as in Proposition 1. The temperature terms give $-(\nabla_{\rm ad}-\nabla)\Delta\ln P$ as before, and the composition term gives $-\nabla_\mu\,\Delta\ln P$. Subtracting the surroundings from the parcel,
361: and $\Delta\ln P = -\Delta r/H$ as before gives the displayed contrast. Newton's second law with the buoyancy force $-g\Delta\rho/\rho$ then gives (4.1), and stability is $N^2\gt 0$, which is the stated inequality. Setting $\nabla_\mu=0$ recovers (3.1). ∎
362: For a general equation of state the two composition coefficients $\delta \equiv -(\partial\ln\rho/\partial\ln T)_{P,\mu}$ and $\varphi \equiv (\partial\ln\rho/\partial\ln\mu)_{P,T}$ do not both equal $1$, and (4.1) carries a factor $\delta$ outside the bracket and $\varphi/\delta$ on $\nabla_\mu$ (Kippenhahn & Weigert, section 6.1). Every gas this module treats is an ideal gas, so that generality is quoted and not used.
365: The sign is the physical content. A $\mu$ that increases inward means $\nabla_\mu \gt 0$, since $P$ also increases inward; a parcel carried up from such a layer keeps its heavy composition and arrives denser than its new surroundings, which stabilises the stratification even where $\nabla \gt \nabla_{\rm ad}$.
367: Table 1's $\nabla_\mu$ column shows the Sun's. The $\mu$ used there is the table's own, $\mu = \rho k_BT/(P\,m_u)$, so no ionisation assumption enters. The share of $N^2$ contributed by the Ledoux term is $+84.05$ per cent at $0.050\,R$ ($\mu = 0.77684$, $\nabla_\mu = +0.37196$), $+74.96$ per cent at $0.100\,R$ ($\mu = 0.69264$, $\nabla_\mu = +0.22043$), $+50.74$ per cent at $0.150\,R$, $+16.53$ per cent at $0.200\,R$, $+3.07$ per cent at $0.251\,R$, $+1.37$ per cent at $0.302\,R$, $+0.67$ per cent at $0.400\,R$ and $+0.87$ per cent at $0.598\,R$. The gradient left by hydrogen burning is large where it exists and dies away outside the burning core.
369: Does the Ledoux term move any boundary in the Sun? No, and the measured size of the "no" is worth printing. The Ledoux term matters only where the two criteria could disagree, which is where $\nabla$ is close to $\nabla_{\rm ad}$. Where $\nabla_\mu$ is large, inside $0.2\,R$, the Schwarzschild margin $\nabla_{\rm ad}-\nabla$ runs from $0.0706$ at $0.050\,R$ to $0.1328$ at $0.200\,R$, so those layers are stable by a wide margin under either criterion. Through the radiative zone outside the burning core, over $0.30$–$0.60\,R$, $|\nabla_\mu| \le 0.00256$. Locating the base of the convection zone again with $\nabla_{\rm ad}+\nabla_\mu$ in place of $\nabla_{\rm ad}$, using the same locator, moves it from $0.7269\,R$ to $0.7306\,R$: a shift of $0.0037\,R$, or $0.37$ per cent of the solar radius. And that is an upper bound, because the $\nabla_\mu$ the table gives near the base is partly the effective-$\mu$ artefact described next.
373: The case where the choice of criterion does matter is a star with a receding convective core, which leaves a large $\nabla_\mu$ and a near-neutral layer in the same place. Such a layer is Schwarzschild-unstable and Ledoux-stable at once, and mixes on a thermal rather than a dynamical timescale — semiconvection. Problem D2 puts numbers on it and shows why the Sun escapes the ambiguity. This module does not develop semiconvection further.
375: The right-hand side of (2.4) has been treated so far as the constant $0.4$. It is not a constant.
376: 5. The collapse of $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ in a partially ionised gas
378: Equation (2.5) assumed a constant $\gamma$. In a gas that is partly ionised, some of the energy a compressed parcel receives goes into pulling electrons off atoms instead of into raising the temperature, and the parcel therefore heats less for the same compression. That lowers $\nabla_{\rm ad}$, and it lowers it a long way.
380: Definition 2 (Saha equilibrium for pure hydrogen). Let $x \equiv n_{\rm II}/n_{\rm H}$ be the ionisation fraction, with $n_{\rm H}$ the total density of hydrogen nuclei, so that $n_e = n_{\rm II} = x\,n_{\rm H}$ and $n_{\rm I} = (1-x)\,n_{\rm H}$. Saha's equation with the statistical weight ratio $g_{\rm II}/g_{\rm I} = 1/2$ for hydrogen is
$$ \frac{n_e\,n_{\rm II}}{n_{\rm I}} \;=\; \left(\frac{2\pi m_ek_BT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\chi_{\rm H}}{k_BT}\right) \;\equiv\; A(T), \tag{5.1}$$
with $\chi_{\rm H} = 13.5984340$ eV the ionisation energy of hydrogen.
384: The total particle density is $n_{\rm H}(1+x)$, so the gas pressure is $P = n_{\rm H}(1+x)k_BT$. Eliminating $n_{\rm H}$ between this and (5.1) gives a closed form with no iteration:
388: For a partially ionised pure-hydrogen gas, the first law with the ionisation energy included in the internal energy gives
$$ \nabla_{\rm ad} \;=\; \frac{2+x(1-x)\Phi}{5+x(1-x)\Phi^2}, \qquad \Phi \;\equiv\; \frac{5}{2}+\frac{\chi_{\rm H}}{k_BT}. \tag{5.3}$$
393: Proposition 4 (the ionisation zone lowers $\nabla_{\rm ad}$). Assume (5.3), with $x$ from (5.2) and $\chi_{\rm H} \gt 0$. Then $\nabla_{\rm ad} \lt 2/5$ at every $0\lt x\lt 1$, and $\nabla_{\rm ad} = 2/5$ exactly at $x=0$ and $x=1$.
396: Write $q \equiv x(1-x)$, which is positive for $0\lt x\lt 1$ and zero at both ends. Comparing (5.3) with $2/5$ by cross-multiplication, and noting that both denominators are positive,
398: Now $\Phi = 5/2 + \chi_{\rm H}/(k_BT) \gt 5/2$ for every finite $T$, so $2\Phi-5 \gt 0$ and $\Phi\gt 0$. The inequality therefore holds exactly when $q\gt 0$, that is for $0\lt x\lt 1$. At $x=0$ or $x=1$, $q=0$ and (5.3) returns $2/5$, the monatomic value of (2.5). ∎
401: The size of the effect is set by $\Phi$, and $\Phi$ is large. At the temperature where the depression is deepest on the photospheric isobar, $T = 12266$ K, $\chi_{\rm H}/k_BT = 12.86$ and so $\Phi = 15.36$; at the hotter temperature where the gas is half ionised, $T = 13136$ K, it is $12.01$. Either way the ionisation reservoir a parcel can pour heat into is about twelve times the thermal reservoir of one particle, which is why the depression is not a small correction. 421: 5.1 What this explains, and what it does not
423: It would be natural to say that the collapse of $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ is what makes the outer part of the Sun convective. The model table says otherwise, and the numbers are unambiguous.
426: The base of the convection zone is at $0.7269\,R$, where the table gives $T = 2.02\times10^{6}$ K. At that temperature hydrogen and helium are fully ionised, $x=1$, and (5.3) returns $\nabla_{\rm ad}=2/5$. The measured plateau, $0.3957$, is the evidence: it is the ideal value to within the model's equation-of-state corrections, right across the convection zone.
427: The collapse of Table 2 sits between about $1.06\times10^{4}$ and $1.68\times10^{4}$ K, the band Problem K1 maps.
428: The outermost row of the table, $0.98308\,R$, is already at $T = 8.12\times10^{4}$ K — above the whole collapse band.
431: So the hydrogen ionisation zone lies outside $0.983\,R$, in the outermost $1.7$ per cent of the radius. It does not open the convection zone, whose outer $27.3$ per cent by radius, from $0.7269\,R$ to the surface, is set the other way round by $\nabla$ rising, as §3.2 showed. What Proposition 4 explains is why convection reaches all the way up to the photosphere instead of stopping below it, and why the strongly superadiabatic layer of §6 sits where it does.
433: Two mechanisms, two depths. $\nabla$ rising to meet a flat $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ sets the base of the solar convection zone at $0.7269\,R$. $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ collapsing by the factor $4.23$ in the partial-ionisation zone sets its top, above $0.983\,R$, where $\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad}$ runs from $1.9\times10^{-6}$ to $0.61$.
435: The criterion has now been derived, corrected for composition, and corrected for ionisation. It still says only whether convection happens. It says nothing about how much heat it carries.
437: 6. Mixing-length theory, and how superadiabatic the Sun must be
439: A stability criterion cannot give a flux, because it contains no statement about how far a parcel travels before it merges with its surroundings. Mixing-length theory supplies exactly one such statement and derives everything else from it.
441: Definition 3 (the mixing length). A convecting parcel is assumed to rise a distance $\ell$, keeping its identity, and then to dissolve into its surroundings and deliver its excess heat. The mixing length is taken proportional to the local pressure scale height,
$$ \ell \;\equiv\; \alpha\,H, \qquad H = \frac{P}{\rho g}, \tag{6.1}$$
with $\alpha$ a dimensionless parameter of order unity. This module uses $\alpha = 2.0$. Notation. Within this module $\alpha$ is the mixing-length parameter and nothing else. Module 5's $\alpha$, the length scale of the isothermal sphere, is not meant here.
448: Proposition 5 (mixing-length flux and velocity, efficient limit). Let a layer have $\nabla \gt \nabla_{\rm ad}$ and write the superadiabatic excess $\Delta\nabla \equiv \nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad}$. Assume the parcel loses no heat while it travels, so that it follows its own adiabat over the whole mixing length, and take the ideal-gas value $\delta = 1$. Adopt the two standard averaging conventions: the parcel's mean displacement over its life is $\ell/2$, and half the buoyancy work done over that displacement becomes the parcel's kinetic energy. Then the mean temperature excess a parcel carries, its velocity, and the convective flux are
$$ \delta T \;=\; T\,\Delta\nabla\,\frac{\ell}{2H}, \qquad v_c \;=\; \frac{\ell}{H}\sqrt{\frac{gH\,\Delta\nabla}{8}}, \tag{6.2}$$
$$ F \;=\; \rho\,v_c\,c_P\,\delta T \;=\; \rho\,c_P\,T\,\alpha^2\sqrt{\frac{gH}{32}}\;\Delta\nabla^{3/2}. \tag{6.3}$$
Consequently, at fixed $F$, $\rho$, $c_P$, $T$, $g$ and $H$, $\Delta\nabla\propto\alpha^{-4/3}$ and $v_c\propto\alpha^{1/3}$.
454: Proposition 1 gives the temperature difference a parcel acquires after rising a distance $\Delta r$: subtracting the two logarithmic temperature changes at equal pressure, $\Delta T/T = \Delta\nabla\,\Delta r/H$. Averaging over the parcel's life, which is taken to be the mean displacement $\ell/2$, gives the first of (6.2).
455: For the velocity, the buoyancy force per unit mass at displacement $\Delta r$ is $-g\,\Delta\rho/\rho = g\,\Delta T/T = g\,\Delta\nabla\,\Delta r/H$, using $\Delta\rho/\rho = -\Delta T/T$ for an ideal gas at fixed pressure. The work done over the mean displacement is
$$ W = \int_0^{\ell/2} \frac{g\,\Delta\nabla}{H}\,\Delta r\;d(\Delta r) = \frac{g\,\Delta\nabla\,\ell^2}{8H}. $$
Half of that work is assumed to go into the kinetic energy of the parcel and half into pushing the surroundings aside, so $\tfrac12 v_c^2 = \tfrac12 W$, that is $v_c^2 = g\Delta\nabla\,\ell^2/(8H)$, which is the second of (6.2).
458: The enthalpy a parcel carries per unit mass is $c_P\,\delta T$, and the mass flux carried upward by the convecting fluid is $\rho v_c$, so $F = \rho v_c c_P \delta T$. Substituting (6.2) and $\ell = \alpha H$,
$$ F = \rho c_P\,T\,\Delta\nabla\frac{\alpha}{2}\cdot\alpha\sqrt{\frac{gH\Delta\nabla}{8}} = \rho c_P T\,\alpha^2\sqrt{\frac{gH}{32}}\,\Delta\nabla^{3/2},$$
since $\tfrac12\sqrt{1/8} = \sqrt{1/32}$. Holding $F$, $\rho$, $c_P$, $T$, $g$ and $H$ fixed and varying $\alpha$ in (6.3) gives $\Delta\nabla\propto\alpha^{-4/3}$, and putting that into (6.2) gives $v_c\propto\alpha\cdot\Delta\nabla^{1/2}\propto\alpha^{1/3}$. ∎
463: The factors $1/2$ in $\delta T$ and $1/8$ in $v_c^2$ are the two averaging conventions of the standard theory. A different convention would move both by a numerical factor, which is absorbed into a different calibrated $\alpha$; it changes no physics and no scaling. What the scalings say is that $\alpha$ is a weak handle on the velocity and a strong one on the excess, and Problem C3 exploits exactly that.

579: 8. Thermal instability: the loss function and Field's criteria
581: The question is the same one Proposition 1 asked — a parcel is perturbed, does the perturbation grow? — with a different restoring mechanism. Compress a parcel of interstellar gas slightly at the pressure of its surroundings. Its density rises, so it radiates more; if it also loses the ability to be reheated fast enough, it cools, and at fixed pressure a cooler parcel is denser still. Nothing in this argument involves gravity, buoyancy or a stratification.
583: Definition 4 (Field's net loss function). Let $\mathcal{L}(\rho,T)$ be the energy losses minus the energy gains of the gas, per gram per second, exclusive of thermal conduction (Field 1965, p. 532). Thermal equilibrium is $\mathcal{L} = 0$. Notation. The loss function is always written $\mathcal{L}$ in this module. The plain letter $L$ is the solar luminosity, as in §6 . The cooling coefficient below is always written $\Lambda(T)$ with its argument shown, to keep it distinct from Module 1's Coulomb logarithm $\ln\Lambda$, which reappears in Problem K3.
586: Proposition 6 (Field's criteria, 1965, eqs. 4a and 4b). Perturb a parcel in thermal equilibrium by $\delta T$. Two idealised paths are compared. A parcel held at constant density runs away if and only if
$$ \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!\rho} \lt 0 \qquad\text{(isochoric, Field 4a)}, \tag{8.1}$$
and a parcel that stays in mechanical equilibrium with its surroundings throughout — which requires the perturbation to evolve slowly compared with its own sound-crossing time, and so is perturbed at constant pressure — runs away if and only if
$$ \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P} \;=\; \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!\rho} - \frac{\rho_0}{T_0}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\rho}\right)_{\!T} \;\lt \;0 \qquad\text{(isobaric, Field 4b)}. \tag{8.2}$$
If $(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\rho)_T \gt 0$, as it is for any cooling rate that increases with density, then (8.1) implies (8.2), and there exist states satisfying (8.2) but not (8.1): the isobaric criterion is the weaker condition, and is met over a wider range of states.
593: Let the gas sit at $(\rho_0,T_0)$ with $\mathcal{L}(\rho_0,T_0) = 0$, and perturb the temperature by $\delta T$. The energy equation gives $c\,\dot{\delta T} = -\delta\mathcal{L}$ for the appropriate specific heat $c\gt 0$, so the perturbation grows exactly when $\delta\mathcal{L}$ and $\delta T$ have opposite signs, that is when the derivative of $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to $T$ along the path of the perturbation is negative. At constant density that derivative is $(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial T)_\rho$, which is (8.1).
594: At constant pressure the density is not free: for an ideal gas of fixed composition $\rho \propto P/T$, so $(\partial\rho/\partial T)_P = -\rho_0/T_0$. The chain rule then gives
$$ \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P} = \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!\rho} + \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\rho}\right)_{\!T}\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P} = \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!\rho} - \frac{\rho_0}{T_0}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\rho}\right)_{\!T},$$
which is (8.2). The subtracted term is positive when $(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\rho)_T\gt 0$, so $(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial T)_P \lt (\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial T)_\rho$, and a state satisfying (8.1) satisfies (8.2) while the converse fails whenever the two derivatives straddle zero. ∎
599: Which criterion applies is not a matter of taste. A parcel held at fixed density but given a different temperature is at the wrong pressure for its surroundings, and the force equation will not let it stay there: it expands or contracts on a sound-crossing time, which in the interstellar medium is far shorter than the cooling time of the warm gas. The isobaric criterion is the one compatible with the momentum equation, and that was Field's point in 1965 against Parker's earlier isochoric treatment. Because (8.2) is the weaker condition, far more of the interstellar medium is unstable than the isochoric criterion would suggest.
603: 8.1 The cooling function, tested before it is used
605: The interstellar gas of this section is heated by photoelectric emission from small grains and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons at a rate $\Gamma$ per hydrogen nucleus, taken independent of density and temperature, and cooled at a rate $n^2\Lambda(T)$ per unit volume. Koyama & Inutsuka (2002) fitted $\Lambda(T)$ over the range of the neutral medium with two terms: Lyman-$\alpha$ cooling, which switches on above about $8000$ K, and the [C II] $158\ \mu$m fine-structure line, whose $92$ K excitation temperature appears explicitly in the exponent and which is what cools the cold neutral medium.
610: The corrected function, used everywhere below, is
614: Proposition 7 (the criterion for this heating law). Let the volumetric heating be $n\Gamma$ and the volumetric cooling $n^2\Lambda(T)$, with $\Gamma$ constant, $\Lambda(T)\gt 0$ and differentiable, and $n = \rho/m_{\rm H}$. Then thermal equilibrium is
$$ n_{\rm eq}(T) \;=\; \frac{\Gamma}{\Lambda(T)}, \tag{8.4}$$
and Field's isobaric runaway condition (8.2) reduces at every equilibrium state to $d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T \lt 1$, so that the gas is stable against an isobaric perturbation exactly when
$$ \frac{d\ln\Lambda}{d\ln T} \;\gt \; 1 \quad\text{for stability}. \tag{8.5}$$
620: Per gram, the losses are $n^2\Lambda/\rho = \rho\Lambda/m_{\rm H}^2$ and the gains are $n\Gamma/\rho = \Gamma/m_{\rm H}$, so
$$ \mathcal{L}(\rho,T) = \frac{\rho\,\Lambda(T)}{m_{\rm H}^2} - \frac{\Gamma}{m_{\rm H}} .$$
Setting $\mathcal{L}=0$ gives $\rho = m_{\rm H}\Gamma/\Lambda(T)$, that is (8.4). Differentiating,
$$ \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!\rho} = \frac{\rho}{m_{\rm H}^2}\frac{d\Lambda}{dT}, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\rho}\right)_{\!T} = \frac{\Lambda}{m_{\rm H}^2},$$
so (8.2) reads
$$ \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P} = \frac{\rho}{m_{\rm H}^2}\left[\frac{d\Lambda}{dT} - \frac{\Lambda}{T}\right] \lt 0 \iff \frac{T}{\Lambda}\frac{d\Lambda}{dT} \lt 1,$$
after dividing by the positive factor $\rho\Lambda/(m_{\rm H}^2T)$. The layer is therefore stable exactly when $d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T \gt 1$, which is (8.5). Note also that $(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\rho)_T = \Lambda/m_{\rm H}^2\gt 0$, so Proposition 6's ordering of the two criteria applies: the isochoric runaway condition here is $d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T \lt 0$, which is strictly harder to meet than $d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T \lt 1$, so every isochorically unstable state on this curve is isobarically unstable and not conversely. ∎

651: 9. The two-phase window
653: Proposition 8 (the pressure window). On the thermal-equilibrium curve (8.4), assume further that $d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T - 1$ changes sign exactly twice on the range considered, which Table 4 verifies for (8.3). Then the equilibrium pressure satisfies
$$ \frac{P}{k_B} \;=\; n_{\rm eq}(T)\,T \;=\; \frac{\Gamma\,T}{\Lambda(T)}, \qquad \frac{d\ln P}{d\ln T} \;=\; 1 - \frac{d\ln\Lambda}{d\ln T}. \tag{9.1}$$
Hence $P$ has a turning point exactly where $d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T = 1$, the boundary case of (8.5), and $dP/dn\lt 0$ exactly on the unstable branch. Between the local minimum $P_{\min}$ and the local maximum $P_{\max}$ of the curve, a single pressure is attained at three densities, of which the two extreme ones are stable and the middle one is not: two stable phases coexist at the same pressure.
658: (9.1) is (8.4) multiplied by $T$, and its logarithmic derivative. A turning point of $P$ along the curve is $d\ln P/d\ln T = 0$, which by (9.1) is $d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T = 1$, which is the boundary case of (8.5). For the density, (8.4) gives $d\ln n/d\ln T = -\,d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T \equiv -s$, so on any part of the curve with $s\neq0$,
$$ \frac{d\ln P}{d\ln n} = \frac{1-s}{-s} = 1 - \frac{1}{s}.$$
For $s\gt 1$, which is the stable cold branch of Table 4, this is positive and less than $1$; for $0\lt s\lt 1$, the unstable branch, $1/s\gt 1$ and the expression is negative, so $dP/dn\lt 0$ there. A continuous curve that rises, falls and rises again in $P$ as $n$ increases takes every value of $P$ strictly between the local minimum and the local maximum at three densities, one on each monotone piece. The outer two lie on branches with $s\gt 1$, which satisfy (8.5) and are stable; the middle one lies on the falling branch, which does not. ∎

750: 10. The anchor: Jenkins & Tripp (2011) against the window
752: Jenkins & Tripp (2011) measured thermal pressures in the diffuse cold neutral medium along 89 sight lines, from the fine-structure excitation of neutral carbon observed with the STIS echelle spectrograph on the Hubble Space Telescope. Their mass-weighted lognormal fit, their equation (3), has mean $\log(p/k) = 3.58$ dex and an rms dispersion $\sigma_{\log P} = 0.175$ dex, which they state is a lower limit. The median is $3801.9\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$ and the $\pm1\sigma$ range is $2541$ to $5689\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$.
754: This module takes two predictions to that measurement, in the shape Module 2 used: one confirmed and one refuted, from the same source table.
756: 10.1 Confirmed: the measured centre lies inside the predicted window
758: CONFIRMED. The predicted window is $[1960, 4810]\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$ with predicted average pressure $3070$. The measured median $3801.9\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$ lies inside it, at a ratio $1.238$ to the predicted average, and $0.738$ of the way from $P_{\min}$ to $P_{\max}$ measured in dex.
760: The content of that confirmation is that a factor-$2.5$ window exists at all, and sits where it does. Neither of its edges was fitted to a pressure value. Wolfire et al. computed $P_{\min}$ and $P_{\max}$ from grain photoelectric heating and [C II]/[O I] cooling, with gas-phase carbon and oxygen abundances fixed by independent Hubble absorption-line studies and a PAH abundance fixed by ISO infrared emission data. One heating parameter, $\varphi_{\rm PAH} = 0.5$, was calibrated to an observed C I/C II column-density ratio; Wolfire's own detailed working credits that ratio to the C I and H I data compiled by Welty & Hobbs (2001), although an earlier passage cites Jenkins & Tripp (2001) alongside it. Jenkins & Tripp (2001) is the predecessor of, and distinct from, the 2011 pressure measurement compared here.
764: 10.2 How far the confirmation survives its own parameters
766: $\varphi_{\rm PAH}$ is a real lever on the window, not a formality. Wolfire's Table 4 tests five model variants, three of which vary $\varphi_{\rm PAH}$ discretely while two hold it at $0.5$ and vary other parameters. Raising $\varphi_{\rm PAH}$ raises all three pressures monotonically, and the authors state the size of the effect themselves: over $\varphi_{\rm PAH} = 0.25$ to $1$, $P_{\min}$ changes by a factor $1.5$ and $P_{\max}$ by $1.9$, with $P_{\max}$ decreasing by about $35$ per cent at $\varphi_{\rm PAH} = 0.25$.

780: What mitigates that must be stated as precisely as the defect, and it is stronger than one row. Wolfire et al. disfavour every non-standard variant, on evidence that is not a pressure. Their four-item summary reads, in their order: "Low $\varphi_{\rm PAH}$" models poorly match the C I*/C$_{\rm tot}$ ratio and the C$^+$ cooling rate, with values lower than observed; "High $\varphi_{\rm PAH}$" models produce temperatures and C I/C II ratios higher than observed; "Low PAH" — Table 4's "Low $n_{\rm PAH}/n$" — gives low C I*/C$_{\rm tot}$ ratios and C$^+$ cooling rate; "Low $G_0$" gives low C I*/C$_{\rm tot}$ and high C I/C II. Separately, they write that only "High $\varphi_{\rm PAH}$" and "Low $G_0$" "can be safely ruled out by the required C I/C II ratio".
782: The two tiers must be kept apart. Two rows are ruled out in those words. Two more, including the one that breaks the confirmation, are called a worse fit on two independent diagnostics without being ruled out. The only variant Wolfire et al. do not argue against is the standard one, and the standard one contains the measured median.
786: One number is stable across all five variants: the measured median exceeds every predicted average pressure, from $1.033$ times the "High $\varphi_{\rm PAH}$" value to $1.713$ times the "Low $\varphi_{\rm PAH}$" one. The measurement runs high against the central model value whatever the parameters. Only the window verdict moves.
788: 10.3 Refuted: a static two-phase medium cannot put cold gas below $P_{\min}$
790: Below $P_{\min}$ the equilibrium curve has no cold branch: by Proposition 8 the only equilibrium state at such a pressure is the warm one. A strict, static two-phase medium therefore allows none of the cold neutral medium to sit below $P_{\min}$. Jenkins & Tripp measured the cold medium, and they report in their section 10.1.2 that $29$ per cent of it is there.

The measurement gives $29$ per cent, an excess over the fit of $5.8$ times. The prediction fails twice over: the fit violates it, and the data violate the fit.
805: Fig. 4 puts both halves of the anchor on one page: the windows and the measured distribution in its upper panel, and in its lower panel the quantity that carries the disagreement, the fraction of cold gas below $P_{\min}$.
809: What is not refuted. Gas above $P_{\max}$ is allowed. Only the warm phase is forbidden there, and this survey measures the cold phase, so the $28$ per cent above $P_{\max}$ violates nothing and is not counted. The refutation rests on the low side alone, where a static cold medium is genuinely impossible.
811: The refutation survives the parameter sweep that the confirmation does not. Only the fitted fraction can be re-evaluated at another $P_{\min}$; the measured $29$ per cent is tied to $P_{\min} = 1960\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$, the value Jenkins & Tripp themselves quote, and cannot be moved without their data. Across the five variants of Table 6, the fitted lognormal below $P_{\min}$ gives $5.0$ per cent (Standard, $P_{\min}=1960$), $1.4$ (Low $\varphi_{\rm PAH}$, $1560$), $10.0$ (High $\varphi_{\rm PAH}$, $2270$), $1.5$ (Low $n_{\rm PAH}/n$, $1580$) and $0.9$ (Low $G_0$, $1460$) — a range of $0.9$ to $10.0$ per cent, against the $0$ per cent a static medium allows, in every variant.
815: 10.4 What fails, precisely: the word "static", not the criterion
817: Jenkins & Tripp offer two readings of their own excess and prefer the second: either the Wolfire curve does not apply to the gas they observe, or, in their words, "rarefactions caused by turbulence create momentary excursions below the curve". Their dispersion of $0.175$ dex is itself read as turbulence at Mach $1$ to $4$. On that reading the thermal-equilibrium curve is an attractor, and the gas is pushed off it faster than it relaxes back. Problem D3 makes that quantitative by putting the cooling time against the crossing time of a turbulent eddy, and finds the two phases differ by a factor $794$ in cooling length — which is why a cold excursion below $P_{\min}$ can only be momentary while a warm departure can be sustained.
819: The same failure seen from the warm side. Heiles & Troland (2003), from 21-cm absorption, find that at least $48$ per cent of the warm neutral medium by column density lies at $500$–$5000$ K, inside the thermally unstable band; a strict two-phase medium allows only the small fraction in transit between phases. Their other numbers: the warm medium is $61$ per cent of the H I column at $|b|\gt 10^\circ$, and the cold-medium spin-temperature histogram peaks at $40$ K with a column-weighted median of $70$ K. Both the $0.61$ and the $0.48$ are column-density weighted and both are lower bounds.
821: The mean state is right; the spread around it is not there. Wolfire et al. compute a cold-medium temperature of $85.0$ K at the average pressure, a ratio of $1.21$ to Heiles & Troland's measured column-weighted median of $70$ K. The two-phase calculation gets the typical temperature and the typical pressure of the neutral interstellar medium right, and gets the width of both distributions wrong, in the same direction, from both ends.

909: 11. What this model captures, and what it misses
911: Each idealisation, and where it is repaid if it is repaid at all.
914: No radiative transfer. The split of the flux between radiation and convection is not predicted anywhere in this module. §7 measures it at $1.3$ per cent at $\tau = 2/3$ and stops there. This is the single largest gap, because it is what makes the most direct test of mixing-length theory a test of something the theory does not claim. It is not repaid in Modules 1–10.
915: Mixing-length theory is a one-parameter closure calibrated on the answer. $\alpha = 2$ was fitted to the solar radius. Problem C3 shows the freedom that leaves is small for the velocity, $\alpha^{1/3}$, and large for the superadiabatic excess, $\alpha^{-4/3}$. Not repaid.
916: No rotation and no magnetic field. Both change convection in the Sun, and the magnetic case changes it qualitatively at the surface. Neither is treated in Modules 1–10.
917: Linear stability only. Both criteria, Schwarzschild's and Field's, say that a perturbation grows. Neither says what it grows into. Module 5's Bonnor–Ebert analysis has exactly the same limit, and §10 shows the cost: the measured distribution of interstellar pressures is a statement about the non-linear, turbulent state, and the linear criterion can only say that the state it predicts is not the one observed.
918: The two-phase medium is treated as static. The anchor identifies this as the assumption that fails. Turbulence is named as the mechanism, on Jenkins & Tripp's own reading of their data, and is not modelled here.
919: The solar opacity is read off a model, not computed. §3.2 reads the rise of $\nabla$ from the table and deliberately does not apportion it between the opacity and the temperature, because this module computes neither.
920: The parcel picture itself. Propositions 1 and 5 follow a single blob against a background that does not respond. Real convection is a connected flow with narrow fast downdrafts and broad slow upflows, which is why Oba et al. measure different upward and downward speeds at the same height. Nothing in this module distinguishes them.

1066: The warm-side measurement. C. Heiles and T. H. Troland, The Millennium Arecibo 21 Centimeter Absorption-Line Survey. II. , The Astrophysical Journal 586 , 1067–1093 (2003), arXiv:astro-ph/0207105 . Quoted from the abstract and sections 2.3.2 and 9.2.1: at least $48$ per cent of the warm neutral medium by column density lies in the thermally unstable range $500$–$5000$ K; $61$ per cent of H I is warm at $|b|\gt 10^\circ$; the cold-medium spin-temperature histogram peaks at $40$ K with a column-weighted median of $70$ K. Both fractions are column-density weighted lower bounds. Their $500$ K is a classification boundary, not a computed turning point, and §9 declines to compare it with the $184$ K of the fit.
