116: 1. The problem: a star that should fall in half an hour
118: Module 2 derived the Euler equation, its equation (7.3), and ended with a table which showed that viscosity may be dropped in the interiors of stars. This module applies that equation in the simplest case it has: a gas that does not move.
120: The simplest case is also the most consequential one, and one number shows why. Take the Sun's mass $M_\odot$ and radius $R_\odot$, remove its pressure, and let it fall. A pressureless uniform sphere of mean density $\bar\rho$ collapses to its centre in the free-fall time
124: derived in Problem D3. Here $G$ is the gravitational constant and $\bar\rho = 3M/(4\pi R^3)$. For the Sun $\bar\rho = 1.4081\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, so $t_{\rm ff} = 1770.3\ \mathrm{s} = 29.5$ minutes.
126: The Sun does not collapse in 29.5 minutes. Its age of $4.6$ Gyr is $8.2\times10^{13}$ free-fall times. Something holds it up, and that something is the pressure gradient. The question this module answers is how precisely the pressure gradient must balance gravity, and what structure that balance forces on a star.
128: A second timescale sharpens the question. The Kelvin–Helmholtz time
132: is the time in which a star of luminosity $L$ would radiate its gravitational binding energy, to order of magnitude. For the Sun it is $9.9089\times10^{14}$ s, or $31.4$ Myr. If the Sun had no nuclear source it would contract on this timescale. It is $5.5973\times10^{11}$ times longer than the free-fall time. A star can change, but it changes on a timescale so much longer than the dynamical one that at each instant it is in balance.
134: The module follows the three-part shape of this book. §3 and §4 derive the terrestrial result: the scale height of the Earth's atmosphere and its lapse rate as a polytrope. §5 to §8 change the regime to a self-gravitating sphere and derive the Lane–Emden equation. §9 and §10 check three predictions against a tabulated standard solar model and against a helioseismic measurement. One prediction is confirmed, one is refuted, and the third finds a 29.4σ disagreement between the model and the measured Sun.
136: 2. The equation, and how well it must hold
138: The Euler equation (7.3) of Module 2 is
142: in which $\rho$ is the mass density in $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $\mathbf{u}$ is the bulk velocity, $P$ is the scalar pressure in $\mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$ (Module 2 wrote it $p$), and $\mathbf{g}$ is the gravitational acceleration. This module writes the pressure as a capital $P$ because lower-case $p$ is not needed and $P$ is the stellar-structure convention.
144: Definition 1 (hydrostatic equilibrium). A fluid is in hydrostatic equilibrium when $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}$ everywhere and at all times considered. Then (2.1) reduces to
$$ \nabla P \;=\; \rho\,\mathbf{g}. \tag{2.2}$$
147: Equation (2.2) says that the net force on each fluid element is zero: the pressure pushes up with the same force per unit volume with which gravity pulls down. For a spherically symmetric body, the gravitational acceleration at radius $r$ depends only on the mass inside that radius.
149: Proposition 1 (spherical hydrostatic equilibrium). In a spherically symmetric, static, self-gravitating fluid with density $\rho(r)$, let
$$ m(r) \;\equiv\; \int_0^r 4\pi r'^2\rho(r')\,dr' \tag{2.3}$$
be the mass inside radius $r$. Then
$$ \frac{dP}{dr} \;=\; -\,\frac{G\,m(r)\,\rho(r)}{r^2}, \qquad \frac{dm}{dr} \;=\; 4\pi r^2\rho. \tag{2.4}$$
154: The second equation of (2.4) is the derivative of the definition (2.3). For the first, apply Gauss's law for gravity, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{g} = -4\pi G\rho$, to a sphere of radius $r$. Spherical symmetry makes $\mathbf{g} = g_r(r)\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}$, so the divergence theorem gives $4\pi r^2 g_r = -4\pi G\,m(r)$, that is $g_r = -Gm(r)/r^2$. The mass outside $r$ contributes nothing, by the same theorem. Spherical symmetry also makes $\nabla P = (dP/dr)\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}$. Substituting both into (2.2) and taking the radial component gives the first equation of (2.4). ∎
158: Equation (2.4) is two equations in three unknown functions, $P$, $\rho$ and $m$. It is not closed. Some relation between $P$ and $\rho$ must be supplied, and the choice of that relation is the whole subject of §5 . First we ask whether setting $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}$ is justified at all.
160: 2.1 How precisely the balance holds
162: A star that contracts is not static. The question is how large an imbalance between the two terms on the right of (2.1) a contraction requires. The answer is a ratio of timescales, and it is small enough to justify (2.2) by a very wide margin.
164: Proposition 2 (the imbalance required by a slow contraction). Let a star of mass $M$ and radius $R$ contract so that its surface accelerates at $|\ddot R| = R/t^2$, which defines the contraction timescale $t$. Then the net force per unit mass at the surface, as a fraction of the surface gravity $GM/R^2$, is
$$ \frac{|\ddot R|}{GM/R^2} \;=\; \frac{8}{\pi^2}\left(\frac{t_{\rm ff}}{t}\right)^{2}, \tag{2.5}$$
with $t_{\rm ff}$ given by (1.1).
168: The radial component of (2.1) for the surface element, which moves with the surface, is $\ddot R = -(1/\rho)\,dP/dr - GM/R^2$. The sum of the two force terms on the right is therefore $\ddot R$, and its size relative to the gravity term is $|\ddot R|/(GM/R^2) = (R/t^2)(R^2/GM) = R^3/(GMt^2)$. Now express $R^3/(GM)$ through the free-fall time. With $\bar\rho = 3M/(4\pi R^3)$, equation (1.1) gives
$$ t_{\rm ff}^2 \;=\; \frac{3\pi}{32\,G}\cdot\frac{4\pi R^3}{3M} \;=\; \frac{\pi^2}{8}\,\frac{R^3}{GM}, $$
so $R^3/(GM) = (8/\pi^2)\,t_{\rm ff}^2$. Substituting gives (2.5). ∎
174: Apply (2.5) to the fastest contraction the Sun could sustain from its own luminosity, $t = t_{\rm KH}$. The coefficient-free factor is $(t_{\rm ff}/t_{\rm KH})^2 = 3.192\times10^{-24}$; the exact coefficient $8/\pi^2 = 0.8106$ multiplies it.
176: Result. If the Sun contracted on its Kelvin–Helmholtz time of $31.4$ Myr, gravity and the pressure gradient would still have to balance to $2.587\times10^{-24}$ of the surface gravity, by (2.5). On the nuclear timescale of $1.036\times10^{10}$ yr, on which the Sun actually evolves, the fraction is $2.379\times10^{-29}$.
178: Note the direction of this argument. The small imbalance is not assumed in order to simplify the equations. It is forced by the observed luminosity. By (2.5), an imbalance larger than about $10^{-24}$ means contraction on a timescale $t$ shorter than $t_{\rm KH}$. Such a contraction releases gravitational energy of order $GM^2/R$ in time $t$, a power $GM^2/(Rt)$ larger than $L$ by the factor $t_{\rm KH}/t$ from (1.2). By the virial argument of §8 , half of that energy must leave the star, and the observed luminosity does not carry it. Setting $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}$ in (2.1) discards a term of that relative size.
182: A sound wave carries the information that restores balance. The isothermal sound speed at the Sun's centre, $\sqrt{P_c/\rho_c}$, is $394.1\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, and the sound crossing time $R/c_s$ is $1765.1$ s, or $29.4$ minutes. It differs from $t_{\rm ff}$ by $0.3$ per cent. Both are order-of-magnitude estimates, so the closeness to $0.3$ per cent is partly accidental. That the two are of the same order is not: the virial theorem, Proposition 12 of §8 , gives $3\int P\,dV = -\Omega$, and with $\Omega\sim -GM^2/R$ and $\int P\,dV\sim (P/\rho)M$ this makes $P/\rho\sim GM/R$, so $R/c_s\sim\sqrt{R^3/GM}\sim t_{\rm ff}$. Any departure from balance is corrected in about half an hour. Module 4 studies those sound waves directly.
184: 3. The plane-parallel atmosphere and the scale height
186: Before the whole star, take a thin layer at its surface, or the atmosphere of a planet. If the layer is thin compared with the radius, the curvature of the surface and the change of $g$ with height can both be ignored.
188: Definition 2 (plane-parallel layer). A plane-parallel layer is a region in which all quantities depend only on a height coordinate $z$, measured upward, and the gravitational acceleration is uniform, $\mathbf{g} = -g\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ with $g\gt 0$ constant. Equation (2.2) then becomes
$$ \frac{dP}{dz} \;=\; -\rho\, g. \tag{3.1}$$
191: Equation (3.1) needs a second relation between $P$ and $\rho$. The ideal gas law supplies one when the temperature is known:
195: in which $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ the temperature, $m_u$ the atomic mass unit, and $\mu$ the mean molecular weight, the mean mass per free particle in units of $m_u$. For dry air $\mu = 28.9647$; for a fully ionised gas of hydrogen mass fraction $X$, helium mass fraction $Y$ and heavy-element fraction $Z$, $\mu = 1/(2X + \tfrac{3}{4}Y + \tfrac{1}{2}Z)$.
197: Proposition 3 (isothermal scale height). In a plane-parallel layer of ideal gas at uniform temperature $T$ and uniform $\mu$,
$$ P(z) \;=\; P(0)\,e^{-z/H}, \qquad H \;\equiv\; \frac{k_B T}{\mu\, m_u\, g}. \tag{3.3}$$
The density falls with the same exponential, $\rho(z) = \rho(0)\,e^{-z/H}$. The length $H$ is the pressure scale height .
201: Solve (3.2) for the density, $\rho = \mu m_u P/(k_BT)$, and substitute into (3.1):
$$ \frac{dP}{dz} \;=\; -\frac{\mu\, m_u\, g}{k_B T}\,P \;=\; -\frac{P}{H}. $$
Because $T$, $\mu$ and $g$ are constant, $H$ is constant, and this linear equation has the solution $P(0)e^{-z/H}$. The density is $\mu m_u P/(k_BT)$ with a constant prefactor, so it has the same dependence on $z$. ∎
207: The scale height is the height over which the pressure falls by a factor $e$. It is the height through which an energy $k_BT$, the thermal energy per particle to within a factor of order one, would lift one particle against its weight $\mu m_u g$. A hot gas or a light gas or a weak gravity gives a thick atmosphere.
209: Proposition 3 was proved under the assumption that $g$ is uniform. For a layer on a body of radius $r$ and mass $M$, $g = GM/r^2$, and it changes by a fractional amount of about $2H/r$ across one scale height. The layer's area also changes by the same fraction. So the plane-parallel form is valid when

227: 4. The polytropic atmosphere: the terrestrial half
229: The Earth's lower atmosphere is not isothermal. Its temperature falls with height at a nearly constant rate, the lapse rate . This section shows that a constant lapse rate is exactly what a power-law relation between pressure and density produces, and so it introduces the closure that the rest of the module applies to stars.
231: Definition 3 (polytrope). A fluid is polytropic with polytropic index $n$ and polytropic constant $K$ if, throughout the region considered,
$$ P \;=\; K\,\rho^{1+1/n}, \tag{4.1}$$
with $K$ and $n$ constant, $0\le n\le\infty$. The case $n\to\infty$ is the isothermal gas; $n=0$ is a gas of uniform density.
235: Definition 3 is a statement about the structure of a body, a relation between $P$ and $\rho$ at different places. It need not be a statement about how one parcel responds when it is compressed, although in §5 it will be one.
237: Proposition 4 (lapse rate of a polytropic atmosphere). In a plane-parallel layer of ideal gas (3.2) with uniform $\mu$ and $g$, obeying the polytropic relation (4.1), the temperature falls linearly with height at the rate
$$ \left|\frac{dT}{dz}\right| \;=\; \frac{\mu\, m_u\, g}{k_B\,(n+1)}, \tag{4.2}$$
and the pressure and temperature are related by
$$ \frac{P}{P(0)} \;=\; \left(\frac{T}{T(0)}\right)^{n+1}. \tag{4.3}$$
242: From (4.1), $\rho = (P/K)^{n/(n+1)}$. Substituting into the ideal gas law (3.2) and solving for $T$,
$$ T \;=\; \frac{\mu m_u}{k_B}\,\frac{P}{\rho} \;=\; \frac{\mu m_u}{k_B}\,K^{n/(n+1)}\,P^{1/(n+1)}. $$
The prefactor is constant, so $T\propto P^{1/(n+1)}$, which is (4.3). Differentiate the logarithm of this relation with respect to $z$:
$$ \frac{1}{T}\frac{dT}{dz} \;=\; \frac{1}{n+1}\,\frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dz} \;=\; -\frac{1}{n+1}\,\frac{\rho\, g}{P}, $$
using (3.1) in the second step. By (3.2), $\rho/P = \mu m_u/(k_BT)$, so the right-hand side is $-\mu m_u g/(k_B T(n+1))$. Multiplying by $T$ gives $dT/dz = -\mu m_u g/(k_B(n+1))$, a constant, which is (4.2). ∎
250: Inverting (4.2) turns a measured lapse rate $\Gamma \equiv |dT/dz|$ into a polytropic index, $n = \mu m_u g/(k_B\Gamma) - 1$. Two lapse rates of the Earth's atmosphere are worth converting.
252: The first is the lapse rate of the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA), $6.5\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$ up to the tropopause at $11$ km. It gives $n = 4.2558$.
254: The second is the dry adiabatic lapse rate, the rate at which a parcel of dry air cools when it rises without exchanging heat. A parcel of ideal gas compressed or expanded adiabatically obeys $P\propto\rho^{\gamma}$, with $\gamma$ the ratio of specific heats. If the atmosphere is well mixed, so that every level lies on the same adiabat, the structure obeys $P=K\rho^{\gamma}$ with one $K$, and comparison with (4.1) gives $1+1/n = \gamma$, or
258: For the diatomic molecules of dry air, $\gamma = 7/5$ and $n = 2.5$. Substituting into (4.2) gives $\Gamma = g(\gamma-1)\mu m_u/(\gamma k_B) = g/c_p$, where $c_p = \gamma k_B/((\gamma-1)\mu m_u) = 1004.7\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ is the specific heat at constant pressure. Numerically $g/c_p = 9.761\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$, and inverting (4.2) returns $n = 2.5000$.
337: Three hydrostatic atmospheres with the same sea-level state, $T(0)=288.15$ K and $P(0)=1013.25$ hPa. Left: temperature against altitude. Right: pressure, from (3.3) for the isothermal case and (4.3) for the two polytropes. The standard atmosphere, $n=4.26$, lies between the isothermal limit and the dry adiabat, $n=2.50$; at $11$ km the dry adiabat is $36$ K colder. The circle marks the tabulated tropopause pressure of $226$ hPa, which the $n=4.26$ curve reaches by construction.
340: Fig. 1 compares the three. The real troposphere cools more slowly than the dry adiabat, by $35.9$ K over $11$ km. The difference is the latent heat released when water vapour condenses in rising air, which warms the parcel as it rises. The index $4.26$ is therefore not an adiabatic index of any gas. It is an effective index that summarises the combined effect of adiabatic cooling and condensation heating on the structure.
342: Equation (4.3) also gives the pressure at the tropopause. With $n = 4.2558$, $T(0) = 288.15$ K and a tropopause temperature of $288.15 - 6.5\times11 = 216.65$ K, it gives $226.32$ hPa, in ratio $1.00001$ to the tabulated $226.32$ hPa.
344: This agreement is a check of the algebra, not a test of nature. The ISA is defined by its sea-level temperature and pressure, its lapse rate and the hydrostatic equation, and its tabulated tropopause values are computed from those constants. Agreement to $1.00001$ confirms that (4.2) and (4.3) are derived and evaluated correctly. It says nothing about whether the real atmosphere is polytropic. The tests against nature in this module are in §10 .
348: 5. Why a polytrope: two indices fixed in advance
350: The spherical equations (2.4) need a closure, and Definition 3 is the simplest one. A closure is only useful if its index can be fixed before the star is examined. If $n$ were chosen to fit a model, agreement with that model would prove nothing. This section derives two indices from physics alone.
352: Proposition 5 (adiabatic convection gives $n = 3/2$). Suppose a region of an ideal monatomic gas has uniform specific entropy, so that every part of it lies on one adiabat $P = K\rho^{\gamma}$ with $\gamma = 5/3$. Then the region is a polytrope of index $n = 3/2$.
354: For an ideal gas whose particles have only the three translational degrees of freedom, the internal energy per particle is $\tfrac{3}{2}k_BT$ (Module 2, Proposition 1), so the specific heats per particle are $c_V = \tfrac{3}{2}k_B$ and $c_P = c_V + k_B = \tfrac{5}{2}k_B$, and $\gamma = c_P/c_V = 5/3$. Along an adiabat, $P\rho^{-\gamma}$ is constant; uniform specific entropy means the same constant $K$ applies everywhere in the region. So $P = K\rho^{5/3}$ throughout, and matching the exponent to (4.1), $1 + 1/n = 5/3$, gives $n = 3/2$. ∎
358: The physical input is the hypothesis of uniform entropy. Convection supplies it: rising and sinking parcels mix the gas, and where convection is efficient it carries the stratification to the adiabatic state. That is asserted here and derived in Module 6 through the Schwarzschild criterion. The index follows from the hypothesis with no free parameter. The monatomic assumption requires the gas to be fully ionised or neutral atomic; in a zone where hydrogen or helium is partly ionised, ionisation absorbs energy and $\gamma$ falls below $5/3$.
360: The second index comes from radiation pressure. A hot star contains a photon gas as well as a particle gas, and both push.
362: Definition 4 (gas-pressure fraction). In a gas of ideal particles and blackbody radiation at temperature $T$, the total pressure is $P = P_{\rm gas} + P_{\rm rad}$ with
$$ P_{\rm gas} \;=\; \frac{k_B}{\mu m_u}\,\rho T, \qquad P_{\rm rad} \;=\; \frac{a_{\rm rad} T^4}{3}, \tag{5.1}$$
where $a_{\rm rad} = 7.5657\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{erg\,cm^{-3}\,K^{-4}}$ is the radiation constant. The gas-pressure fraction is $\beta \equiv P_{\rm gas}/P$, so $1-\beta = P_{\rm rad}/P$.
366: Proposition 6 (Eddington's standard model gives $n = 3$). If $\beta$ and $\mu$ are the same everywhere in a star, with $0\lt\beta\lt 1$, then
$$ P \;=\; K\rho^{4/3}, \qquad K \;=\; \left[\frac{3}{a_{\rm rad}}\,\frac{1-\beta}{\beta^4}\right]^{1/3}\left(\frac{k_B}{\mu m_u}\right)^{4/3}, \tag{5.2}$$
so the star is a polytrope of index $n = 3$.
370: Eliminate $T$ between the two parts of (5.1). From $P_{\rm rad} = (1-\beta)P$, the temperature is $T = [3(1-\beta)P/a_{\rm rad}]^{1/4}$. From $P_{\rm gas} = \beta P$,
$$ \beta P \;=\; \frac{k_B}{\mu m_u}\,\rho\,\left[\frac{3(1-\beta)P}{a_{\rm rad}}\right]^{1/4}. $$
Divide both sides by $\beta P^{1/4}$:
$$ P^{3/4} \;=\; \frac{k_B}{\mu m_u}\,\frac{1}{\beta}\left[\frac{3(1-\beta)}{a_{\rm rad}}\right]^{1/4}\rho. $$
Raise both sides to the power $4/3$:
$$ P \;=\; \left(\frac{k_B}{\mu m_u}\right)^{4/3}\beta^{-4/3}\left[\frac{3(1-\beta)}{a_{\rm rad}}\right]^{1/3}\rho^{4/3}, $$
which is (5.2) after collecting $\beta^{-4/3} = (\beta^{-4})^{1/3}$ inside the bracket. If $\beta$ and $\mu$ are constant, $K$ is constant and $P\propto\rho^{4/3}$; by (4.1), $1+1/n = 4/3$, so $n=3$. ∎
380: Note what the proof needs. It needs $\beta$ to be constant, and it needs $1-\beta$ to be non-zero, since otherwise $K$ vanishes and the relation says nothing. It does not need $1-\beta$ to be large. The hypothesis of constant $\beta$ has a direct test. Since $1-\beta = a_{\rm rad}T^4/(3P)$, constant $\beta$ means $T^4\propto P$, that is $d\ln T/d\ln P = 1/4$ at every depth. §10 measures that slope through the Sun, and measures the size of $1-\beta$ at its centre.
382: Both indices are now fixed. The number $3/2$ follows from uniform entropy in a monatomic gas, and the number $3$ follows from a uniform radiation share. Neither was read from a solar model, and so each can be tested against one.
384: 6. The Lane–Emden equation
386: Substituting the polytropic closure (4.1) into the spherical equations (2.4) produces one second-order equation for the density. Scaling out the dimensional constants leaves an equation that depends on $n$ alone.
388: Proposition 7 (the Lane–Emden equation). Let a spherical star in hydrostatic equilibrium (2.4) obey the polytropic relation (4.1) with index $n\gt 0$, and let $\rho_c$ be its central density. Define the dimensionless density variable $\theta$ and radius $\xi$ by
$$ \rho \;=\; \rho_c\,\theta^{n}, \qquad r \;=\; a\,\xi, \qquad a^2 \;\equiv\; \frac{(n+1)\,K\,\rho_c^{1/n-1}}{4\pi G}. \tag{6.1}$$
Then $\theta(\xi)$ satisfies
$$ \frac{1}{\xi^2}\,\frac{d}{d\xi}\left(\xi^2\,\frac{d\theta}{d\xi}\right) \;=\; -\,\theta^{n}, \tag{6.2}$$
with the boundary conditions
$$ \theta(0) \;=\; 1, \qquad \theta'(0) \;=\; 0. \tag{6.3}$$
The surface of the star is the first zero of $\theta$, at $\xi = \xi_1$, where $R = a\,\xi_1$.
396: First combine the two equations of (2.4) into one. Multiply the first by $r^2/\rho$, so that $r^2\rho^{-1}\,dP/dr = -Gm$, and differentiate with respect to $r$. The second equation replaces $dm/dr$:
$$ \frac{1}{r^2}\,\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{r^2}{\rho}\,\frac{dP}{dr}\right) \;=\; -4\pi G\rho. $$
Next write the pressure in terms of $\theta$. With $\rho = \rho_c\theta^n$, the closure (4.1) gives $P = K\rho_c^{1+1/n}\theta^{n+1}$, so
$$ \frac{dP}{dr} \;=\; (n+1)\,K\rho_c^{1+1/n}\,\theta^{n}\,\frac{d\theta}{dr}, \qquad \frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dP}{dr} \;=\; (n+1)\,K\rho_c^{1/n}\,\frac{d\theta}{dr}. $$
Substituting and dividing by $4\pi G\rho_c$,
$$ \frac{(n+1)\,K\rho_c^{1/n-1}}{4\pi G}\;\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{d\theta}{dr}\right) \;=\; -\theta^n. $$
The prefactor is $a^2$ by (6.1), and it has dimensions of length squared. Change variable to $\xi = r/a$. Then $d/dr = a^{-1}\,d/d\xi$ and $r^2 = a^2\xi^2$, so $r^{-2}\,d(r^2\,d\theta/dr)/dr = a^{-2}\,\xi^{-2}\,d(\xi^2\,d\theta/d\xi)/d\xi$. The factor $a^{-2}$ cancels the prefactor $a^2$, which gives (6.2).

For the boundary conditions: $\theta(0)=1$ because $\rho(0)=\rho_c$ by definition. For the second, note that $m(r)\approx\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3\rho_c$ near the centre, so $dP/dr = -Gm\rho/r^2\approx -\tfrac{4}{3}\pi G\rho_c^2\, r \to 0$ as $r\to 0$. Since $dP/dr$ is proportional to $\theta^n\,d\theta/dr$ and $\theta(0)=1$, this requires $\theta'(0)=0$. The pressure, and therefore the density, first vanish where $\theta$ first vanishes, which is the surface. ∎
408: The scaling has removed $K$, $\rho_c$ and $G$. Every polytrope of index $n$, whatever its mass and radius, is the same function $\theta_n(\xi)$, stretched in radius by $a$ and in density by $\rho_c$. So one integration for each $n$ describes all stars of that index.
410: The point $\xi=0$ is a singular point of (6.2), because the term $2\theta'/\xi$ has a zero denominator. A numerical integration cannot start there, and it starts instead from a short series.
412: Proposition 8 (series near the centre). The solution of (6.2) with (6.3) has the expansion
$$ \theta(\xi) \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{\xi^2}{6} \;+\; \frac{n\,\xi^4}{120} \;-\; \cdots. \tag{6.4}$$
415: Expanding (6.2) gives $\theta'' + 2\theta'/\xi = -\theta^n$. The equation is unchanged under $\xi\to-\xi$, and the conditions (6.3) are even, so the regular solution is an even function. Write $\theta = 1 + b_2\xi^2 + b_4\xi^4 + O(\xi^6)$. Then $\theta' = 2b_2\xi + 4b_4\xi^3$ and $\theta'' = 2b_2 + 12b_4\xi^2$, so
$$ \theta'' + \frac{2\theta'}{\xi} \;=\; 6b_2 + 20b_4\,\xi^2 + O(\xi^4). $$
On the right, by the binomial expansion, $\theta^n = 1 + n\,b_2\,\xi^2 + O(\xi^4)$. Matching the constant terms gives $6b_2 = -1$, so $b_2 = -1/6$. Matching the $\xi^2$ terms gives $20b_4 = -n b_2 = n/6$, so $b_4 = n/120$. ∎
421: The case $n=0$ needs one remark before it can be used. With $n=0$, the substitution $\rho=\rho_c\theta^n$ makes $\rho$ uniform and says nothing about $\theta$, and $\rho_c^{1/n}$ in (6.1) is undefined. Since $P_c = K\rho_c^{1+1/n}$, the length in (6.1) can be written $a^2 = (n+1)P_c/(4\pi G\rho_c^2)$, and the closure gives $P = P_c\,\theta^{n+1}$. Both forms remain meaningful at $n=0$, and we take them as the definitions of $a$ and $\theta$ there. For uniform density, (2.4) gives $dP/dr = -\tfrac{4}{3}\pi G\rho_c^2 r$, so $P = P_c - \tfrac{2}{3}\pi G\rho_c^2 r^2$; with $\theta = P/P_c$ and $r = a\xi$, $a^2 = P_c/(4\pi G\rho_c^2)$, this is $\theta = 1-\xi^2/6$, which is the solution of (6.2) with $\theta^0 = 1$. So (6.2) and (6.3) hold at $n=0$ with this reading, and (7.2) below applies to it.
421: Three values of $n$ have solutions in closed form. They are the test cases for the numerical integrator, and each shows a different kind of star.
423: Proposition 9 (exact solutions for $n=0$, $1$, $5$). The solutions of (6.2) and (6.3) for these three indices are
$$ \theta_0 \;=\; 1 - \frac{\xi^2}{6}, \qquad \theta_1 \;=\; \frac{\sin\xi}{\xi}, \qquad \theta_5 \;=\; \left(1+\frac{\xi^2}{3}\right)^{-1/2}. \tag{6.5}$$
Their surfaces are at $\xi_1 = \sqrt6$ with $\theta_0'(\xi_1) = -\sqrt6/3$, and at $\xi_1 = \pi$ with $\theta_1'(\xi_1) = -1/\pi$. The $n=5$ solution has no zero, so its radius is infinite, but $-\xi^2\theta_5'\to\sqrt3$ as $\xi\to\infty$, so its mass is finite.
427: Case $n=0$. Then $\theta^0=1$ and (6.2) is $(\xi^2\theta')' = -\xi^2$. Integrate once: $\xi^2\theta' = -\xi^3/3 + C$. At $\xi=0$ the left side is zero, so $C=0$ and $\theta' = -\xi/3$. Integrate again with $\theta(0)=1$ to get $\theta_0 = 1-\xi^2/6$. It vanishes at $\xi_1=\sqrt6$, where $\theta' = -\sqrt6/3$. The density $\rho_c\theta^0$ is uniform, but the pressure $\propto\theta^{n+1} = \theta$ is not. Case $n=1$. Substitute $\theta = \chi/\xi$. Then $\xi^2\theta' = \xi\chi' - \chi$, and its derivative is $\xi\chi''$, so (6.2) becomes $\chi''/\xi = -\chi/\xi$, that is $\chi'' = -\chi$. The general solution is $\chi = A\sin\xi + B\cos\xi$. The term $B\cos\xi/\xi$ diverges at the origin, so $B=0$; and $\sin\xi/\xi\to1$ as $\xi\to0$, so $A=1$. The first zero of $\sin\xi$ after the origin is $\xi_1=\pi$. There $\theta_1' = (\xi\cos\xi - \sin\xi)/\xi^2 = -\pi/\pi^2 = -1/\pi$. Case $n=5$. Let $s = 1+\xi^2/3$. Differentiating $\theta_5 = s^{-1/2}$ gives $\theta_5' = -(\xi/3)\,s^{-3/2}$, so $\xi^2\theta_5' = -(\xi^3/3)\,s^{-3/2}$. Differentiating again,
$$ (\xi^2\theta_5')' \;=\; -\xi^2 s^{-3/2} + \frac{\xi^3}{3}\cdot\frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{2\xi}{3}\,s^{-5/2} \;=\; -\xi^2 s^{-5/2}\left(s - \frac{\xi^2}{3}\right) \;=\; -\xi^2 s^{-5/2}. $$
Dividing by $\xi^2$ gives $-s^{-5/2} = -\theta_5^5$, which is (6.2). The conditions (6.3) hold by inspection. $\theta_5\gt0$ for every $\xi$, so there is no surface. For large $\xi$, $s^{-3/2}\to(\xi^2/3)^{-3/2} = 3^{3/2}\xi^{-3}$, so $-\xi^2\theta_5' \to (\xi^3/3)\cdot3^{3/2}\xi^{-3} = \sqrt3$. ∎

544: 7. Properties of polytropes
546: The dimensionless solution fixes every structural ratio of a polytrope. This section extracts the ones that can be compared with a star of known mass and radius.
548: Proposition 10 (mass, radius, central condensation and central pressure). A polytrope of index $n\lt 5$ with first zero $\xi_1$ and surface slope $\theta_1' \equiv \theta'(\xi_1)$ has
$$ R \;=\; a\,\xi_1, \qquad M \;=\; 4\pi a^3\rho_c\left(-\xi_1^2\theta_1'\right), \tag{7.1}$$
$$ D_n \;\equiv\; \frac{\rho_c}{\bar\rho} \;=\; \frac{\xi_1}{3\,|\theta_1'|}, \qquad W_n \;\equiv\; \frac{P_c\,R^4}{G M^2} \;=\; \frac{1}{4\pi(n+1)\,\theta_1'^{\,2}}, \tag{7.2}$$
and, for $n\neq3$, the mass–radius relation
$$ R \;\propto\; K^{\,n/(3-n)}\,M^{(1-n)/(3-n)}. \tag{7.3}$$
554: The radius follows from $r=a\xi$ at the surface. For the mass, integrate (2.3) with $\rho=\rho_c\theta^n$ and $r=a\xi$:
$$ M \;=\; 4\pi a^3\rho_c\int_0^{\xi_1}\xi^2\theta^n\,d\xi \;=\; 4\pi a^3\rho_c\int_0^{\xi_1}\left(-\frac{d}{d\xi}\big(\xi^2\theta'\big)\right)d\xi \;=\; 4\pi a^3\rho_c\left(-\xi_1^2\theta_1'\right), $$
using (6.2) in the middle step and $\xi^2\theta'=0$ at $\xi=0$.

For $D_n$: $\bar\rho = 3M/(4\pi R^3) = 3\rho_c(-\xi_1^2\theta_1')/\xi_1^3 = 3\rho_c|\theta_1'|/\xi_1$, and dividing $\rho_c$ by this gives the first of (7.2).

For $W_n$: the central pressure is $P_c = K\rho_c^{1+1/n}$. By (6.1), $K\rho_c^{1/n} = 4\pi G a^2\rho_c/(n+1)$, so $P_c = 4\pi G a^2\rho_c^2/(n+1)$. From (7.1), $\rho_c = M/(4\pi a^3\mu_1)$ with $\mu_1\equiv-\xi_1^2\theta_1'$. Substituting,
$$ P_c \;=\; \frac{4\pi G a^2}{n+1}\cdot\frac{M^2}{16\pi^2a^6\mu_1^2} \;=\; \frac{GM^2}{4\pi(n+1)\,a^4\mu_1^2}. $$
With $a = R/\xi_1$, $a^4\mu_1^2 = R^4\xi_1^{-4}\cdot\xi_1^4\theta_1'^2 = R^4\theta_1'^2$, which gives the second of (7.2).

For (7.3): from (6.1), $a^2\propto K\rho_c^{(1-n)/n}$, and from (7.1), $\rho_c\propto M/a^3$. Substituting, $a^2\propto K M^{(1-n)/n}a^{-3(1-n)/n}$, so $a^{(3-n)/n}\propto K M^{(1-n)/n}$. Raising to the power $n/(3-n)$ and using $R\propto a$ gives (7.3). ∎

585: 8. What balance gives with no closure
587: Everything in §6 and §7 depends on the closure (4.1). Before testing a closure, it is worth asking what hydrostatic equilibrium implies with no closure at all. The answer is two exact results. One is an inequality, and one is an identity.
589: Proposition 11 (Chandrasekhar's lower bound on the central pressure). In a spherical star of mass $M$ and radius $R$ in hydrostatic equilibrium (2.4), with finite central density and non-negative surface pressure,
$$ P_c \;\ge\; \frac{G M^2}{8\pi R^4}. \tag{8.1}$$
592: Define $F(r) \equiv P(r) + Gm(r)^2/(8\pi r^4)$. Differentiate, using both equations of (2.4):
$$ \frac{dF}{dr} \;=\; -\frac{Gm\rho}{r^2} \;+\; \frac{2Gm}{8\pi r^4}\cdot4\pi r^2\rho \;-\; \frac{4Gm^2}{8\pi r^5} \;=\; -\frac{Gm^2}{2\pi r^5} \;\le\; 0. $$
The first two terms cancel exactly. So $F$ does not increase outward, and $F(0)\ge F(R)$. At the centre, $m\approx\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3\rho_c$ makes $m^2/r^4\propto r^2\to0$, so $F(0)=P_c$. At the surface, $F(R) = P(R) + GM^2/(8\pi R^4) \ge GM^2/(8\pi R^4)$. Combining gives (8.1). ∎
598: For the Sun, with the tabulated radius of §9 , the bound is $4.4750\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$. The tabulated central pressure is $2.3380\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$, which exceeds the bound by a factor $522.5$. The bound is true and it excludes almost nothing. The factor of $522$ is the information that a closure must supply. In the notation of (7.2), the bound is $W\ge1/(8\pi) = 0.03979$; the tabulated Sun has $W = 20.7881$, and $n=3$ gives $W_3 = 11.0507$.
600: Proposition 12 (the virial theorem for a static star). Under the hypotheses of Proposition 11, with zero surface pressure $P(R)=0$,
$$ 3\int_0^R P\,dV \;=\; -\,\Omega, \qquad \Omega \;\equiv\; -\int_0^M \frac{G\,m}{r}\,dm, \tag{8.2}$$
where $dV = 4\pi r^2 dr$ and $\Omega$ is the gravitational potential energy of the star.
604: Multiply the first equation of (2.4) by $4\pi r^3$ and integrate from $0$ to $R$. On the left, integrate by parts:
$$ \int_0^R 4\pi r^3\frac{dP}{dr}\,dr \;=\; \Big[4\pi r^3 P\Big]_0^R - \int_0^R 12\pi r^2 P\,dr \;=\; -3\int_0^R P\,dV, $$
since the boundary term vanishes at $r=0$ and, because $P(R)=0$, at $r=R$. On the right,
$$ -\int_0^R 4\pi r^3\,\frac{Gm\rho}{r^2}\,dr \;=\; -\int_0^R \frac{Gm}{r}\,4\pi r^2\rho\,dr \;=\; -\int_0^M\frac{Gm}{r}\,dm \;=\; \Omega, $$
using $dm = 4\pi r^2\rho\,dr$. Equating the two sides gives $-3\int P\,dV = \Omega$, which is (8.2). ∎
612: For an ideal monatomic gas, $\tfrac{3}{2}P$ is the internal energy per unit volume (Module 2, Proposition 1), so (8.2) says that the thermal energy $U = \int\tfrac{3}{2}P\,dV$ is $-\Omega/2$. The total energy is then $E = U + \Omega = \Omega/2$. A star that radiates loses energy, so $E$ and $\Omega$ decrease; a more negative $\Omega$ means a more compact star, and $U = -\Omega/2$ increases. The star contracts and its thermal energy rises, and half the released gravitational energy is radiated. That is why $t_{\rm KH}$ in (1.2), the binding energy divided by the luminosity, is the timescale of contraction.
614: Integrating the tabulated solar model of §9 gives $3\int P\,dV = 6.1594\times10^{48}$ erg and $-\Omega = 6.1564\times10^{48}$ erg, in ratio $1.00049$.
616: This agreement does not confirm hydrostatic equilibrium. The solar model was built by solving (2.4) numerically. Any correct reading of its table must satisfy (8.2), so a ratio of $1.00049$ confirms that the table was read and integrated correctly, and nothing more. The residual $4.9\times10^{-4}$ has not been decomposed; two candidate sources are the trapezoid rule and the table ending at $0.983\,R$ rather than at $P=0$. Module 2's solar-wind check did not have this circularity, because Venzmer & Bothmer fitted the density and speed exponents with no continuity constraint imposed. Here the tests must be of a closure , given the equation, and §10 is built that way.

655: 10.2 Check 2: the convection zone as $n=3/2$
657: To test a polytropic index locally, define it locally. The first step of the proof of Proposition 4 used only the ideal gas law (3.2) and the closure (4.1), and gave $T\propto P^{1/(n+1)}$ when $\mu$ is constant. The logarithmic slope of $T$ against $P$ is therefore constant and fixes $n$.
659: Definition 5 (temperature gradient and effective index). The dimensionless temperature gradient of a stratified star is
$$ \nabla \;\equiv\; \frac{d\ln T}{d\ln P}, \tag{10.1}$$
evaluated along the radius. The effective polytropic index at each radius is
$$ n_{\rm eff} \;\equiv\; \frac{1}{\nabla} - 1. \tag{10.2}$$
For a polytrope of index $n$ with uniform $\mu$, $\nabla = 1/(n+1)$ and $n_{\rm eff}=n$ everywhere. For the adiabat of a monatomic ideal gas, $\nabla = \nabla_{\rm ad} = (\gamma-1)/\gamma = 0.4$ and $n_{\rm eff} = 3/2$. Where $\mu$ varies with radius, $T\propto\mu P/\rho$ adds a term $d\ln\mu/d\ln P$ to $\nabla$, and $n_{\rm eff}$ is then not the exponent in $P\propto\rho^{1+1/n}$.
665: The table quotes $P$, $\rho$ and $T$ to four significant figures, so a two-point difference of $\ln T$ against $\ln P$ is dominated by rounding. The script computes $\nabla$ as a least-squares slope over $\pm25$ rows. In the band $0.80$ to $0.92\,R$ the slope is flat, and its median is the plateau value.

The orange line is the model's published base, $0.7280\,R$; the violet line and band are the helioseismic measurement, $(0.7133\pm0.0005)\,R$. The gap is $0.0147\,R$, $29$ standard deviations of the measurement (Check 3).
775: Check 3: the model is refuted by a measurement. The model's published base, $0.7280\,R$, lies $0.0147\,R$ above the measured $0.7133\pm0.0005\,R$. That is a fractional error of $2.06$ per cent and a departure of $29.4\sigma$. The base recovered by this module's locator, $0.7269\,R$, is $27.2\sigma$ from the measurement. Bahcall, Serenelli & Basu quote the measurement rounded to $0.713\pm0.001$; against that uncertainty the published base departs by $15.0\sigma$.
777: The number of standard deviations would mean little if the locator could move the base by a comparable amount. Across a grid of fitting windows of $\pm10$, $\pm25$ and $\pm50$ rows and thresholds of $0.98$, $0.99$ and $0.995$ of the plateau, the recovered base ranges from $0.7255$ to $0.7308\,R$. The spread, $0.0054\,R$, is $0.37$ of the model–measurement gap. The grid value nearest the measurement, $0.7255\,R$, is still $24.3\sigma$ from it.
779: State exactly what this check refutes. It does not refute hydrostatic equilibrium, and it does not refute the $n=3/2$ closure of the convection zone. Both are the instrument: they define the plateau and locate the boundary, and Check 2 confirms the closure in the region where it is claimed. What fails is an input of this particular model, the physics that sets where $\nabla$ first reaches $\nabla_{\rm ad}$. In a radiative region, that radius is set by the opacity and by the heavy-element abundance on which the opacity depends. BS2005-AGS,OP was built with the lower heavy-element abundances denoted "AGS" in its name, and Table 1 of Bahcall, Serenelli & Basu (2005) lists its base, $0.7280$, beside the measured $0.713\pm0.001$ that the paper quotes in its equation (1). This disagreement between low-abundance solar models and helioseismology is known as the solar abundance problem .
783: 11. What this model captures, and what it misses
785: The equations of this module are (2.4) with the closure (4.1). The table names each idealisation and the module that repays it.
788: Idealisation made here What it costs Where it is repaid
789: Static: $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}$ Excludes oscillations, convective motions and flows. §2.1 showed the error in the mean structure is below $10^{-23}$ of gravity, but the small perturbations about the equilibrium are where helioseismology gets its data Module 4 linearises Euler and continuity about this equilibrium. The acoustic cutoff frequency $\nu_{\rm ac} = c_s/(4\pi H)$ contains the scale height $H$ of Proposition 3. Module 9's Proposition 1 shows the idealisation cannot hold at all for a corona in a $1/r$ potential, where an isothermal hydrostatic atmosphere keeps a non-zero pressure at infinity and a wind follows. Module 10's Check 3 prices it where it does hold: treating the Perseus cluster core as static costs $3.98$ per cent of its mass, or $7.66$ per cent if the large-scale shear doubles the turbulent pressure
790: A closure $P=K\rho^{1+1/n}$ asserted, not derived Check 2 shows it holds with $n=3/2$ in the convection zone. Check 1 and Fig. 3 show that no single index holds through the Sun Module 6 derives the Schwarzschild criterion, which decides where $\nabla=\nabla_{\rm ad}$ and therefore where $n=3/2$ applies
791: Uniform entropy in the convection zone (Proposition 5) Correct where convection is efficient; wrong in the thin superadiabatic layer below the photosphere, and $\gamma\ne5/3$ in ionisation zones Module 6
792: No rotation Excludes centrifugal flattening and the rotational support of discs Module 11 (accretion discs)
793: No magnetic field Omits magnetic pressure and tension, which can support gas against gravity Module 12
794: Spherical symmetry Excludes binaries, tidal distortion and any non-spherical structure Not repaid in this module
795: Ideal gas (3.2), with radiation only in Proposition 6 Wrong where electrons are degenerate, as in white dwarfs, where the closure becomes the degenerate equation of state; the tabulated $\mu$ already differs from the ideal-gas value by $0.41$ per cent at the Sun's centre Module 13 (radiation); degeneracy is used only in Problem D1
798: One limitation belongs to the checks rather than to the equations. Every comparison in §10 is with a model built from (2.4). Only Check 3 reaches the real Sun, and it reaches it through a helioseismic inversion that itself uses a solar model as a reference. The agreement of Check 2 is a statement about BS2005-AGS,OP; it becomes a statement about the Sun only to the extent that the model's convection zone matches the Sun's, which helioseismology tests separately.
