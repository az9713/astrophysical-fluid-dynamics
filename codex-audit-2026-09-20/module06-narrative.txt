92: Module 6 — Convection and Thermal Instability
93: Hydrostatic balance is not stability. A stratified fluid can fail in two independent ways: buoyancy can stop restoring a displaced parcel, which is convection, and cooling can run away under compression, which is thermal instability. The Schwarzschild and Ledoux criteria, the collapse of the adiabatic gradient in an ionisation zone, mixing-length theory and its price, then Field's criterion and the two-phase interstellar medium. Two checks against measured data: mixing-length theory against solar granulation, and the predicted two-phase pressure window against 89 measured lines of sight.
116: 1. Two fluids that will not sit still
118: Module 3 solved the hydrostatic equation and built stars out of it. Nothing in that module asked whether the solutions it found would survive being disturbed. A stratified fluid in hydrostatic balance has every force balanced at every point, and that is a statement about the fluid at rest; it says nothing about what happens to a parcel of it that is nudged. Balance is not stability.
120: This module asks the stability question twice, of two different restoring mechanisms, and gets two different instabilities.
122: The first is buoyancy. Displace a parcel upward a little and it expands to the lower ambient pressure. If it arrives denser than its new surroundings it sinks back and the layer is stable. If it arrives lighter it keeps rising, and the layer convects. §2 turns that sentence into an inequality between two logarithmic derivatives, one belonging to the star and one to the gas, and §3 turns the inequality into a frequency. §4 asks what a composition gradient does to it and §5 what partial ionisation does. §6 then asks the quantitative question the criterion cannot answer — how much heat a convecting layer carries — and §7 takes the answer to two measurements of the solar surface.
124: The second is cooling. Compress a parcel of interstellar gas a little, at the pressure of its surroundings. If the compression makes it radiate away energy faster than it gains it, the parcel cools, and at fixed pressure a cooler parcel is denser, so it contracts further. No gravity is involved at all. §8 derives Field's criterion for that runaway, §9 shows that it carves the interstellar medium into two stable phases separated by a narrow band of pressures, and §10 compares the predicted band with 89 measured sight lines. That comparison is the anchor of this module, and it confirms one prediction and refutes another from the same measurement.
126: A terrestrial case sets the difficulty of the first question immediately. The International Standard Atmosphere fixes the tropospheric lapse rate at $6.50\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$, against a dry adiabatic value $g/c_p = 9.761\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$ for dry air. The ratio is $0.6659$, which makes the standard troposphere a polytrope of index $n = 4.2558$, with $\nabla = 1/(n+1) = 0.19026$ against the diatomic adiabatic value $2/7 = 0.28571$. By the criterion of §2 the troposphere is stable, with $33$ per cent to spare. It nevertheless convects every afternoon. The resolution is that air is not dry: a saturated parcel releases about $2.5\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}}$ of latent heat as it rises and condenses, which warms it on the way up and lowers its effective lapse rate to $5$–$6\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$, below the ambient $6.5$. Moist convection is unstable where dry convection is not. Problem C1 works this through.
128: The Sun has no latent-heat term of that kind, so the solar criterion is the clean one, and it is derived first.
130: 2. The displaced parcel and the Schwarzschild criterion
132: The whole of this section follows from the Euler equation of Module 2 and one assumption about the parcel. No transport theory is needed, and none is used.
172: Setup for Proposition 1. A parcel at the level where the ambient pressure is $P_0$ is displaced upward by $\delta r$ to the level $P_1$. It stays in pressure balance with its new surroundings throughout, and changes state adiabatically, so it arrives at $P_1$ with its own temperature $T_{\rm p}$. Only the comparison of $T_{\rm p}$ with the ambient $T$ at the same pressure decides the sign of the buoyancy force, because at equal pressure a hotter ideal gas of the same composition is the lighter one.
188: Four premises were used and each will be paid for later. Premise (i), uniform composition, is lifted in §4 . Premise (iii), instantaneous pressure balance, requires the parcel to move slowly compared with the sound speed, and §6 finds $v_c/c_s = 0.4271$ at the solar photosphere, where the premise is therefore spent. Premise (iv), adiabatic change, requires the parcel to lose no appreciable heat while it travels, and §6 and §7 find that at the photosphere radiation carries $98.7$ per cent of the flux, so it is spent there too. Only premise (ii) survives everywhere this module uses the criterion.
190: The criterion has one immediate consequence. For an ideal gas with a constant ratio of specific heats $\gamma$, an adiabatic change obeys $P\propto\rho^\gamma$ and the ideal gas law gives $P\propto\rho T$, so $T\propto P^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}$ and

Everything later in the module either changes the left-hand side of (2.4), which §3 does by reading $\nabla$ off a solar model, or the right-hand side, which §5 does by breaking the constancy of $\gamma$.

245: Read the $\nabla$ column of Table 1 outward, from the centre to the surface. It falls through $0.2116$ at $0.3016\,R$ to a shallow minimum — $0.1968$ at $0.4002\,R$ and $0.1962$ at $0.5018\,R$ — and then rises : $0.2074$ at $0.5979\,R$, $0.2616$ at $0.6804\,R$, $0.3259$ at $0.7099\,R$, and $0.3963$ at $0.7500\,R$. The interval is not monotone, and the sampling matters: the rows printed above dip to $0.1962$ at $0.5018\,R$, while the full profile reaches its minimum of $0.1937$ at $0.4531\,R$, a radius Table 1 does not sample. Measured from that minimum the factor is $2.046$. Either way the rise carries $\nabla$ straight across the flat adiabatic plateau $0.3957$. The base of the convection zone is where the crossing happens, at $0.7269\,R$, which is $T = 2.02\times10^{6}$ K in the same table.
247: It is worth being exact about which side moves. In this star the convection zone opens because $\nabla$ rises to meet $\nabla_{\rm ad}$, not because $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ falls to meet $\nabla$: over the whole radiative interior $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ is the flat plateau value. A radiative layer carries its flux with $\nabla = \nabla_{\rm rad}\propto \kappa L P/(m\,T^4)$, so a rising opacity $\kappa$ and a falling temperature both push $\nabla$ up, and both are happening here. This module computes neither. It reads the rise off the model and does not apportion it between opacity and temperature; doing so would need the opacity tables the model was built with, which are not part of this module. §5 shows the other mechanism, the one that moves $\nabla_{\rm ad}$, and shows that it operates somewhere else entirely.

348: 4. Ledoux: what a composition gradient does
350: Premise (i) of Proposition 1 was uniform composition. The Sun has burned hydrogen in its core for $4.6$ Gyr, so its $\mu$ is not uniform, and the premise has to be paid for.
365: The sign is the physical content. A $\mu$ that increases inward means $\nabla_\mu \gt 0$, since $P$ also increases inward; a parcel carried up from such a layer keeps its heavy composition and arrives denser than its new surroundings, which stabilises the stratification even where $\nabla \gt \nabla_{\rm ad}$.
367: Table 1's $\nabla_\mu$ column shows the Sun's. The $\mu$ used there is the table's own, $\mu = \rho k_BT/(P\,m_u)$, so no ionisation assumption enters. The share of $N^2$ contributed by the Ledoux term is $+84.05$ per cent at $0.050\,R$ ($\mu = 0.77684$, $\nabla_\mu = +0.37196$), $+74.96$ per cent at $0.100\,R$ ($\mu = 0.69264$, $\nabla_\mu = +0.22043$), $+50.74$ per cent at $0.150\,R$, $+16.53$ per cent at $0.200\,R$, $+3.07$ per cent at $0.251\,R$, $+1.37$ per cent at $0.302\,R$, $+0.67$ per cent at $0.400\,R$ and $+0.87$ per cent at $0.598\,R$. The gradient left by hydrogen burning is large where it exists and dies away outside the burning core.
373: The case where the choice of criterion does matter is a star with a receding convective core, which leaves a large $\nabla_\mu$ and a near-neutral layer in the same place. Such a layer is Schwarzschild-unstable and Ledoux-stable at once, and mixes on a thermal rather than a dynamical timescale — semiconvection. Problem D2 puts numbers on it and shows why the Sun escapes the ambiguity. This module does not develop semiconvection further.
375: The right-hand side of (2.4) has been treated so far as the constant $0.4$. It is not a constant.
376: 5. The collapse of $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ in a partially ionised gas
378: Equation (2.5) assumed a constant $\gamma$. In a gas that is partly ionised, some of the energy a compressed parcel receives goes into pulling electrons off atoms instead of into raising the temperature, and the parcel therefore heats less for the same compression. That lowers $\nabla_{\rm ad}$, and it lowers it a long way.
384: The total particle density is $n_{\rm H}(1+x)$, so the gas pressure is $P = n_{\rm H}(1+x)k_BT$. Eliminating $n_{\rm H}$ between this and (5.1) gives a closed form with no iteration:
388: For a partially ionised pure-hydrogen gas, the first law with the ionisation energy included in the internal energy gives
$$ \nabla_{\rm ad} \;=\; \frac{2+x(1-x)\Phi}{5+x(1-x)\Phi^2}, \qquad \Phi \;\equiv\; \frac{5}{2}+\frac{\chi_{\rm H}}{k_BT}. \tag{5.3}$$
401: The size of the effect is set by $\Phi$, and $\Phi$ is large. At the temperature where the depression is deepest on the photospheric isobar, $T = 12266$ K, $\chi_{\rm H}/k_BT = 12.86$ and so $\Phi = 15.36$; at the hotter temperature where the gas is half ionised, $T = 13136$ K, it is $12.01$. Either way the ionisation reservoir a parcel can pour heat into is about twelve times the thermal reservoir of one particle, which is why the depression is not a small correction. 421: 5.1 What this explains, and what it does not
423: It would be natural to say that the collapse of $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ is what makes the outer part of the Sun convective. The model table says otherwise, and the numbers are unambiguous.
431: So the hydrogen ionisation zone lies outside $0.983\,R$, in the outermost $1.7$ per cent of the radius. It does not open the convection zone, whose outer $27.3$ per cent by radius, from $0.7269\,R$ to the surface, is set the other way round by $\nabla$ rising, as §3.2 showed. What Proposition 4 explains is why convection reaches all the way up to the photosphere instead of stopping below it, and why the strongly superadiabatic layer of §6 sits where it does.
435: The criterion has now been derived, corrected for composition, and corrected for ionisation. It still says only whether convection happens. It says nothing about how much heat it carries.
437: 6. Mixing-length theory, and how superadiabatic the Sun must be
439: A stability criterion cannot give a flux, because it contains no statement about how far a parcel travels before it merges with its surroundings. Mixing-length theory supplies exactly one such statement and derives everything else from it.
463: The factors $1/2$ in $\delta T$ and $1/8$ in $v_c^2$ are the two averaging conventions of the standard theory. A different convention would move both by a numerical factor, which is absorbed into a different calibrated $\alpha$; it changes no physics and no scaling. What the scalings say is that $\alpha$ is a weak handle on the velocity and a strong one on the excess, and Problem C3 exploits exactly that.

514: 7. Check A: mixing-length theory against solar granulation
516: Mixing-length theory makes two predictions about the solar photosphere that can be compared with measurements taken there. Both measurements are of the quiet Sun at disk centre, and neither used a stellar model to produce the quantity compared.
518: 7.1 A1: the size of a granule
520: The overturning eddy of Definition 3 is one mixing length across. Seen from above, a convection cell has upflow in the middle and downflow at its edges, one mixing length each way, so the natural prediction for the diameter of a granule is $2\ell$. With $H = 143$ km and $\alpha = 2$, that is $\ell = 286$ km and $2\ell = 572$ km.

546: 7.3 A3, worked: what flux does the measured granulation actually carry?
548: This subsection uses no stellar model beyond the photospheric inputs already in use, and it rests on three assumptions that must be named before the numbers. The density $\rho$ at $\tau = 2/3$ is an input carried from Module 3, not a measurement. The observed intensity contrast is converted to a temperature contrast through a Planck source function, as if the contrast formed at a single depth. And the convective flux is formed as the product of a mean upflow speed with an rms temperature contrast, which assumes the two are perfectly correlated across the granulation pattern. A fourth mismatch is not an assumption but a fact worth stating: Oba et al.'s $0.65\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ is measured at $h = 40$ km, $\tau_{500} = 0.55$, while $\rho$ and $c_P$ are taken at $\tau = 2/3$.

579: 8. Thermal instability: the loss function and Field's criteria
581: The question is the same one Proposition 1 asked — a parcel is perturbed, does the perturbation grow? — with a different restoring mechanism. Compress a parcel of interstellar gas slightly at the pressure of its surroundings. Its density rises, so it radiates more; if it also loses the ability to be reheated fast enough, it cools, and at fixed pressure a cooler parcel is denser still. Nothing in this argument involves gravity, buoyancy or a stratification.
599: Which criterion applies is not a matter of taste. A parcel held at fixed density but given a different temperature is at the wrong pressure for its surroundings, and the force equation will not let it stay there: it expands or contracts on a sound-crossing time, which in the interstellar medium is far shorter than the cooling time of the warm gas. The isobaric criterion is the one compatible with the momentum equation, and that was Field's point in 1965 against Parker's earlier isochoric treatment. Because (8.2) is the weaker condition, far more of the interstellar medium is unstable than the isochoric criterion would suggest.

750: 10. The anchor: Jenkins & Tripp (2011) against the window
752: Jenkins & Tripp (2011) measured thermal pressures in the diffuse cold neutral medium along 89 sight lines, from the fine-structure excitation of neutral carbon observed with the STIS echelle spectrograph on the Hubble Space Telescope. Their mass-weighted lognormal fit, their equation (3), has mean $\log(p/k) = 3.58$ dex and an rms dispersion $\sigma_{\log P} = 0.175$ dex, which they state is a lower limit. The median is $3801.9\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$ and the $\pm1\sigma$ range is $2541$ to $5689\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$.
754: This module takes two predictions to that measurement, in the shape Module 2 used: one confirmed and one refuted, from the same source table.
756: 10.1 Confirmed: the measured centre lies inside the predicted window
760: The content of that confirmation is that a factor-$2.5$ window exists at all, and sits where it does. Neither of its edges was fitted to a pressure value. Wolfire et al. computed $P_{\min}$ and $P_{\max}$ from grain photoelectric heating and [C II]/[O I] cooling, with gas-phase carbon and oxygen abundances fixed by independent Hubble absorption-line studies and a PAH abundance fixed by ISO infrared emission data. One heating parameter, $\varphi_{\rm PAH} = 0.5$, was calibrated to an observed C I/C II column-density ratio; Wolfire's own detailed working credits that ratio to the C I and H I data compiled by Welty & Hobbs (2001), although an earlier passage cites Jenkins & Tripp (2001) alongside it. Jenkins & Tripp (2001) is the predecessor of, and distinct from, the 2011 pressure measurement compared here.
764: 10.2 How far the confirmation survives its own parameters
766: $\varphi_{\rm PAH}$ is a real lever on the window, not a formality. Wolfire's Table 4 tests five model variants, three of which vary $\varphi_{\rm PAH}$ discretely while two hold it at $0.5$ and vary other parameters. Raising $\varphi_{\rm PAH}$ raises all three pressures monotonically, and the authors state the size of the effect themselves: over $\varphi_{\rm PAH} = 0.25$ to $1$, $P_{\min}$ changes by a factor $1.5$ and $P_{\max}$ by $1.9$, with $P_{\max}$ decreasing by about $35$ per cent at $\varphi_{\rm PAH} = 0.25$.

788: 10.3 Refuted: a static two-phase medium cannot put cold gas below $P_{\min}$
790: Below $P_{\min}$ the equilibrium curve has no cold branch: by Proposition 8 the only equilibrium state at such a pressure is the warm one. A strict, static two-phase medium therefore allows none of the cold neutral medium to sit below $P_{\min}$. Jenkins & Tripp measured the cold medium, and they report in their section 10.1.2 that $29$ per cent of it is there.

811: The refutation survives the parameter sweep that the confirmation does not. Only the fitted fraction can be re-evaluated at another $P_{\min}$; the measured $29$ per cent is tied to $P_{\min} = 1960\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$, the value Jenkins & Tripp themselves quote, and cannot be moved without their data. 815: 10.4 What fails, precisely: the word "static", not the criterion
817: Jenkins & Tripp offer two readings of their own excess and prefer the second: either the Wolfire curve does not apply to the gas they observe, or, in their words, "rarefactions caused by turbulence create momentary excursions below the curve". Their dispersion of $0.175$ dex is itself read as turbulence at Mach $1$ to $4$. On that reading the thermal-equilibrium curve is an attractor, and the gas is pushed off it faster than it relaxes back. Problem D3 makes that quantitative by putting the cooling time against the crossing time of a turbulent eddy, and finds the two phases differ by a factor $794$ in cooling length — which is why a cold excursion below $P_{\min}$ can only be momentary while a warm departure can be sustained.
819: The same failure seen from the warm side. Heiles & Troland (2003), from 21-cm absorption, find that at least $48$ per cent of the warm neutral medium by column density lies at $500$–$5000$ K, inside the thermally unstable band; a strict two-phase medium allows only the small fraction in transit between phases. Their other numbers: the warm medium is $61$ per cent of the H I column at $|b|\gt 10^\circ$, and the cold-medium spin-temperature histogram peaks at $40$ K with a column-weighted median of $70$ K. Both the $0.61$ and the $0.48$ are column-density weighted and both are lower bounds.
