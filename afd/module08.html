1. A fluid with no length

Write out the equations of Module 2 and look for a length in them. There is none. Continuity, Euler and the energy equation contain densities, velocities, pressures and derivatives, and not one intrinsic scale — the viscosity and conductivity that would supply one were dropped in Module 2 on the strength of Module 1's Knudsen numbers. Every module since has treated that as a licence to work with smooth fields. This one treats it as a licence to do the opposite.

If the equations contain no length, nothing in them forbids a solution in which $\rho$, $v$ and $p$ change by a finite amount across zero distance. Such a surface is a shock. The physical layer is of course not of zero thickness: Problem D2 works out that in sea-level air it is a few mean free paths, about $2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{cm}$, which is $2\times10^{8}$ times thinner than the Trinity fireball was one millisecond after detonation. Against every other length in the problem that is zero, and the idealisation is safe by eight orders of magnitude.

The gain from the idealisation is larger than it first appears. Because the layer is unresolved, no equation of motion inside it is available — and none is needed. The three conservation laws, integrated across the layer, relate the state on one side to the state on the other with nothing said about the interior at all. That is §2, and it is the whole of shock theory in three lines. What comes out is not a small correction: the downstream gas can be a hundred times hotter than the upstream gas, and the ratio is fixed by the upstream Mach number alone.

A second absence of length drives the rest of the module. Deposit an energy $E$ at a point, instantaneously, in a uniform gas of density $\rho_0$, and ask for the radius $R(t)$ of the shock that runs out from it. The problem contains $E$, $\rho_0$ and $t$ and nothing else — no length, again. There is exactly one length that can be built from those three, so $R$ must be proportional to $(Et^2/\rho_0)^{1/5}$ and the exponent $2/5$ is settled before any dynamics is done. That argument is §4, and G. I. Taylor made it in 1941.

Four years later the argument met a test that no theoretician arranges for himself. On 16 July 1945 the first nuclear device was fired in New Mexico, and the fireball was photographed by a high-speed camera with a length scale in the frame. In 1950 Taylor published twenty-five radii against twenty-five times, read off that film, and compared them with the prediction he had made nine years before seeing it. §5 repeats the comparison from his own table, and finds two things: the exponent is confirmed to better than two and a half per cent over a factor of 620 in time, and the model that produced the exponent is refuted by the residuals from the very same fit. Both statements come from one table, and the second is the more instructive.

The module then leaves the laboratory twice. §6 uses a shock that a spacecraft flew through and measured from inside — Voyager 2 at the solar-wind termination shock in 2007, with Neptune's bow shock from the same instrument eighteen years earlier as a control. The control confirms the compression ceiling exactly; the termination shock refutes the single-fluid closure this book has used since Module 2, and the way it fails is precisely the way Module 1 said a collisionless plasma would fail. §7 then follows a supernova remnant from the moment the Sedov solution becomes valid to the moment it stops being valid, and §8 pays a debt: Module 7 closed by recording that its Rayleigh–Taylor half had no astronomical check anywhere in Modules 1–10. It has one now, and the check refutes the transfer.

2. The jump conditions

Everything in this module is a statement about the configuration defined next, and every hypothesis it carries is listed once here so that the propositions can name it rather than restate it.

Definition 1 (the shock). A surface across which $\rho$, $v$ and $p$ are discontinuous, of thickness negligible against every other length in the problem, moving through the gas at speed $D$. All quantities below are measured in the rest frame of the surface, in which the flow is steady and, locally, one-dimensional along the normal $\hat n$. Subscript $1$ denotes the state ahead of the surface (upstream) and subscript $2$ the state behind it (downstream), with $v_1, v_2 \gt 0$ the normal flow speeds in that frame, so the gas passes from side $1$ to side $2$. The gas is an ideal polytropic gas of ratio of specific heats $\gamma$ on both sides, with specific enthalpy $w = \gamma p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$.

Three hypotheses there will be paid for later, and it is worth naming which section pays. Steady in the surface frame fails when the shock accelerates on a timescale comparable to the crossing time; it does not fail for any case in this module. Polytropic with the same $\gamma$ on both sides is the assumption §5 finds broken at Trinity, where dissociation, vibration and radiation all act behind the front and Taylor argues — with evidence, and not quite successfully — that they cancel. One fluid, one temperature is the assumption §6 finds broken at the termination shock, where the energy goes to a population of pickup ions that the single-fluid description has no room for.

Definition 2 (the upstream Mach number). $$ M_1 \;\equiv\; \frac{v_1}{c_1}, \qquad c_1 \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\gamma p_1}{\rho_1}}, \tag{2.1}$$ with $v_1$ the normal flow speed in the frame of the surface, which is the speed at which the shock advances into gas that is at rest in the laboratory. $c_1$ is the adiabatic sound speed of Module 4 Proposition 1, evaluated in the upstream gas. A shock is called strong when $M_1 \gg 1$; Problem D1 puts a number on how large that has to be.

Fig. 1 is the whole proof in a picture, and the question mark in the middle of it is the content of the proposition.

Proposition 1 (the Rankine–Hugoniot conditions). Across the surface of Definition 1, $$ \rho_1 v_1 \;=\; \rho_2 v_2, \tag{2.2}$$ $$ \rho_1 v_1^2 + p_1 \;=\; \rho_2 v_2^2 + p_2, \tag{2.3}$$ $$ w_1 + \tfrac12 v_1^2 \;=\; w_2 + \tfrac12 v_2^2, \qquad w = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{p}{\rho}. \tag{2.4}$$ No property of the interior of the layer enters, and no transport coefficient appears.
Take the conservation forms of Module 2 — Module 2 Proposition 1 for mass, Proposition 2 for momentum and Proposition 5 for total energy — in the frame of Definition 1, where $\partial_t = 0$ and only the normal derivative survives. Each then reads $\mathrm{d}F/\mathrm{d}n = 0$ for its own flux $F$: $$ F_{\rm mass} = \rho v, \qquad F_{\rm mom} = \rho v^2 + p, \qquad F_{\rm en} = \rho v\left(w + \tfrac12 v^2\right). $$ Integrate each across the layer, from a point at $n = -\epsilon$ ahead of it to a point at $n = +\epsilon$ behind it. The integral of an exact derivative is the difference of its endpoint values, so $F(+\epsilon) = F(-\epsilon)$ for each of the three, whatever happens in between, and taking $\epsilon$ to the layer thickness gives (2.2)–(2.4). Dividing the third by the first, which is legitimate because $\rho v \neq 0$ for a shock, turns the energy flux equality into the enthalpy form (2.4). ∎

Two remarks on what the proof used. It required each equation to be a divergence, which is why the conservation forms were taken and not the convective ones: $\partial_t v + (v\cdot\nabla)v = \dots$ is not a divergence and cannot be integrated across a discontinuity. And it required the energy equation with no source term, so a layer that radiates away a finite amount of energy per unit area breaks (2.4) — which is exactly the isothermal shock of Proposition 4, where (2.4) is deliberately replaced.

The three fluxes are stated here for a gas with no magnetic field. A magnetised shock carries two further jump conditions, on the normal and tangential components of $\mathbf B$, and the momentum flux acquires the magnetic terms of Module 2. §6 uses a shock that is magnetised and quasi-perpendicular, and it is careful to compare only the quantities for which the difference does not matter; the place where it would matter is named there and the comparison is refused.

It is worth pausing on how little was assumed. Nothing was said about viscosity, about heat conduction, about how thick the layer is or about whether the gas inside it is even in local thermodynamic equilibrium. All of that is real, and all of it is invisible to (2.2)–(2.4). What the conservation laws give up in exchange is any information about the structure of the transition: they say where the gas ends up, never how it got there. Problem D2 shows that the trade is a good one in air and a very bad one at the termination shock, where the layer is $10^5$ to $10^6$ times thinner than a collisional mean free path and the phrase "local thermodynamic equilibrium" has no referent at all.

3. What the jumps say: a ceiling, a direction, and an escape

Proposition 1 is three equations in the three downstream unknowns $\rho_2$, $v_2$, $p_2$, given the upstream state. Solving them is algebra, and the answer is worth more than the algebra.

Proposition 2 (the jump ratios, and the ceiling on compression). For the gas of Definition 1, the non-trivial solution of (2.2)–(2.4) is $$ \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} \;=\; \frac{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}{(\gamma-1)M_1^2+2}, \qquad \frac{p_2}{p_1} \;=\; \frac{2\gamma M_1^2-(\gamma-1)}{\gamma+1}, \tag{3.1}$$ $$ \frac{T_2}{T_1} \;=\; \frac{p_2/p_1}{\rho_2/\rho_1}, \qquad M_2^2 \;=\; \frac{(\gamma-1)M_1^2+2}{2\gamma M_1^2-(\gamma-1)}. \tag{3.2}$$ As $M_1\to\infty$ the density ratio saturates at $$ \left.\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}\right|_{\max} \;=\; \frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}, \tag{3.3}$$ which is $4$ for $\gamma = 5/3$ and $6$ for $\gamma = 7/5$, while $p_2/p_1$ and $T_2/T_1$ grow without bound, both as $M_1^2$.
Write $r = \rho_2/\rho_1$. Mass conservation (2.2) gives $v_2 = v_1/r$. Substituting into the momentum condition (2.3), $$ p_2 - p_1 \;=\; \rho_1v_1^2\left(1-\frac1r\right), $$ and dividing by $p_1 = \rho_1c_1^2/\gamma$ turns it into $p_2/p_1 = 1 + \gamma M_1^2(1-1/r)$. Put that and $v_2 = v_1/r$ into the enthalpy condition (2.4), multiply through by $(\gamma-1)/c_1^2$, and the result is a quadratic in $r$ whose two roots are $r = 1$ — the trivial solution, no shock — and the first of (3.1). The pressure ratio follows by back-substitution, the temperature ratio from the ideal gas law $T \propto p/\rho$, and $M_2$ from $M_2^2 = v_2^2\rho_2/(\gamma p_2) = (v_1^2/r^2)(r\rho_1)/(\gamma p_2)$. Taking $M_1\to\infty$ in the first of (3.1) leaves $(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$, which is (3.3). ∎

The two behaviours in Proposition 2 are physically different and the contrast is the point of Fig. 2. Compression is bounded because the gas that piles up behind the front pushes back, and past a certain density no increase in the ram pressure can make it denser; heating is unbounded because the kinetic energy that has to go somewhere goes into internal energy, and internal energy has no ceiling. A strong shock is a device for making a hot gas, not a dense one. That single sentence explains why supernova remnants shine in X-rays rather than looking like dense shells, and why §7 can speak of $10^8\ \mathrm{K}$ gas at a density of a few particles per cubic centimetre.

The ceiling is approached quickly. At $\gamma = 5/3$, a Mach 3 shock has reached 75.00 per cent of it, Mach 5 gives 89.29 per cent, Mach 10 gives 97.09 per cent and Mach 30 gives 99.67 per cent. Problem D1 asks for the Mach number at which 99 per cent is reached and gets $M_1 = 17.2$ for $\gamma = 5/3$ and $22.2$ for $\gamma = 7/5$; the Trinity shock at one millisecond was at $M_1 = 43$ and was therefore strong by any measure.

3.1 Why there is no such thing as a rarefaction shock

Nothing in Proposition 1 prefers one direction. Put $M_1 \lt 1$ into (3.1) and the formulae return perfectly finite ratios: at $M_1 = 0.7$ and $\gamma = 5/3$ they give $\rho_2/\rho_1 = 0.5616$ and $p_2/p_1 = 0.3625$, a jump from dense to rarefied that satisfies mass, momentum and energy conservation exactly. Such a thing is never seen. The reason is not in the conservation laws, and this is the only place in Modules 1–8 where the selection is made by thermodynamics rather than by dynamics.

Proposition 3 (the entropy jump fixes the direction). Across the surface of Definition 1, the entropy change per particle is $$ \frac{\Delta s}{k_B} \;=\; \frac{1}{\gamma-1}\,\ln\!\left[\frac{p_2}{p_1}\left(\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\right)^{\!\gamma}\right], \tag{3.4}$$ with the ratios of (3.1). It is zero at $M_1 = 1$, positive for $M_1 \gt 1$ and negative for $M_1 \lt 1$. The second law therefore admits only $M_1 \gt 1$: a shock is always compressive, and always advances into its gas supersonically.
For an ideal gas the entropy per particle is $s = c_v\ln(p\rho^{-\gamma}) + \text{const}$ with $c_v = k_B/(\gamma-1)$, which gives (3.4) directly. Set $M_1 = 1+\epsilon$ and expand both ratios of (3.1) to third order in $\epsilon$. The first-order and second-order terms cancel identically in the combination (3.4), and what survives is $$ \frac{\Delta s}{k_B} \;=\; \frac{16\gamma}{3(\gamma+1)^2}\,\epsilon^3 + O(\epsilon^4), $$ an odd leading power. An odd leading power is the whole argument: $\Delta s$ changes sign with $\epsilon$, so the two branches are not symmetric and exactly one of them is admissible. The sign is confirmed away from $M_1 = 1$ by evaluation: at $\gamma = 5/3$, $\Delta s/k_B = +0.00101$ at $M_1 = 1.1$, $+0.85031$ at $M_1 = 3$, and $-0.00159$ at $M_1 = 0.9$, $-1.21225$ at $M_1 = 0.5$. ∎

A second obstruction sits below the first, and it is worth separating because it is not thermodynamic at all. The pressure ratio in (3.1) vanishes at $M_1 = \sqrt{(\gamma-1)/2\gamma}$, which is $0.4472$ for $\gamma = 5/3$, and goes negative below it — at $M_1 = 0.4$ it returns $p_2/p_1 = -0.0500$. Below that Mach number the "rarefaction shock" is not a solution that the second law forbids; it is not a solution at all, because it would require a negative pressure. So the two objections are of different kinds and cover different ranges, and Problem C3 asks the reader to state both.

What happens instead is a rarefaction fan: an expansion spreads out into a continuous region whose width grows with time, because each characteristic in it travels at its own speed and the faster ones run ahead. This module does not derive it. What matters here is why one is needed — the discontinuous alternative is forbidden — and that is a statement Proposition 3 makes without any further machinery.

3.2 The escape: a shock that radiates

The ceiling (3.3) is a consequence of (2.4), and (2.4) assumed that no energy leaves the layer. Molecular gas radiates efficiently, and a dense shocked molecular cloud can lose its thermal energy far faster than the gas crosses the layer. Then the correct closure is not "no losses" but "the temperature does not change", and the ceiling disappears.

Proposition 4 (the isothermal shock). Replace (2.4) by $T_2 = T_1$, keeping (2.2) and (2.3). Then $$ \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} \;=\; \mathcal{M}^2, \qquad \mathcal{M} \;\equiv\; \frac{v_1}{c_T}, \quad c_T \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{\mu m_u}}, \tag{3.5}$$ and there is no upper bound on the compression.
With $T$ fixed, $p = \rho c_T^2$ on both sides. Mass conservation gives $v_2 = v_1\rho_1/\rho_2$, and momentum conservation becomes $$ \rho_1v_1^2 + \rho_1c_T^2 \;=\; \rho_2 v_2^2 + \rho_2c_T^2 \;=\; \frac{\rho_1^2v_1^2}{\rho_2} + \rho_2c_T^2. $$ Writing $r = \rho_2/\rho_1$ and dividing by $\rho_1c_T^2$ gives $\mathcal{M}^2 + 1 = \mathcal{M}^2/r + r$, whose roots are $r = 1$ and $r = \mathcal{M}^2$. ∎

The numbers are startling and they matter for the rest of the book. Molecular hydrogen at $10\ \mathrm{K}$ with $\mu = 2.33$ has $c_T = 0.189\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. A $10\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ flow — a very ordinary speed in the interstellar medium, far below anything a supernova produces — is then at $\mathcal{M} = 52.9$ and compresses the gas by a factor $2802$, against $3.996$ for the adiabatic shock at the same speed. That is three orders of magnitude, and it is why the dense filaments in which Module 5's Jeans criterion is actually met are made by shocks and not by gravity alone. The whole difference is one closure: whether the layer keeps its heat or radiates it away.

$\mathcal{M}$, the isothermal Mach number of (3.5), is written in a different typeface from $M_1$ deliberately. They are ratios to different speeds — $c_T$ is the isothermal sound speed, smaller than the adiabatic $c$ of Definition 2 by $\sqrt\gamma$ — so quoting a Mach number for a radiating shock without saying which one is quoted is an error of a factor $1.29$ at $\gamma = 5/3$. This book uses $M$ for adiabatic and $\mathcal{M}$ for isothermal throughout this module and nowhere else.

4. The Sedov–Taylor blast wave

Deposit energy $E$ at a point at $t = 0$, in a uniform gas of density $\rho_0$ and negligible pressure, and follow the shock that runs outward. The problem is the cleanest use in this book of an argument that needs no equations at all.

Proposition 5 (the exponent, from dimensions alone). If the flow depends on no quantity other than $E$, $\rho_0$ and $t$, then the shock radius is $$ R(t) \;=\; \xi_0\left(\frac{E\,t^2}{\rho_0}\right)^{1/5}, \tag{4.1}$$ with $\xi_0$ a dimensionless number. The exponent $2/5$ is forced, and no property of the gas enters it.
In the dimensions $[\text{mass}]$, $[\text{length}]$, $[\text{time}]$, $E$ has dimensions $\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}$, $\rho_0$ has $\mathrm{M\,L^{-3}}$ and $t$ has $\mathrm{T}$. Seek $R = E^a\rho_0^b t^c$ with $\mathrm{L}$ on the left: matching mass gives $a + b = 0$; matching time gives $-2a + c = 0$; matching length gives $2a - 3b = 1$. The first and third give $a = 1/5$, $b = -1/5$, and the second then gives $c = 2/5$. The solution is unique, so (4.1) is the only possible form. ∎

Three consequences follow immediately and none needs the interior solution. The shock speed is $v_s = \mathrm{d}R/\mathrm{d}t = \tfrac25 R/t \propto t^{-3/5}$, so the blast decelerates; by Proposition 7 below the post-shock temperature goes as $v_s^2 \propto t^{-6/5}$; and, because $R^5t^{-2} = \xi_0^5E/\rho_0$ is a constant of the motion, a single measurement of $R$ at a single $t$ determines $E$ once $\xi_0$ and $\rho_0$ are known. Taylor used exactly that, and Problem K3 shows what it hides.

What dimensional analysis cannot supply is $\xi_0$. That number contains all the dynamics, and getting it requires solving the interior flow.

Proposition 6 (the similarity solution and the constant $\xi_0$). Write $\lambda = r/R(t)$ and $$ v(r,t) = \frac{r}{t}\,u(\lambda), \qquad \rho(r,t) = \rho_0\,g(\lambda), \qquad p(r,t) = \rho_0\frac{r^2}{t^2}\,\Pi(\lambda). \tag{4.2}$$ Then the spherical Euler equations of Module 2 reduce to three ordinary differential equations in $\ln\lambda$, with boundary values at $\lambda = 1$ given by the strong-shock limit of Proposition 2, $$ u(1) = \frac{4}{5(\gamma+1)}, \qquad g(1) = \frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}, \qquad \Pi(1) = \frac{8}{25(\gamma+1)}, \tag{4.3}$$ and the constant of (4.1) is fixed by the energy integral, $$ 1 \;=\; 4\pi\,\xi_0^5\,J, \qquad J \;=\; \int_0^1 \lambda^4\left[\tfrac12 g u^2 + \frac{\Pi}{\gamma-1}\right]\mathrm{d}\lambda. \tag{4.4}$$ Numerically $\xi_0 = 1.151666$ for $\gamma = 5/3$ and $\xi_0 = 1.032777$ for $\gamma = 7/5$.
Substitute (4.2) into continuity, Euler and the entropy equation, using $\partial\lambda/\partial t = -\tfrac25\lambda/t$ and $\partial\lambda/\partial r = \lambda/r$, which follow from $R \propto t^{2/5}$. Writing $'$ for $\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\ln\lambda$ and $a \equiv u - \tfrac25$, every factor of $r$ and $t$ cancels and the three equations become $$ a\,\frac{g'}{g} + 3u + u' = 0, \qquad a\,u' + u^2 - u + \frac{\Pi}{g}\left(2 + \frac{\Pi'}{\Pi}\right) = 0, \qquad a\left(\frac{\Pi'}{\Pi} - \gamma\frac{g'}{g}\right) + 2(u-1) = 0. $$ That every factor cancels is the content of the similarity hypothesis, and it is a check on (4.2): had the substitution left an explicit $t$, no solution of this form would exist. Eliminating $g'/g$ and $\Pi'/\Pi$ from the middle equation gives $u'$ explicitly, $$ u'\left(a^2 - \gamma\frac{\Pi}{g}\right) \;=\; a\left(u - u^2\right) + \frac{\Pi}{g}\left(3\gamma u - \tfrac65\right), $$ and the system is integrated inward from $\lambda = 1$ with (4.3).
For (4.3): in the strong-shock limit Proposition 2 gives $\rho_2 = \rho_0(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$, $v_2 = 2D/(\gamma+1)$ and $p_2 = 2\rho_0D^2/(\gamma+1)$, with $D = \mathrm{d}R/\mathrm{d}t = \tfrac25R/t$ supplied by (4.1) itself. Evaluating (4.2) at $r = R$ and dividing gives $u(1) = v_2t/R = \tfrac25\cdot 2/(\gamma+1)$, $g(1) = \rho_2/\rho_0$ and $\Pi(1) = p_2/[\rho_0(R/t)^2] = 2(4/25)/(\gamma+1)$, which are (4.3). So §4 introduces no new physical input: the boundary condition of the blast wave is Proposition 2 plus similarity.

For (4.4): the total energy inside the shock is $$ E = \int_0^R\left[\tfrac12\rho v^2 + \frac{p}{\gamma-1}\right]4\pi r^2\,\mathrm{d}r = 4\pi\rho_0\frac{R^5}{t^2}\,J $$ after substituting (4.2) and $r = \lambda R$. But $R^5/t^2 = \xi_0^5E/\rho_0$ by (4.1), so $E$ cancels from both sides and what is left is $1 = 4\pi\xi_0^5J$. The energy integral does not determine the energy; it determines the constant. ∎

The integration is done in $\ln\lambda$ rather than $\lambda$ because $g \to 0$ and $\Pi \to \infty$ at the centre as pure powers of $\lambda$, so their logarithms are linear and the integration stays well conditioned. It is stopped at $\lambda = 9.1\times10^{-4}$ rather than at $\lambda = 0$, and nothing is lost: the integrand of (4.4) carries $\lambda^4$, so everything inside $\lambda = 10^{-3}$ contributes less than $10^{-15}$ of the total, and $\xi_0$ is unchanged in its eighth digit whether the integration stops at $6.7\times10^{-3}$ or at $3.4\times10^{-4}$. The reason a stopping point is needed at all is that the system has a fixed point at $u = (2/5)/\gamma$ towards which the physical solution is unstable going inward, so rounding error overwhelms it below about $\lambda = 10^{-4}$.

Two independent verifications are worth stating, because a constant obtained by numerical integration of an eliminated system is exactly the sort of number that can be wrong without looking wrong.

First, internal. The elimination that produced $u'$ used two of the three equations and discarded them. Differentiating the integrated solution numerically and substituting it back into the discarded continuity and entropy equations gives residuals no larger than $7\times10^{-9}$ anywhere between $\lambda = 0.1$ and $\lambda = 0.9$, for both values of $\gamma$. That tests the algebra, not merely the convergence of the integrator.

Second, external. Tang & Chevalier (2017) state that for $\gamma = 5/3$ the constant in $R = (\xi Et^2/\rho)^{1/5}$ equals $2.026$, citing Taylor (1946), Sedov (1959) and Book (1994); that is $\xi_0 = 2.026^{1/5} = 1.151670$ against $1.151666$ computed here, a ratio of $0.999997$. Kamm & Timmes (2007) define their spherical standard problem by $\rho_0 = 1\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $\gamma = 1.4$ and $E = 0.851072\ \mathrm{erg}$, which puts the shock at $r = 1.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ at $t = 1.0\ \mathrm{s}$; that statement is the constant, $\xi_0 = 0.851072^{-1/5} = 1.032777$, and it agrees to all six figures.

A third comparison, and the one that matters for §5. Taylor obtained $K \equiv 1/\xi_0^5$ for $\gamma = 1.40$ in 1941 by hand integration and printed $K = 0.856$. The exact value is $0.851072$. His approximate quadratures were therefore good to 0.58 per cent — and because $E$ is linear in $K$, that error propagates to his energy as itself, which is small compared with everything else that will go wrong in §5.

The interior of the solution is as instructive as the constant. Nearly all the shocked mass sits in a thin shell just behind the front: for $\gamma = 5/3$ the mass median radius is $r/R = 0.9392$, the density at $r/R = 0.5$ has fallen to $1.53$ per cent of its post-shock value, and the pressure has flattened to a plateau at $0.306$ of the post-shock pressure and stays there all the way in. A hot, nearly empty, nearly uniform-pressure interior driving a thin dense shell — that is the picture, and §7 shows it becoming literally true as the snowplough phase arrives.

5. The anchor: Trinity, 16 July 1945

Every module in this book is checked against a published measurement, and the checks have been of uneven quality. Module 3's solar comparison was partly circular, because the model it tested had gone into the model that produced the "measurement". Module 7's laboratory check carried a standard deviation but its astronomical half carried none. This module's anchor has neither defect, and it is worth saying why before any number appears.

Taylor's Table 1 is twenty-five radii read off photographic film with a length scale in the frame, against twenty-five shutter times read off the same film. Nothing about the blast-wave solution went into producing those numbers. No one solved (4.1) to obtain them and no fitted model stands between the film and the table. So the table can test the theory, which is precisely what Module 3's solar model could not do. The prediction, moreover, was published in 1941 — four years before the explosion, nine years before Taylor saw the film — so there was no opportunity to tune it.

The weaknesses are worth stating with equal directness. The radii are read by eye from 1945 photographs and carry no quoted uncertainty. The times come from the camera. Taylor groups his rows into four blocks by the source of the photograph, his "authority" column, and those blocks turn out to matter. And the reader is asked to believe a table transcribed from a 1950 letterpress page; §5.1 closes that gap with a check of the data file against the paper's own logarithm columns.

6. The astrophysical check: Voyager 2 inside a shock

Every shock in this book so far has been inferred from a distance. In 2007 a spacecraft flew through one and measured it from the inside. Voyager 2's plasma experiment recorded a decrease in solar-wind speed from about 9 June 2007, culminating in several crossings of the solar-wind termination shock at a heliocentric radius of 84 AU between 30 August and 1 September 2007.

The paper reporting it supplies something rarer than the crossing itself: a control. The same instrument on the same spacecraft crossed Neptune's inbound bow shock in August 1989, eighteen years earlier, at 30 AU. So the comparison below is not merely theory against observation; it is one shock against another, through one instrument, with the theory saying what should differ and what should not.

6.1 CONFIRMED: the ceiling, and a consistency test with no free parameter

From the figure caption comparing the two crossings, read directly: at Neptune's bow shock the solar-wind speed fell by a factor of four and the density increased by a factor of four, while the temperature increased by a factor of 100.

Fig. 4 draws all five comparisons of this section on one logarithmic axis, as measured divided by predicted, so that a confirmation and a refutation are the same kind of mark in the same place.

The factor of four in density is the ceiling (3.3) for $\gamma = 5/3$, and $\gamma = 5/3$ is not fitted — it is fixed by the gas being monatomic. Ratio of measured to predicted: 1.000. That the density jump and the speed drop are the same number is mass conservation, (2.2), holding to the precision of the read.

The temperature supplies a second, independent test of the same shock. A temperature ratio of 100 implies, by inverting (3.2) at $\gamma = 5/3$, an upstream Mach number $M_1 = 17.8$. That Mach number then predicts a density ratio of $3.963$ through the first of (3.1), against the measured 4. Consistency ratio 0.991. One Mach number, two independent Rankine–Hugoniot relations, one answer: that is the shape of a real test, and it is what Proposition 2 is for.

The caption gives no uncertainty on any of the three factors, and the Neptune curves are normalised before comparison — speed divided by 1.3, density by five, temperature by two — so that the two shocks can be overlaid. The numbers are therefore read to one or two significant figures, and nothing finer is claimed from them. This is the same weakness Module 7's solar anchor had, declared here for the same reason.

6.2 REFUTED: the closure, not the conservation laws

At the termination shock the same instrument found the temperature increasing by a factor of only ten and the density by only two. The authors state the consequence plainly: if all the flow energy were transferred to the thermal ions, as happens at planetary bow shocks, the heliosheath temperature would be $10^6$ K, whereas the observed temperature is $10^5$ K.

Check 5, REFUTED. Observed over predicted, $10^5/10^6 = \mathbf{0.10}$: a factor of ten short. What fails is the closure — one fluid, one temperature — and not the conservation laws of Proposition 1.

The authors' explanation is what makes this a refutation of a specific hypothesis rather than a puzzle. The energy per proton falls by 80 per cent across the shock, and it goes to pickup ions — hot protons created when neutral interstellar atoms are ionised inside the heliosphere — rather than to the thermal plasma. A single-fluid ideal gas has nowhere to put that energy, so it predicts a hot thermal population that is not there. And Module 1 has already said why this is possible: the solar wind at 84 AU is collisionless, so no mechanism forces one temperature on all the ion populations. The failure was licensed by Module 1 and is observed here.

6.3 The comparison this module refuses to make

The same paper's Table 1 gives, for the second crossing, a compression ratio of $2.38 \pm 0.14$ against an upstream fast-mode Mach number of $4.9 \pm 0.1$. The gas-dynamic prediction of (3.1) at $M_1 = 4.9$ and $\gamma = 5/3$ is $3.556$, so the measurement is $0.669$ of it — a departure of 8.4 formal standard deviations, which would be by far the largest refutation in this book. It is not used, for two reasons.

First, it is partly circular. The table's caption states that the shock orientation is found as "the direction that minimizes the differences of the Rankine–Hugoniot conditions across the shock", and that the shock speed, the field angle and the Mach numbers are then calculated from that orientation. The compression ratio and the Mach number therefore come from one Rankine–Hugoniot fit to one data window, so comparing them against the Rankine–Hugoniot prediction tests the fit against itself. This is Module 3's circularity in a new costume, and it is refused for the same reason.

Second, it is the wrong formula. The same table gives the angle between the shock normal and the magnetic field as $82.8^\circ \pm 3.9^\circ$: this is a quasi-perpendicular magnetohydrodynamic shock, and (3.1) is a gas-dynamic result. The right formula is the oblique magnetohydrodynamic jump condition, which this book does not derive anywhere. Module 12 builds magnetohydrodynamics — flux freezing, magnetic pressure and tension, Alfvén waves, the magnetorotational instability — and stops short of shock jump conditions. Saying so is better than promising a repayment that no planned module makes.

The temperature comparison of §6.2 has neither defect. The observed $10^5$ K and the predicted $10^6$ K are separate statements: the first is a measurement, the second is what full thermalisation of the measured flow energy would give, and no fit connects them.

7. A supernova remnant through its phases

The Sedov solution is exact for a point explosion in a pressureless uniform gas, and a supernova remnant is neither at the start nor at the end of its life. This section puts brackets around the interval in which (4.1) is the right description, and the brackets are as much a result as the solution is.

Take the standard numbers: $E = 10^{51}$ erg, ambient hydrogen density $n_{\rm H} = 1\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, so $\rho_0 = 1.4\,m_u n_{\rm H} = 2.325\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ with helium at cosmic abundance, $\mu = 0.61$ for the fully ionised gas behind the shock, and $\gamma = 5/3$.

Proposition 7 (the post-shock temperature of a strong shock). In the limit $M_1 \gg 1$, $$ T_2 \;=\; \frac{2(\gamma-1)}{(\gamma+1)^2}\,\frac{\mu m_u v_s^2}{k_B}, \tag{7.1}$$ which for $\gamma = 5/3$ is $T_2 = 3\mu m_u v_s^2/(16 k_B)$.
In the strong-shock limit Proposition 2 gives $p_2 = 2\rho_1v_s^2/(\gamma+1)$ and $\rho_2 = \rho_1(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$. The ideal gas law in the form $T = \mu m_u p/(k_B\rho)$ then gives $$ T_2 = \frac{\mu m_u}{k_B}\frac{p_2}{\rho_2} = \frac{\mu m_u}{k_B}\cdot\frac{2\rho_1v_s^2}{\gamma+1}\cdot\frac{\gamma-1}{\rho_1(\gamma+1)}, $$ which is (7.1). At $\gamma = 5/3$ the prefactor is $2(2/3)/(64/9) = 3/16$. ∎
$\mu$ must be stated with every answer from (7.1), and the book's convention from Module 6 — $\mu$ measured against $m_u$ — is used here. The second branch of (7.2) is not a refinement. Using bremsstrahlung alone at all temperatures moves $t_{\rm rad}$ to $3.32\times10^5$ yr — a factor 8 later. Line cooling is the dominant term everywhere below $10^7$ K, and the remnant spends almost its entire life below $10^7$ K. A calculation that omits it does not get the answer slightly wrong; it gets the phase structure of the remnant wrong.

7.1 What the transition criterion is worth

A one-line criterion is not a simulation, and the honest thing is to say by how much it misses. Cioffi, McKee & Bertschinger (1988) computed the radiative transition with a hydrodynamic code and fitted the result. Their equations (3.33a) and (3.33b) give, for solar abundances and $E_{51} = n_0 = 1$,

$$ R_{\rm PDS} = 14.0\ \mathrm{pc}, \qquad v_{\rm PDS} = 413\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}, \qquad t_{\rm PDS} = \tfrac25 R_{\rm PDS}/v_{\rm PDS} = 1.326\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{yr}. $$ $t_{\rm PDS}$ above is derived from their two fitted quantities using $R = \tfrac25v_st$, which holds at the end of the Sedov stage. It can be checked against the same paper by a second route: their equation (3.10) gives a shell-formation time $t_{\rm sf} = 3.61\times10^4$ yr for these parameters, and their equation (3.11) sets $t_{\rm PDS} = t_{\rm sf}/e$, which is $1.328\times10^4$ yr. The two routes agree to 0.15 per cent.

Comparing:

Check 6, a calibration and not a confirmation. $R_{\rm rad}/R_{\rm PDS} = 1.573$ and $t_{\rm rad}/t_{\rm PDS} = 3.081$. A one-line criterion applied to a stated cooling function gives a radius 1.57 times and a time 3.08 times those of a full radiative-hydrodynamic simulation. The time is less accurate than the radius, and the reason is (4.1): because $R \propto t^{2/5}$, a factor 3.08 in $t$ is only a factor 1.57 in $R$. That is what should be claimed for the criterion, and no more.
The two calculations do not use the same cooling function. Cioffi et al. use $\Lambda = 1.6\times10^{-19}\,\zeta_m T^{-1/2}$ for $10^5 \lt T \lt 10^{7.5}$ K; this section uses the $T^{-0.7}$ branch of (7.2). So the ratios 1.573 and 3.081 test the criterion and the cooling curve together, and neither can be blamed separately on this evidence.

After $t_{\rm rad}$ the shell is thin, dense and driven by the pressure of the hot interior — the interior that Proposition 6 showed is nearly empty and nearly uniform in pressure. Then $R \propto t^{0.30}$ rather than $t^{0.40}$, and the similarity solution of §4 no longer applies. That phase is not derived in this book.

8. The debt from Module 7: Rayleigh–Taylor at Tycho

Module 7 closed by recording a gap: its Rayleigh–Taylor half was checked against a laser experiment and against nothing in the sky, and no module from 1 to 10 was going to repay it. This section repays it, using the structure that §7 has just built, and the answer is a refutation.

A young supernova remnant is not one shock but three surfaces. The ejecta drive a forward shock (the blast wave) into the interstellar medium. The ambient gas pushes back on the ejecta through a reverse shock running into them. Between the two lies the contact discontinuity, separating shocked ejecta from shocked ambient gas. Shocked ejecta are the heavier fluid and sit inside; shocked ambient gas is lighter and sits outside; and the shell decelerates. In the frame of the decelerating interface that is exactly Module 7's unstable ordering, and the contact discontinuity grows Rayleigh–Taylor fingers.

8.1 The comparison needs no distance and no age

Module 7 measured a mixing law, $h = \alpha A g t^2$, for a plane interface under a constant acceleration, and derived $g = m(1-m)R/t^2$ for a shell whose radius follows $R \propto t^m$. Putting the second into the first is the whole of this section's theory.

Proposition 9 (the mixing width of a decelerating shell). For a shell with $R \propto t^m$ whose interface carries Atwood number $A$ and mixing coefficient $\alpha$, $$ h \;=\; \alpha A\, m(1-m)\, R_{\rm cd}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{h}{R_{\rm BW}} \;=\; \alpha\,A\,m(1-m)\,\frac{R_{\rm cd}}{R_{\rm BW}}. \tag{8.1}$$ Both $t$ and $R$ cancel. Moreover $A \le 1$ by its definition and $m(1-m) \le \tfrac14$ with equality at $m = \tfrac12$, so $$ \frac{h}{R_{\rm BW}} \;\le\; \frac{\alpha}{4}\,\frac{R_{\rm cd}}{R_{\rm BW}} \tag{8.2}$$ for every shell, whatever its Atwood number and whatever its expansion index.
Substitute $g = m(1-m)R_{\rm cd}/t^2$ into $h = \alpha Agt^2$; the $t^2$ cancels identically, leaving $h = \alpha A m(1-m)R_{\rm cd}$, and dividing by $R_{\rm BW}$ gives (8.1). For (8.2): $A = (\rho_2-\rho_1)/(\rho_1+\rho_2) \le 1$ because both densities are positive, and $m(1-m)$ is a downward parabola in $m$ with maximum $\tfrac14$ at $m = \tfrac12$. ∎

Two consequences are worth stating before any number is put in. No distance and no age are needed: (8.1) is a ratio of radii, so the disagreement among published distances to Tycho — which is real — cannot affect it, and neither can the epoch at which the remnant is observed. And (8.2) turns the comparison into a test rather than a fit: if the observed width exceeds $\alpha/4$ times the radius ratio, no choice of $A$ or $m$ can rescue the prediction.

The inputs, each read from the paper that supplies it:

  • $\alpha$. Module 7 verified $\alpha_B = 0.038 \pm 0.008$ from the National Ignition Facility experiments. It is the bubble coefficient.
  • $A$. Taken at its ceiling, $A = 1$. The one-dimensional solution has a density peak at the contact discontinuity — Wang & Chevalier note its absence in their two-dimensional runs by comparison with the one-dimensional profile — so the shocked ejecta are far denser than the shocked ambient gas there and $A \to 1$. Taking $A = 1$ makes the prediction an upper bound in this variable.
  • $m$. Katsuda et al. (2010) measure Tycho's forward-shock expansion index from Chandra proper motions: it varies from $0.26$ to $0.65$ around the rim and averages $0.52$. Wang & Chevalier's model gives $m = 0.47$ at Tycho's epoch. Over that whole range $m(1-m)$ runs only from $0.192$ to $0.250$ — Tycho sits essentially at the maximum of (8.2), so the azimuthal spread in $m$ changes nothing.
  • $R_{\rm cd}/R_{\rm BW}$. $0.77$, the one-dimensional contact discontinuity of pure hydrodynamics, read by Warren et al. from Wang & Chevalier's Figure 1.

Putting those into (8.1):

$$ \frac{h}{R_{\rm BW}} \;=\; 0.038 \times 1 \times 0.2496 \times 0.77 \;=\; 0.0073, \qquad\text{0.73 per cent of the blast-wave radius,} $$

8.3 What is refuted, and what is not

Refuted: the transfer of a plane-interface, constant-acceleration, two-uniform-fluid, forgotten-seed mixing law to a decelerating supernova shell. Not refuted: the law itself, which Module 7 checked in the regime in which it was measured and which is not under test here.

Three reasons the transfer fails, in order of size, and the module claims no more precision for them than it has:

  1. $\alpha_B$ is the bubble coefficient. The observed quantity is a spike penetration — ejecta reaching outward into lighter gas — and at Atwood number near 1 spikes outrun bubbles, because a dense spike falling through a light fluid feels little drag while a light bubble rising through a dense one feels a great deal. The direction is certain. No number for $\alpha_{\rm spike}$ is printed anywhere in this module, because no paper this book holds gives one. That makes the $0.0073$ a lower bound and the factor 11 an upper bound on the agreement.
  2. The seed is not forgotten. The mixing law assumes the initial conditions are lost after about three merger generations — Module 7 quotes the assumption in the experimenters' own words. At a remnant's contact discontinuity the reverse shock feeds the interface continuously, and Wang & Chevalier find that reproducing Tycho's observed protrusions at all requires ejecta clumps with a density contrast of at least 100 over their surroundings. A structure that needs a seed of contrast 100 is not a structure that has forgotten its seed.
  3. $g$ is not constant. $g = m(1-m)R/t^2$ falls as $t^{m-2}$, and $\iint g\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}t$ taken from $t = 0$ does not converge, so the coefficient in (8.1) depends on when the deceleration began. (8.1) is therefore a scaling, good to a factor of a few, and not a theorem applied. It is quoted that way here, which is why a factor 11 counts as a refutation and a factor 2 would not have.
One caveat that pushes the same way, and must be read before the 0.93 is used for anything. Warren et al. conclude that the measured ratio $0.93$ is "inconsistent with adiabatic hydrodynamical models, even when Rayleigh–Taylor instabilities are taken into account", and they explain the unexpectedly small gap by cosmic-ray acceleration at the forward shock, which raises the compression factor above 4 and pulls the blast wave inward towards the contact discontinuity. So part of the observed 0.16 is not Rayleigh–Taylor mixing at all. Nothing in Check 7 rests on the 0.93: taking their reading at face value and using only the pure-hydrodynamic simulation still leaves a factor 11.
Warren's radii carry two further systematics, both printed here because they are of the size of the quantity being discussed. Projection: a structured shell seen in projection favours its protruding parts, and their simulations put the bias at about 3 per cent for the blast wave and about 6 per cent for the contact discontinuity — which is why the projected ratio $241''/251'' = 0.9602$ becomes $0.93$ after deprojection. Threshold: their contact-discontinuity radius moves from $0.94$ to $0.98$ of $R_{\rm BW}$ as the detection threshold goes from 2 to 4 standard deviations.

What this section does not use, and why. Warren's strongest single azimuthal mode of the contact discontinuity, $n = 6$, has an amplitude of 3.3 per cent of the mean radius — a tempting comparator, and the right order of magnitude to make the prediction look better. It is refused, because the same sentence records that "the BW shows nearly the same value", and the blast wave is not Rayleigh–Taylor unstable. A number that both surfaces share cannot be measuring an instability that only one of them has. What is specific to the contact discontinuity is the high-wavenumber power, where it carries nearly an order of magnitude more than the blast wave, and its spectral slope, $P \propto k^{-1.5}$ against $k^{-2.2}$. Those are qualitative, and they are quoted as qualitative.

This changes Module 7. Module 7 §10 records that its Rayleigh–Taylor half has no astronomical check and that Modules 1–10 do not repay it. That sentence is superseded by this section, and Module 7 has been edited to point here — and to say that the check, once made, refuted the transfer rather than confirming the law.

9. What this model captures, and what it misses

Every idealisation used above is named here, with the module that repays it where one exists and a plain statement of "not repaid in this book" where none does.

  • Zero thickness. The layer has structure — viscous stress, heat conduction, ionisation — and none of it appears in Proposition 1. Problem D2 bounds the error in air at eight orders of magnitude and finds the opposite verdict at the termination shock, where the layer is $10^5$–$10^6$ times thinner than a collisional mean free path. Not repaid: this book never solves the internal structure of a shock.
  • One fluid, one temperature. Refuted in §6.2 by the measurement itself. The electrons and the ions need not share a temperature, and at the termination shock a third population — pickup ions — takes 80 per cent of the energy. Module 1 supplies the criterion that says when this is allowed to happen; no module in this book carries a two-fluid or three-fluid shock.
  • Constant $\gamma$ across the front. Broken at Trinity, by Taylor's own account and in the direction his own argument predicts. Not repaid: a variable-$\gamma$ equation of state is beyond this book.
  • No magnetic field. Proposition 1 has no field, and §6.3 refuses a comparison for exactly that reason. Module 12 builds magnetohydrodynamics but stops short of oblique shock jump conditions, so this one is not repaid either, and the refusal in §6.3 is permanent rather than provisional.
  • A point explosion in a uniform, pressureless medium. Every word of that is idealisation. Finite source size and residual energy in radiation show up at $t = 0.10$ ms at Trinity; the neglected ambient pressure shows up after 5 ms; and the uniform medium is the assumption a circumstellar wind would break, which Tang & Chevalier treat with a density profile $\rho \propto r^{-s}$ that this module does not carry.
  • No radiative losses during the Sedov phase. That is precisely the assumption whose failure defines the end of the phase, in Proposition 8, and it is handled by bracketing rather than by correction.
  • A stated cooling function. (7.2) is two power laws, not a computed collisional-ionisation-equilibrium curve, and §7.1 shows the consequence: the transition radius is right to 57 per cent and the transition time to a factor 3.1.
  • Spherical symmetry. Dropped explicitly in §8, where the interface is unstable and the fingers are the subject. Everywhere else in the module it is assumed and never tested.
  • The mixing law transferred from a plane interface. Refuted in §8, by a factor 11, with the three reasons stated and no claim to have found the right law.

One thing this module does not miss is worth recording, because it is unusual. The check in §5 is against a table that no model touched, and the check in §6 is against an instrument that flew through the object. Neither has the circularity that Module 3 had to declare. What they have instead is a shortage of quoted uncertainties, and that is why §5.3 leans on Selby's $\sigma$ — the only honest standard deviation in the whole Trinity comparison.

C1. A supernova blast wave moves at $v_s = 5000\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Find the post-shock temperature from (7.1) for $\mu = 0.61$, $\mu = 1.00$ and $\mu = 0.50$, and state what the spread means for any quoted remnant temperature.
Solution

(7.1) at $\gamma = 5/3$ is $T_2 = 3\mu m_u v_s^2/(16k_B)$, so $T_2 \propto \mu$ exactly. With $v_s = 5\times10^8\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$: $\mu = 0.61$ gives $3.439\times10^8$ K, $\mu = 1.00$ gives $5.638\times10^8$ K, $\mu = 0.50$ gives $2.819\times10^8$ K.

The three differ by a factor 2.0 across the range of $\mu$ that a real remnant might have — ionised cosmic abundance, neutral atomic hydrogen, fully ionised pure hydrogen. A post-shock temperature quoted without its $\mu$ is therefore uncertain by a factor 2 before any measurement error, which is larger than most of the observational uncertainties it would be compared against.

C2. Earth's bow shock stands in a solar wind of $v = 400\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, $n = 5\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $T = 10^5$ K, at $M_1 = 8$. Find all four ratios of Proposition 2 and the downstream density. Then compute $T_2$ twice — once from the exact ratio, once from the strong-shock formula (7.1) — and explain the difference.
Solution

At $M_1 = 8$, $\gamma = 5/3$: $\rho_2/\rho_1 = 3.8209$, which is 95.5 per cent of the ceiling 4; $p_2/p_1 = 79.75$; $T_2/T_1 = 20.87$; $M_2 = 0.4583$. The downstream density is $n_2 = 3.8209\times5 = 19.10\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$.

By the exact ratio, $T_2 = 20.87\times10^5 = 2.087\times10^6$ K. By (7.1) with $v_s = 4\times10^7\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$ and $\mu = 0.61$, $T_2 = 2.201\times10^6$ K. The two differ by 5 per cent because $M_1 = 8$ is not infinite: (7.1) drops the $-(\gamma-1)$ in the numerator of $p_2/p_1$ and the $+2$ in the denominator of $\rho_2/\rho_1$, and at $M_1 = 8$ those terms are not yet negligible. The strong-shock form is an asymptote, and D1 asks where it becomes safe.

Note also $\Delta s/k_B = 3.2171$ from (3.4) — the entropy jump is of order the Boltzmann constant per particle, which is what "irreversible" means quantitatively here.

C3. Compute the entropy jump at $M_1 = 3$ for both $\gamma = 5/3$ and $\gamma = 7/5$. Then evaluate the same formulae at $M_1 = 0.9$, $0.7$ and $0.5$, and give the two separate reasons a rarefaction shock does not exist.
Solution

At $M_1 = 3$: $\gamma = 5/3$ gives $\rho_2/\rho_1 = 3.0000$, $p_2/p_1 = 11.0000$ and $\Delta s/k_B = 0.8503$; $\gamma = 7/5$ gives $3.8571$, $10.3333$ and $1.1137$.

Below $M_1 = 1$ the conservation laws still return solutions: $M_1 = 0.9$ gives $\rho_2/\rho_1 = 0.8504$, $p_2/p_1 = 0.7625$, $\Delta s/k_B = -0.00159$; $M_1 = 0.7$ gives $0.5616$, $0.3625$, $-0.07970$; $M_1 = 0.5$ gives $0.3077$, $0.0625$, $-1.21225$.

Reason one, thermodynamic. Every one of those entropy jumps is negative, so every one is forbidden by the second law. This applies on the whole range $M_1 \lt 1$.

Reason two, and it is not thermodynamic. Below $M_1 = \sqrt{(\gamma-1)/2\gamma} = 0.4472$ for $\gamma = 5/3$, the formula for $p_2/p_1$ returns a negative pressure — $-0.0500$ at $M_1 = 0.4$. On that range the rarefaction shock is not a solution the second law rejects; it is not a solution at all. The two reasons cover different ranges and must not be merged.

D1. How strong is "strong"? Find, for each of $\gamma = 5/3$ and $\gamma = 7/5$, the upstream Mach number at which $\rho_2/\rho_1$ reaches 99 per cent of its ceiling (3.3). Then check whether the Trinity shock at $t = 1$ ms qualifies.
Solution

Solve $\rho_2/\rho_1 = 0.99(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$ for $M_1$ using the first of (3.1). For $\gamma = 5/3$: $M_1 = 17.2$, where the ratio is $3.9600$ against the ceiling $4.0000$. For $\gamma = 7/5$: $M_1 = 22.2$, where it is $5.9400$ against $6.0000$. The diatomic gas needs a stronger shock because its ceiling is further away in ratio terms.

Trinity at 1 ms: from Taylor's table $R \approx 38\ \mathrm{m}$, so $v_s = \tfrac25R/t = 14.4\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. The ambient sound speed in air at one atmosphere with Taylor's $\rho_0$ is $0.337\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, giving $M_1 = 43$. Strong by any measure, and comfortably past the 22.2 that $\gamma = 7/5$ requires.

D2. An ideal-gas shock has no thickness at all in the equations of Module 2, because those equations contain no length. Estimate the real thickness two ways — in sea-level air, and at the solar-wind termination shock — and say what each answer means for Definition 1.
Solution

Air. Adding viscosity gives the layer a width of a few mean free paths. In sea-level air $\lambda_{\rm mfp} \sim 6.5\times10^{-6}$ cm, so the layer is $\sim 2\times10^{-5}$ cm. The Trinity fireball at 1 ms had a radius of 38 m $= 3.8\times10^3$ cm, so the layer is $2\times10^{8}$ times thinner than the object it bounds. Definition 1 is safe by eight orders of magnitude.

The termination shock. The measured layer widths were 100 000–300 000 km, described by the observers as "a few times the ion inertial length", so $10^{10}$–$3\times10^{10}$ cm. The collisional mean free path for proton–proton Coulomb collisions, $\lambda = (k_BT)^2/(\pi n e^4\ln\Lambda)$ with $n \approx 2\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $T \approx 10^5$ K read approximately off the published figure axes and $\ln\Lambda = 25$, is $2.28\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{cm} = 1524$ AU.

The layer is therefore $8\times10^{5}$ to $2\times10^{6}$ times thinner than a mean free path. That is what "collisionless shock" means, and it is why the one-fluid closure fails there while the conservation laws do not: Proposition 1 needs only that fluxes balance, which holds whatever mediates the transition, but the assumption that one temperature describes all the ions needs collisions, and there are none.

D3. Using Proposition 6, find the radius inside which half the shocked mass lies, and describe the pressure in the interior. Do it for both values of $\gamma$.
Solution

Integrate $\lambda^2 g(\lambda)$ outward and find where it reaches half its total. For $\gamma = 5/3$ the mass median is at $r/R = 0.9392$; for $\gamma = 7/5$ at $r/R = 0.9581$. Nearly all the mass is in a thin shell just behind the front.

The interior is correspondingly empty. For $\gamma = 5/3$, $\rho/\rho_2$ is $0.3563$ at $r/R = 0.9$, $0.01531$ at $0.5$ and $3.4\times10^{-10}$ at $0.01$; for $\gamma = 7/5$, $0.2054$, $0.00172$ and $3.1\times10^{-16}$.

The pressure does the opposite: $p/p_2$ is $0.3098$ at $r/R = 0.5$ and $0.3062$ at $r/R = 0.01$ for $\gamma = 5/3$ — flat. That is what it must be. A region with almost no mass in it cannot support a pressure gradient, because there is nothing for the gradient to accelerate. The snowplough phase of §7 is the limit of this picture: a thin dense shell driven by a uniform interior pressure.
