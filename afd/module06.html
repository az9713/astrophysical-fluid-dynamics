Module 6 — Convection and Thermal Instability

Hydrostatic balance is not stability. A stratified fluid can fail in two independent ways: buoyancy can stop restoring a displaced parcel, which is convection, and cooling can run away under compression, which is thermal instability. Two checks against measured data: mixing-length theory against solar granulation, and the predicted two-phase pressure window against 89 measured lines of sight.

1. Two fluids that will not sit still

Module 3 solved the hydrostatic equation and built stars out of it. Nothing in that module asked whether the solutions it found would survive being disturbed. A stratified fluid in hydrostatic balance has every force balanced at every point, and that is a statement about the fluid at rest; it says nothing about what happens to a parcel of it that is nudged. Balance is not stability.

This module asks the stability question twice, of two different restoring mechanisms, and gets two different instabilities.

The first is buoyancy. Displace a parcel upward a little and it expands to the lower ambient pressure. If it arrives denser than its new surroundings it sinks back and the layer is stable. If it arrives lighter it keeps rising, and the layer convects. §2 turns that sentence into an inequality between two logarithmic derivatives, one belonging to the star and one to the gas, and §3 turns the inequality into a frequency. §4 asks what a composition gradient does to it and §5 what partial ionisation does. §6 then asks the quantitative question the criterion cannot answer — how much heat a convecting layer carries — and §7 takes the answer to two measurements of the solar surface.

The second is cooling. Compress a parcel of interstellar gas a little, at the pressure of its surroundings. If the compression makes it radiate away energy faster than it gains it, the parcel cools, and at fixed pressure a cooler parcel is denser, so it contracts further. No gravity is involved at all. §8 derives Field's criterion for that runaway, §9 shows that it carves the interstellar medium into two stable phases separated by a narrow band of pressures, and §10 compares the predicted band with 89 measured sight lines. That comparison is the anchor of this module, and it confirms one prediction and refutes another from the same measurement.

A terrestrial case sets the difficulty of the first question immediately. The International Standard Atmosphere fixes the tropospheric lapse rate at $6.50\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$, against a dry adiabatic value $g/c_p = 9.761\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$ for dry air. The ratio is $0.6659$, which makes the standard troposphere a polytrope of index $n = 4.2558$, with $\nabla = 1/(n+1) = 0.19026$ against the diatomic adiabatic value $2/7 = 0.28571$. By the criterion of §2 the troposphere is stable, with $33$ per cent to spare. It nevertheless convects every afternoon. The resolution is that air is not dry: a saturated parcel releases about $2.5\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}}$ of latent heat as it rises and condenses, which warms it on the way up and lowers its effective lapse rate to $5$–$6\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$, below the ambient $6.5$. Moist convection is unstable where dry convection is not. Problem C1 works this through.

The Sun has no latent-heat term of that kind, so the solar criterion is the clean one, and it is derived first.

2. The displaced parcel and the Schwarzschild criterion

The whole of this section follows from the Euler equation of Module 2 and one assumption about the parcel. No transport theory is needed, and none is used.

Definition 1 (the two logarithmic gradients). For a stratified fluid in hydrostatic balance, the stratification gradient is the logarithmic temperature gradient of the background, taken along the radius, $$ \nabla \;\equiv\; \frac{d\ln T}{d\ln P}, \tag{2.1}$$ which is Module 3, Definition 5. The adiabatic gradient is the corresponding derivative for a parcel of the gas at fixed specific entropy $s$, $$ \nabla_{\rm ad} \;\equiv\; \left(\frac{\partial\ln T}{\partial\ln P}\right)_{\!s}. \tag{2.2}$$ The first is a property of the star; the second is a property of the gas. Fig. 1 sets out the comparison the criterion will make. Notation. In this module the symbol $\nabla$ with no argument always means (2.1). The vector operator is written only with an argument attached, as in $\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{u}$ or $\nabla\Phi$.
Proposition 1 (the Schwarzschild criterion). Consider a plane-parallel stratification of an ideal gas in hydrostatic balance, with (i) uniform chemical composition, so that the mean molecular weight $\mu$ is the same in the parcel and its surroundings, and (ii) $\rho = \mu m_u P/(k_BT)$. Displace a parcel from the level $r$ by $\delta r$ with $|\delta r| \ll H$, where $H \equiv P/(\rho g)$ is the pressure scale height, subject to (iii) instantaneous pressure balance with the surroundings, and (iv) adiabatic change of state. Then to first order in $\delta r$ the density contrast the parcel acquires is $$ \frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho} \;=\; -\,(\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad})\,\frac{\delta r}{H}, \tag{2.3}$$ and the buoyancy force per unit mass on it is $+g\,(\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad})\,\delta r/H$, directed along $\delta r$. The force therefore restores the parcel to its starting level if and only if $$ \nabla \;\lt \; \nabla_{\rm ad}. \tag{2.4}$$

Write $\Delta$ for the difference between the parcel and its surroundings at the same height, and $\delta$ for the change along the displacement. Both the parcel and the surroundings are ideal gases of the same $\mu$ by (i) and (ii), so $\ln\rho = \ln P - \ln T + \mathrm{const}$ for each, and

$$ \delta\ln\rho \;=\; \delta\ln P - \delta\ln T .$$

By (iii) the parcel's pressure is the ambient pressure at every instant, so $\delta\ln P$ is the same number for both. By (iv) the parcel's temperature follows its own adiabat, $\delta\ln T_{\rm p} = \nabla_{\rm ad}\,\delta\ln P$, while the surroundings supply $\delta\ln T = \nabla\,\delta\ln P$ by (2.1). Subtracting,

$$ \frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho} \;=\; \delta\ln\rho_{\rm p} - \delta\ln\rho \;=\; -(\nabla_{\rm ad}-\nabla)\,\delta\ln P \;=\;(\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad})\,\delta\ln P .$$

Hydrostatic balance gives $dP/dr = -\rho g$, so $d\ln P/dr = -\rho g/P = -1/H$ and $\delta\ln P = -\delta r/H$ to first order. Substituting gives (2.3).

Archimedes' principle in the Euler equation is the statement that a parcel of density $\rho+\Delta\rho$ in surroundings of density $\rho$ feels a net force per unit mass $-g\,\Delta\rho/\rho$ in the direction of increasing $r$. With (2.3) that force is $+g(\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad})\delta r/H$. Since $g$, $H$ and $\rho$ are positive, the force has the sign of $(\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad})\,\delta r$: it points along $\delta r$, and so amplifies the displacement, when $\nabla \gt \nabla_{\rm ad}$, and against $\delta r$, restoring it, when $\nabla \lt \nabla_{\rm ad}$. ∎

Four premises were used and each will be paid for later. Premise (i), uniform composition, is lifted in §4. Premise (iii), instantaneous pressure balance, requires the parcel to move slowly compared with the sound speed, and §6 finds $v_c/c_s = 0.4271$ at the solar photosphere, where the premise is therefore spent. Premise (iv), adiabatic change, requires the parcel to lose no appreciable heat while it travels, and §6 and §7 find that at the photosphere radiation carries $98.7$ per cent of the flux, so it is spent there too. Only premise (ii) survives everywhere this module uses the criterion.

The criterion has one immediate consequence. For an ideal gas with a constant ratio of specific heats $\gamma$, an adiabatic change obeys $P\propto\rho^\gamma$ and the ideal gas law gives $P\propto\rho T$, so $T\propto P^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}$ and

$$ \nabla_{\rm ad} \;=\; \frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}, \tag{2.5}$$

which is $2/5 = 0.400000$ for a monatomic gas and $2/7 = 0.285714$ for a diatomic one. These are the two numbers §3 and Problem C1 compare against. Everything later in the module either changes the left-hand side of (2.4), which §3 does by reading $\nabla$ off a solar model, or the right-hand side, which §5 does by breaking the constancy of $\gamma$.

3. The buoyancy frequency, and where the Sun's convection zone starts

Proposition 1 gives a sign. Its magnitude is a frequency, and the frequency is a measurable property of a star.

Proposition 2 (the Brunt–Väisälä frequency). Under the hypotheses of Proposition 1, and neglecting any drag on the parcel, the displacement obeys $$ \ddot{\delta r} \;=\; -N^2\,\delta r, \qquad N^2 \;\equiv\; \frac{g}{H}\,(\nabla_{\rm ad}-\nabla). \tag{3.1}$$ The layer is stable if and only if $N^2\gt 0$, and a displaced parcel then oscillates about its starting level with angular frequency $N$.

The parcel has unit mass and the force per unit mass computed in Proposition 1 is $+g(\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad})\delta r/H$. Newton's second law gives $\ddot{\delta r} = g(\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad})\delta r/H = -(g/H)(\nabla_{\rm ad}-\nabla)\,\delta r$, which is (3.1). When $N^2\gt 0$ this is the equation of a harmonic oscillator of angular frequency $N$; when $N^2\lt 0$ its general solution is $A e^{|N|t}+Be^{-|N|t}$ and the growing branch is convection. The coefficients $g$, $H$, $\nabla$ and $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ are evaluated at the starting level and held fixed, which is the same first-order approximation used in Proposition 1. ∎

Equation (3.1) is the Schwarzschild criterion again, with a rate attached. It also identifies what the criterion costs when it is close: $N^2$ is a small difference of two numbers of order $0.4$, so a model table has to resolve $\nabla$ well to give $N^2$ at all inside a convection zone. That limit is stated below and respected.

3.1 Reading the criterion off a solar model

The stratification is the tabulated standard solar model BS2005-AGS,OP of Bahcall, Serenelli & Basu (2005), the same table Module 3 used. From its columns $\nabla$ is a sliding least-squares slope of $\ln T$ against $\ln P$ over $\pm25$ rows, $g = Gm(r)/r^2$ from the tabulated mass fraction, and $H = P/(\rho g)$.

The table is an input, not a checked quantity. It is used here only as a stratification: a set of $(P,\rho,T,m)$ values to differentiate. Module 3 measured its adiabatic plateau, $\nabla = 0.3957$ over $0.80$–$0.92\,R$, and located the base of its convection zone at $0.7269\,R$; this module's script recovers both numbers with the same locator. That agreement demonstrates that the two scripts read the table identically. It is not a check, because both numbers come from the same file.

3.2 Why the convection zone reaches down to $0.727\,R$

Read the $\nabla$ column of Table 1 outward, from the centre to the surface. It falls through $0.2116$ at $0.3016\,R$ to a shallow minimum — $0.1968$ at $0.4002\,R$ and $0.1962$ at $0.5018\,R$ — and then rises: $0.2074$ at $0.5979\,R$, $0.2616$ at $0.6804\,R$, $0.3259$ at $0.7099\,R$, and $0.3963$ at $0.7500\,R$. Across the whole interval $0.4002\,R$ to $0.7500\,R$, $\nabla$ rises by a factor $2.014$. The interval is not monotone, and the sampling matters: the rows printed above dip to $0.1962$ at $0.5018\,R$, while the full profile reaches its minimum of $0.1937$ at $0.4531\,R$, a radius Table 1 does not sample. Measured from that minimum the factor is $2.046$. Either way the rise carries $\nabla$ straight across the flat adiabatic plateau $0.3957$. The base of the convection zone is where the crossing happens, at $0.7269\,R$, which is $T = 2.02\times10^{6}$ K in the same table.

It is worth being exact about which side moves. In this star the convection zone opens because $\nabla$ rises to meet $\nabla_{\rm ad}$, not because $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ falls to meet $\nabla$: over the whole radiative interior $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ is the flat plateau value. A radiative layer carries its flux with $\nabla = \nabla_{\rm rad}\propto \kappa L P/(m\,T^4)$, so a rising opacity $\kappa$ and a falling temperature both push $\nabla$ up, and both are happening here. This module computes neither. It reads the rise off the model and does not apportion it between opacity and temperature; doing so would need the opacity tables the model was built with, which are not part of this module. §5 shows the other mechanism, the one that moves $\nabla_{\rm ad}$, and shows that it operates somewhere else entirely.

Honest limit on the sign of $N^2$ inside the convection zone. At $r = 0.90\,R$ the table gives $\nabla = 0.394599$ against the plateau $0.395673$, a difference of $-1.07\times10^{-3}$. §6 shows that the true superadiabatic excess there is about $1.9\times10^{-6}$, three orders of magnitude smaller. The table quotes $P$, $\rho$ and $T$ to four significant figures and cannot resolve a difference that small. So $N^2$ in the convection zone is reported here as zero to within the table's precision, and the "imaginary" entries of Table 1 are not a measurement of a negative $N^2$. The radiative-interior maximum is robust by contrast, because there $\nabla_{\rm ad}-\nabla$ is of order $0.1$.

4. Ledoux: what a composition gradient does

Premise (i) of Proposition 1 was uniform composition. The Sun has burned hydrogen in its core for $4.6$ Gyr, so its $\mu$ is not uniform, and the premise has to be paid for.

Proposition 3 (the Ledoux criterion). Keep every hypothesis of Proposition 1 except (i), and let the composition of the surroundings vary with depth, with $\nabla_\mu \equiv d\ln\mu/d\ln P$, while the displaced parcel carries its own composition unchanged. Then (2.3) becomes $$ \frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho} \;=\; -\left[\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad}-\nabla_\mu\right]\frac{\Delta r}{H}, $$ the buoyancy frequency becomes $$ N^2 \;=\; \frac{g}{H}\left[\nabla_{\rm ad}-\nabla+\nabla_\mu\right], \tag{4.1}$$ and the layer is stable if and only if $\nabla \lt \nabla_{\rm ad} + \nabla_\mu$. Here $\Delta r$ is the displacement written $\delta r$ in Proposition 1; it is renamed for this proof only, so that no symbol has to serve both as a variation and as a coefficient.

For an ideal gas $\rho = \mu m_uP/(k_BT)$, so along the displacement $\Delta\ln\rho = \Delta\ln P - \Delta\ln T + \Delta\ln\mu$ for the surroundings, and the same without the last term for the parcel, which carries its composition unchanged. The parcel and its surroundings share $\Delta\ln P$ by premise (iii), so those terms cancel in the difference exactly as in Proposition 1. The temperature terms give $-(\nabla_{\rm ad}-\nabla)\Delta\ln P$ as before, and the composition term gives $-\nabla_\mu\,\Delta\ln P$. Subtracting the surroundings from the parcel,

$$ \frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho} = \left[(\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad}) - \nabla_\mu\right]\Delta\ln P,$$

and $\Delta\ln P = -\Delta r/H$ as before gives the displayed contrast. Newton's second law with the buoyancy force $-g\Delta\rho/\rho$ then gives (4.1), and stability is $N^2\gt 0$, which is the stated inequality. Setting $\nabla_\mu=0$ recovers (3.1). ∎

For a general equation of state the two composition coefficients $\delta \equiv -(\partial\ln\rho/\partial\ln T)_{P,\mu}$ and $\varphi \equiv (\partial\ln\rho/\partial\ln\mu)_{P,T}$ do not both equal $1$, and (4.1) carries a factor $\delta$ outside the bracket and $\varphi/\delta$ on $\nabla_\mu$ (Kippenhahn & Weigert, section 6.1). Every gas this module treats is an ideal gas, so that generality is quoted and not used.

The sign is the physical content. A $\mu$ that increases inward means $\nabla_\mu \gt 0$, since $P$ also increases inward; a parcel carried up from such a layer keeps its heavy composition and arrives denser than its new surroundings, which stabilises the stratification even where $\nabla \gt \nabla_{\rm ad}$.

Table 1's $\nabla_\mu$ column shows the Sun's. The $\mu$ used there is the table's own, $\mu = \rho k_BT/(P\,m_u)$, so no ionisation assumption enters. The share of $N^2$ contributed by the Ledoux term is $+84.05$ per cent at $0.050\,R$ ($\mu = 0.77684$, $\nabla_\mu = +0.37196$), $+74.96$ per cent at $0.100\,R$ ($\mu = 0.69264$, $\nabla_\mu = +0.22043$), $+50.74$ per cent at $0.150\,R$, $+16.53$ per cent at $0.200\,R$, $+3.07$ per cent at $0.251\,R$, $+1.37$ per cent at $0.302\,R$, $+0.67$ per cent at $0.400\,R$ and $+0.87$ per cent at $0.598\,R$. The gradient left by hydrogen burning is large where it exists and dies away outside the burning core.

Does the Ledoux term move any boundary in the Sun? No, and the measured size of the "no" is worth printing. The Ledoux term matters only where the two criteria could disagree, which is where $\nabla$ is close to $\nabla_{\rm ad}$. Where $\nabla_\mu$ is large, inside $0.2\,R$, the Schwarzschild margin $\nabla_{\rm ad}-\nabla$ runs from $0.0706$ at $0.050\,R$ to $0.1328$ at $0.200\,R$, so those layers are stable by a wide margin under either criterion. Through the radiative zone outside the burning core, over $0.30$–$0.60\,R$, $|\nabla_\mu| \le 0.00256$. Locating the base of the convection zone again with $\nabla_{\rm ad}+\nabla_\mu$ in place of $\nabla_{\rm ad}$, using the same locator, moves it from $0.7269\,R$ to $0.7306\,R$: a shift of $0.0037\,R$, or $0.37$ per cent of the solar radius. And that is an upper bound, because the $\nabla_\mu$ the table gives near the base is partly the effective-$\mu$ artefact described next.
One caution on the outer rows of Table 1, and it is not a footnote. Beyond about $0.72\,R$ the $\mu$ printed there is an effective $\mu = \rho k_BT/(P\,m_u)$, which absorbs the changing ionisation state of hydrogen and helium as well as the composition. Its gradient in those rows is an ionisation effect, not a composition gradient, and it must not be fed into Proposition 3: the parcel does not carry its ionisation state unchanged, it re-ionises. The percentages quoted above therefore stop at $0.60\,R$.

The case where the choice of criterion does matter is a star with a receding convective core, which leaves a large $\nabla_\mu$ and a near-neutral layer in the same place. Such a layer is Schwarzschild-unstable and Ledoux-stable at once, and mixes on a thermal rather than a dynamical timescale — semiconvection. Problem D2 puts numbers on it and shows why the Sun escapes the ambiguity. This module does not develop semiconvection further.

The right-hand side of (2.4) has been treated so far as the constant $0.4$. It is not a constant.

5. The collapse of $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ in a partially ionised gas

Equation (2.5) assumed a constant $\gamma$. In a gas that is partly ionised, some of the energy a compressed parcel receives goes into pulling electrons off atoms instead of into raising the temperature, and the parcel therefore heats less for the same compression. That lowers $\nabla_{\rm ad}$, and it lowers it a long way.

Definition 2 (Saha equilibrium for pure hydrogen). Let $x \equiv n_{\rm II}/n_{\rm H}$ be the ionisation fraction, with $n_{\rm H}$ the total density of hydrogen nuclei, so that $n_e = n_{\rm II} = x\,n_{\rm H}$ and $n_{\rm I} = (1-x)\,n_{\rm H}$. Saha's equation with the statistical weight ratio $g_{\rm II}/g_{\rm I} = 1/2$ for hydrogen is $$ \frac{n_e\,n_{\rm II}}{n_{\rm I}} \;=\; \left(\frac{2\pi m_ek_BT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\chi_{\rm H}}{k_BT}\right) \;\equiv\; A(T), \tag{5.1}$$ with $\chi_{\rm H} = 13.5984340$ eV the ionisation energy of hydrogen.

The total particle density is $n_{\rm H}(1+x)$, so the gas pressure is $P = n_{\rm H}(1+x)k_BT$. Eliminating $n_{\rm H}$ between this and (5.1) gives a closed form with no iteration:

$$ \frac{x^2}{1-x^2} \;=\; \frac{A(T)\,k_BT}{P} \;\equiv\; B(T,P), \qquad x \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{B}{1+B}}. \tag{5.2}$$

For a partially ionised pure-hydrogen gas, the first law with the ionisation energy included in the internal energy gives $$ \nabla_{\rm ad} \;=\; \frac{2+x(1-x)\Phi}{5+x(1-x)\Phi^2}, \qquad \Phi \;\equiv\; \frac{5}{2}+\frac{\chi_{\rm H}}{k_BT}. \tag{5.3}$$

(5.3) is quoted from Kippenhahn & Weigert, section 14.3, and is not derived in this module. It is the only result in Module 6 taken on authority rather than proved. Its two limits are checkable without the derivation, and Proposition 4 checks them: at $x=0$ and $x=1$ it must return the monatomic $2/5$ of (2.5), and it does.
Proposition 4 (the ionisation zone lowers $\nabla_{\rm ad}$). Assume (5.3), with $x$ from (5.2) and $\chi_{\rm H} \gt 0$. Then $\nabla_{\rm ad} \lt 2/5$ at every $0\lt x\lt 1$, and $\nabla_{\rm ad} = 2/5$ exactly at $x=0$ and $x=1$.

Write $q \equiv x(1-x)$, which is positive for $0\lt x\lt 1$ and zero at both ends. Comparing (5.3) with $2/5$ by cross-multiplication, and noting that both denominators are positive,

$$ \nabla_{\rm ad} \lt \tfrac25 \iff 5\,(2+q\Phi) \lt 2\,(5+q\Phi^2) \iff 5q\Phi \lt 2q\Phi^2 \iff q\,\Phi\,(2\Phi-5) \gt 0 .$$

Now $\Phi = 5/2 + \chi_{\rm H}/(k_BT) \gt 5/2$ for every finite $T$, so $2\Phi-5 \gt 0$ and $\Phi\gt 0$. The inequality therefore holds exactly when $q\gt 0$, that is for $0\lt x\lt 1$. At $x=0$ or $x=1$, $q=0$ and (5.3) returns $2/5$, the monatomic value of (2.5). ∎

The size of the effect is set by $\Phi$, and $\Phi$ is large. At the temperature where the depression is deepest on the photospheric isobar, $T = 12266$ K, $\chi_{\rm H}/k_BT = 12.86$ and so $\Phi = 15.36$; at the hotter temperature where the gas is half ionised, $T = 13136$ K, it is $12.01$. Either way the ionisation reservoir a parcel can pour heat into is about twelve times the thermal reservoir of one particle, which is why the depression is not a small correction. 5.1 What this explains, and what it does not

It would be natural to say that the collapse of $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ is what makes the outer part of the Sun convective. The model table says otherwise, and the numbers are unambiguous.

  • The base of the convection zone is at $0.7269\,R$, where the table gives $T = 2.02\times10^{6}$ K. At that temperature hydrogen and helium are fully ionised, $x=1$, and (5.3) returns $\nabla_{\rm ad}=2/5$. The measured plateau, $0.3957$, is the evidence: it is the ideal value to within the model's equation-of-state corrections, right across the convection zone.
  • The collapse of Table 2 sits between about $1.06\times10^{4}$ and $1.68\times10^{4}$ K, the band Problem K1 maps.
  • The outermost row of the table, $0.98308\,R$, is already at $T = 8.12\times10^{4}$ K — above the whole collapse band.

So the hydrogen ionisation zone lies outside $0.983\,R$, in the outermost $1.7$ per cent of the radius. It does not open the convection zone, whose outer $27.3$ per cent by radius, from $0.7269\,R$ to the surface, is set the other way round by $\nabla$ rising, as §3.2 showed. What Proposition 4 explains is why convection reaches all the way up to the photosphere instead of stopping below it, and why the strongly superadiabatic layer of §6 sits where it does.

Two mechanisms, two depths. $\nabla$ rising to meet a flat $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ sets the base of the solar convection zone at $0.7269\,R$. $\nabla_{\rm ad}$ collapsing by the factor $4.23$ in the partial-ionisation zone sets its top, above $0.983\,R$, where $\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad}$ runs from $1.9\times10^{-6}$ to $0.61$.

The criterion has now been derived, corrected for composition, and corrected for ionisation. It still says only whether convection happens. It says nothing about how much heat it carries.

6. Mixing-length theory, and how superadiabatic the Sun must be

A stability criterion cannot give a flux, because it contains no statement about how far a parcel travels before it merges with its surroundings. Mixing-length theory supplies exactly one such statement and derives everything else from it.

Definition 3 (the mixing length). A convecting parcel is assumed to rise a distance $\ell$, keeping its identity, and then to dissolve into its surroundings and deliver its excess heat. The mixing length is taken proportional to the local pressure scale height, $$ \ell \;\equiv\; \alpha\,H, \qquad H = \frac{P}{\rho g}, \tag{6.1}$$ with $\alpha$ a dimensionless parameter of order unity. This module uses $\alpha = 2.0$.
Notation. Within this module $\alpha$ is the mixing-length parameter and nothing else. Module 5's $\alpha$, the length scale of the isothermal sphere, is not meant here.
$\alpha$ is not derived. It is calibrated: a value near $2$ is what makes a solar evolution model reach the observed solar radius at the solar age. Every prediction in §7 therefore rests on a parameter that was fitted to the Sun, and the checks say so where they are made. Problem C3 measures how much freedom that leaves.
Proposition 5 (mixing-length flux and velocity, efficient limit). Let a layer have $\nabla \gt \nabla_{\rm ad}$ and write the superadiabatic excess $\Delta\nabla \equiv \nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad}$. Assume the parcel loses no heat while it travels, so that it follows its own adiabat over the whole mixing length, and take the ideal-gas value $\delta = 1$. Adopt the two standard averaging conventions: the parcel's mean displacement over its life is $\ell/2$, and half the buoyancy work done over that displacement becomes the parcel's kinetic energy. Then the mean temperature excess a parcel carries, its velocity, and the convective flux are $$ \delta T \;=\; T\,\Delta\nabla\,\frac{\ell}{2H}, \qquad v_c \;=\; \frac{\ell}{H}\sqrt{\frac{gH\,\Delta\nabla}{8}}, \tag{6.2}$$ $$ F \;=\; \rho\,v_c\,c_P\,\delta T \;=\; \rho\,c_P\,T\,\alpha^2\sqrt{\frac{gH}{32}}\;\Delta\nabla^{3/2}. \tag{6.3}$$ Consequently, at fixed $F$, $\rho$, $c_P$, $T$, $g$ and $H$, $\Delta\nabla\propto\alpha^{-4/3}$ and $v_c\propto\alpha^{1/3}$.

Proposition 1 gives the temperature difference a parcel acquires after rising a distance $\Delta r$: subtracting the two logarithmic temperature changes at equal pressure, $\Delta T/T = \Delta\nabla\,\Delta r/H$. Averaging over the parcel's life, which is taken to be the mean displacement $\ell/2$, gives the first of (6.2).

For the velocity, the buoyancy force per unit mass at displacement $\Delta r$ is $-g\,\Delta\rho/\rho = g\,\Delta T/T = g\,\Delta\nabla\,\Delta r/H$, using $\Delta\rho/\rho = -\Delta T/T$ for an ideal gas at fixed pressure. The work done over the mean displacement is $$ W = \int_0^{\ell/2} \frac{g\,\Delta\nabla}{H}\,\Delta r\;d(\Delta r) = \frac{g\,\Delta\nabla\,\ell^2}{8H}. $$ Half of that work is assumed to go into the kinetic energy of the parcel and half into pushing the surroundings aside, so $\tfrac12 v_c^2 = \tfrac12 W$, that is $v_c^2 = g\Delta\nabla\,\ell^2/(8H)$, which is the second of (6.2).

The enthalpy a parcel carries per unit mass is $c_P\,\delta T$, and the mass flux carried upward by the convecting fluid is $\rho v_c$, so $F = \rho v_c c_P \delta T$. Substituting (6.2) and $\ell = \alpha H$, $$ F = \rho c_P\,T\,\Delta\nabla\frac{\alpha}{2}\cdot\alpha\sqrt{\frac{gH\Delta\nabla}{8}} = \rho c_P T\,\alpha^2\sqrt{\frac{gH}{32}}\,\Delta\nabla^{3/2},$$ since $\tfrac12\sqrt{1/8} = \sqrt{1/32}$. Holding $F$, $\rho$, $c_P$, $T$, $g$ and $H$ fixed and varying $\alpha$ in (6.3) gives $\Delta\nabla\propto\alpha^{-4/3}$, and putting that into (6.2) gives $v_c\propto\alpha\cdot\Delta\nabla^{1/2}\propto\alpha^{1/3}$. ∎

The factors $1/2$ in $\delta T$ and $1/8$ in $v_c^2$ are the two averaging conventions of the standard theory. A different convention would move both by a numerical factor, which is absorbed into a different calibrated $\alpha$; it changes no physics and no scaling. What the scalings say is that $\alpha$ is a weak handle on the velocity and a strong one on the excess, and Problem C3 exploits exactly that.

7. Check A: mixing-length theory against solar granulation

Mixing-length theory makes two predictions about the solar photosphere that can be compared with measurements taken there. Both measurements are of the quiet Sun at disk centre, and neither used a stellar model to produce the quantity compared.

7.1 A1: the size of a granule

The overturning eddy of Definition 3 is one mixing length across. Seen from above, a convection cell has upflow in the middle and downflow at its edges, one mixing length each way, so the natural prediction for the diameter of a granule is $2\ell$. With $H = 143$ km and $\alpha = 2$, that is $\ell = 286$ km and $2\ell = 572$ km.

Against the $3.451\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ of §6.2, the ratios are $5.3$ against the $0.65\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ upflow and $5.8$ against the $0.6\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ rms. Too fast by a factor $5.3$, against the factor $1.25$ the same theory returns in §7.3 once it is given the measured flux.

That assumption can be tested against the same two measurements, and the independent warning $v_c/c_s = 0.43$ already says the theory has been pushed past its own premise.

8. Thermal instability: the loss function and Field's criteria

The question is the same one Proposition 1 asked — a parcel is perturbed, does the perturbation grow? — with a different restoring mechanism. Compress a parcel of interstellar gas slightly at the pressure of its surroundings. Its density rises, so it radiates more; if it also loses the ability to be reheated fast enough, it cools, and at fixed pressure a cooler parcel is denser still. Nothing in this argument involves gravity, buoyancy or a stratification.

Definition 4 (Field's net loss function). Let $\mathcal{L}(\rho,T)$ be the energy losses minus the energy gains of the gas, per gram per second, exclusive of thermal conduction (Field 1965, p. 532). Thermal equilibrium is $\mathcal{L} = 0$.
Notation. The loss function is always written $\mathcal{L}$ in this module. The plain letter $L$ is the solar luminosity, as in §6. The cooling coefficient below is always written $\Lambda(T)$ with its argument shown, to keep it distinct from Module 1's Coulomb logarithm $\ln\Lambda$, which reappears in Problem K3.
Proposition 6 (Field's criteria, 1965, eqs. 4a and 4b). Perturb a parcel in thermal equilibrium by $\delta T$. Two idealised paths are compared. A parcel held at constant density runs away if and only if $$ \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!\rho} \lt 0 \qquad\text{(isochoric, Field 4a)}, \tag{8.1}$$ and a parcel that stays in mechanical equilibrium with its surroundings throughout — which requires the perturbation to evolve slowly compared with its own sound-crossing time, and so is perturbed at constant pressure — runs away if and only if $$ \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P} \;=\; \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!\rho} - \frac{\rho_0}{T_0}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\rho}\right)_{\!T} \;\lt \;0 \qquad\text{(isobaric, Field 4b)}. \tag{8.2}$$ If $(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\rho)_T \gt 0$, as it is for any cooling rate that increases with density, then (8.1) implies (8.2), and there exist states satisfying (8.2) but not (8.1): the isobaric criterion is the weaker condition, and is met over a wider range of states.

Let the gas sit at $(\rho_0,T_0)$ with $\mathcal{L}(\rho_0,T_0) = 0$, and perturb the temperature by $\delta T$. The energy equation gives $c\,\dot{\delta T} = -\delta\mathcal{L}$ for the appropriate specific heat $c\gt 0$, so the perturbation grows exactly when $\delta\mathcal{L}$ and $\delta T$ have opposite signs, that is when the derivative of $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to $T$ along the path of the perturbation is negative. At constant density that derivative is $(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial T)_\rho$, which is (8.1).

At constant pressure the density is not free: for an ideal gas of fixed composition $\rho \propto P/T$, so $(\partial\rho/\partial T)_P = -\rho_0/T_0$. The chain rule then gives $$ \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P} = \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!\rho} + \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\rho}\right)_{\!T}\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P} = \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!\rho} - \frac{\rho_0}{T_0}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\rho}\right)_{\!T},$$ which is (8.2). The subtracted term is positive when $(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\rho)_T\gt 0$, so $(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial T)_P \lt (\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial T)_\rho$, and a state satisfying (8.1) satisfies (8.2) while the converse fails whenever the two derivatives straddle zero. ∎

Which criterion applies is not a matter of taste. A parcel held at fixed density but given a different temperature is at the wrong pressure for its surroundings, and the force equation will not let it stay there: it expands or contracts on a sound-crossing time, which in the interstellar medium is far shorter than the cooling time of the warm gas. The isobaric criterion is the one compatible with the momentum equation, and that was Field's point in 1965 against Parker's earlier isochoric treatment. Because (8.2) is the weaker condition, far more of the interstellar medium is unstable than the isochoric criterion would suggest.

A form $(\partial(\mathcal{L}/T)/\partial T)_P \lt 0$ appears in some later texts. It coincides with (8.2) only on the equilibrium curve, where $\mathcal{L} = 0$. This module uses (8.2) as Field printed it.

8.1 The cooling function, tested before it is used

The interstellar gas of this section is heated by photoelectric emission from small grains and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons at a rate $\Gamma$ per hydrogen nucleus, taken independent of density and temperature, and cooled at a rate $n^2\Lambda(T)$ per unit volume. Koyama & Inutsuka (2002) fitted $\Lambda(T)$ over the range of the neutral medium with two terms: Lyman-$\alpha$ cooling, which switches on above about $8000$ K, and the [C II] $158\ \mu$m fine-structure line, whose $92$ K excitation temperature appears explicitly in the exponent and which is what cools the cold neutral medium.

Their equation (4) as printed contains two typographical errors, and the printed form misses the measured interstellar pressures by three orders of magnitude. As printed, with coefficient $14$ on the second term and $114800$ in the first exponent, the equilibrium relation of (8.3) below gives $P/k = 1.7924\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$ at $100$ K and $6.3632$ at $8000$ K. Corrected, the same relation gives $1792.4$ and $388.6$. Measured interstellar pressures are near $3802\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$ (§10). The printed form misses by three orders of magnitude.
The correction is not a guess. Vázquez-Semadeni et al. (2007) print the corrected equations (3) and (4), and their footnote 5 reads: "Note that eq. (4) in Koyama & Inutsuka (2002) contains two typographical errors. The form used here incorporates the necesary corrections, kindly provided by H. Koyama." The change $114800 \to 1.184\times10^{5}$ is a transposition of digits, since $114800 = 1.148\times10^{5}$, not a change of notation.

The corrected function, used everywhere below, is

$$ \frac{\Lambda(T)}{\Gamma} \;=\; 10^{7}\exp\!\left(\frac{-1.184\times10^{5}}{T+1000}\right) + 1.4\times10^{-2}\,\sqrt{T}\,\exp\!\left(\frac{-92}{T}\right)\ \mathrm{cm^{3}}, \qquad \Gamma = 2\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}. \tag{8.3}$$
Proposition 7 (the criterion for this heating law). Let the volumetric heating be $n\Gamma$ and the volumetric cooling $n^2\Lambda(T)$, with $\Gamma$ constant, $\Lambda(T)\gt 0$ and differentiable, and $n = \rho/m_{\rm H}$. Then thermal equilibrium is $$ n_{\rm eq}(T) \;=\; \frac{\Gamma}{\Lambda(T)}, \tag{8.4}$$ and Field's isobaric runaway condition (8.2) reduces at every equilibrium state to $d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T \lt 1$, so that the gas is stable against an isobaric perturbation exactly when $$ \frac{d\ln\Lambda}{d\ln T} \;\gt \; 1 \quad\text{for stability}. \tag{8.5}$$

Per gram, the losses are $n^2\Lambda/\rho = \rho\Lambda/m_{\rm H}^2$ and the gains are $n\Gamma/\rho = \Gamma/m_{\rm H}$, so $$ \mathcal{L}(\rho,T) = \frac{\rho\,\Lambda(T)}{m_{\rm H}^2} - \frac{\Gamma}{m_{\rm H}} .$$ Setting $\mathcal{L}=0$ gives $\rho = m_{\rm H}\Gamma/\Lambda(T)$, that is (8.4). Differentiating, $$ \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!\rho} = \frac{\rho}{m_{\rm H}^2}\frac{d\Lambda}{dT}, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\rho}\right)_{\!T} = \frac{\Lambda}{m_{\rm H}^2},$$ so (8.2) reads $$ \left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P} = \frac{\rho}{m_{\rm H}^2}\left[\frac{d\Lambda}{dT} - \frac{\Lambda}{T}\right] \lt 0 \iff \frac{T}{\Lambda}\frac{d\Lambda}{dT} \lt 1,$$ after dividing by the positive factor $\rho\Lambda/(m_{\rm H}^2T)$. The layer is therefore stable exactly when $d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T \gt 1$, which is (8.5). Note also that $(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\rho)_T = \Lambda/m_{\rm H}^2\gt 0$, so Proposition 6's ordering of the two criteria applies: the isochoric runaway condition here is $d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T \lt 0$, which is strictly harder to meet than $d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T \lt 1$, so every isochorically unstable state on this curve is isobarically unstable and not conversely. ∎

9. The two-phase window

Proposition 8 (the pressure window). On the thermal-equilibrium curve (8.4), assume further that $d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T - 1$ changes sign exactly twice on the range considered, which Table 4 verifies for (8.3). Then the equilibrium pressure satisfies $$ \frac{P}{k_B} \;=\; n_{\rm eq}(T)\,T \;=\; \frac{\Gamma\,T}{\Lambda(T)}, \qquad \frac{d\ln P}{d\ln T} \;=\; 1 - \frac{d\ln\Lambda}{d\ln T}. \tag{9.1}$$ Hence $P$ has a turning point exactly where $d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T = 1$, the boundary case of (8.5), and $dP/dn\lt 0$ exactly on the unstable branch. Between the local minimum $P_{\min}$ and the local maximum $P_{\max}$ of the curve, a single pressure is attained at three densities, of which the two extreme ones are stable and the middle one is not: two stable phases coexist at the same pressure.

(9.1) is (8.4) multiplied by $T$, and its logarithmic derivative. A turning point of $P$ along the curve is $d\ln P/d\ln T = 0$, which by (9.1) is $d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T = 1$, which is the boundary case of (8.5). For the density, (8.4) gives $d\ln n/d\ln T = -\,d\ln\Lambda/d\ln T \equiv -s$, so on any part of the curve with $s\neq0$, $$ \frac{d\ln P}{d\ln n} = \frac{1-s}{-s} = 1 - \frac{1}{s}.$$ For $s\gt 1$, which is the stable cold branch of Table 4, this is positive and less than $1$; for $0\lt s\lt 1$, the unstable branch, $1/s\gt 1$ and the expression is negative, so $dP/dn\lt 0$ there. A continuous curve that rises, falls and rises again in $P$ as $n$ increases takes every value of $P$ strictly between the local minimum and the local maximum at three densities, one on each monotone piece. The outer two lie on branches with $s\gt 1$, which satisfy (8.5) and are stable; the middle one lies on the falling branch, which does not. ∎

From the corrected fit (8.3), the turning points are

with geometric mean $2828.2\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$ and width $P_{\max}/P_{\min} = 3.134$. Fig. 3 draws the curve, with the unstable branch between those two points marked separately.

In 2011 the same two authors measured again, on 89 sight lines and with revised C I atomic data, and that second measurement is what §10 puts against the window.

10. The anchor: Jenkins & Tripp (2011) against the window

Jenkins & Tripp (2011) measured thermal pressures in the diffuse cold neutral medium along 89 sight lines, from the fine-structure excitation of neutral carbon observed with the STIS echelle spectrograph on the Hubble Space Telescope. Their mass-weighted lognormal fit, their equation (3), has mean $\log(p/k) = 3.58$ dex and an rms dispersion $\sigma_{\log P} = 0.175$ dex, which they state is a lower limit. The median is $3801.9\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$ and the $\pm1\sigma$ range is $2541$ to $5689\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$.

This module takes two predictions to that measurement, in the shape Module 2 used: one confirmed and one refuted, from the same source table.

10.1 Confirmed: the measured centre lies inside the predicted window

CONFIRMED. The predicted window is $[1960, 4810]\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$ with predicted average pressure $3070$. The measured median $3801.9\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$ lies inside it, at a ratio $1.238$ to the predicted average, and $0.738$ of the way from $P_{\min}$ to $P_{\max}$ measured in dex.

The content of that confirmation is that a factor-$2.5$ window exists at all, and sits where it does. Neither of its edges was fitted to a pressure value. Wolfire et al. computed $P_{\min}$ and $P_{\max}$ from grain photoelectric heating and [C II]/[O I] cooling, with gas-phase carbon and oxygen abundances fixed by independent Hubble absorption-line studies and a PAH abundance fixed by ISO infrared emission data. One heating parameter, $\varphi_{\rm PAH} = 0.5$, was calibrated to an observed C I/C II column-density ratio; Wolfire's own detailed working credits that ratio to the C I and H I data compiled by Welty & Hobbs (2001), although an earlier passage cites Jenkins & Tripp (2001) alongside it. Jenkins & Tripp (2001) is the predecessor of, and distinct from, the 2011 pressure measurement compared here.

Two things this module does not claim. It does not claim that the $\varphi_{\rm PAH}$ calibration and the 2011 pressures share a technique or a set of sight lines: a search of the 2001 paper's own text finds no C I/C II ratio, no total carbon column and no N(C II) reported in it. And it does not claim that the two lineages are wholly separate: Wolfire et al. also compared their predicted average pressure against Jenkins & Tripp's (2001) derived value of $2240\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$, after the fact and without adjusting any parameter, reporting their own value higher by a factor $3070/2240 = 1.37$, which is $0.14$ dex. The 2011 revision from $2240$ to $3801.9$ is attributed by its authors to three reasons of their own — updated C I atomic rates, revised optical pumping, and a corrected temperature — none of which depends on Wolfire et al.

Second, Jenkins & Tripp state that their lognormal understates the observed material outside $3.2 \lt \log(p/k) \lt 4.0$, that is below $1585\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$. Three of the five variants have $P_{\min}$ below that line — $1560$, $1580$ and $1460$ — so their $1.4$, $1.5$ and $0.9$ per cent are extrapolations of a fit its own authors say runs low there, and the true fractions are larger. The direction favours the refutation; the rows are labelled extrapolated anyway.

10.4 What fails, precisely: the word "static", not the criterion

Jenkins & Tripp offer two readings of their own excess and prefer the second: either the Wolfire curve does not apply to the gas they observe, or, in their words, "rarefactions caused by turbulence create momentary excursions below the curve". Their dispersion of $0.175$ dex is itself read as turbulence at Mach $1$ to $4$. On that reading the thermal-equilibrium curve is an attractor, and the gas is pushed off it faster than it relaxes back. Problem D3 makes that quantitative by putting the cooling time against the crossing time of a turbulent eddy, and finds the two phases differ by a factor $794$ in cooling length — which is why a cold excursion below $P_{\min}$ can only be momentary while a warm departure can be sustained.

The same failure seen from the warm side. Heiles & Troland (2003), from 21-cm absorption, find that at least $48$ per cent of the warm neutral medium by column density lies at $500$–$5000$ K, inside the thermally unstable band; a strict two-phase medium allows only the small fraction in transit between phases. Their other numbers: the warm medium is $61$ per cent of the H I column at $|b|\gt 10^\circ$, and the cold-medium spin-temperature histogram peaks at $40$ K with a column-weighted median of $70$ K. Both the $0.61$ and the $0.48$ are column-density weighted and both are lower bounds.

The mean state is right; the spread around it is not there. Wolfire et al. compute a cold-medium temperature of $85.0$ K at the average pressure, a ratio of $1.21$ to Heiles & Troland's measured column-weighted median of $70$ K. The two-phase calculation gets the typical temperature and the typical pressure of the neutral interstellar medium right, and gets the width of both distributions wrong, in the same direction, from both ends.

For reference, against the analytic-fit window $[1597, 5007]$ the same lognormal puts $73.7$ per cent of the mass inside, rather than the $67.0$ per cent quoted above for $[1960, 4810]$.

11. What this model captures, and what it misses

Each idealisation, and where it is repaid if it is repaid at all.

  • No radiative transfer. The split of the flux between radiation and convection is not predicted anywhere in this module. §7 measures it at $1.3$ per cent at $\tau = 2/3$ and stops there. This is the single largest gap, because it is what makes the most direct test of mixing-length theory a test of something the theory does not claim. It is not repaid in Modules 1–10.
  • Mixing-length theory is a one-parameter closure calibrated on the answer. $\alpha = 2$ was fitted to the solar radius. Problem C3 shows the freedom that leaves is small for the velocity, $\alpha^{1/3}$, and large for the superadiabatic excess, $\alpha^{-4/3}$. Not repaid.
  • No rotation and no magnetic field. Both change convection in the Sun, and the magnetic case changes it qualitatively at the surface. Neither is treated in Modules 1–10.
  • Linear stability only. Both criteria, Schwarzschild's and Field's, say that a perturbation grows. Neither says what it grows into. Module 5's Bonnor–Ebert analysis has exactly the same limit, and §10 shows the cost: the measured distribution of interstellar pressures is a statement about the non-linear, turbulent state, and the linear criterion can only say that the state it predicts is not the one observed.
  • The two-phase medium is treated as static. The anchor identifies this as the assumption that fails. Turbulence is named as the mechanism, on Jenkins & Tripp's own reading of their data, and is not modelled here.
  • The solar opacity is read off a model, not computed. §3.2 reads the rise of $\nabla$ from the table and deliberately does not apportion it between the opacity and the temperature, because this module computes neither.
  • The parcel picture itself. Propositions 1 and 5 follow a single blob against a background that does not respond. Real convection is a connected flow with narrow fast downdrafts and broad slow upflows, which is why Oba et al. measure different upward and downward speeds at the same height. Nothing in this module distinguishes them.

C1. The International Standard Atmosphere fixes the tropospheric lapse rate at $6.50\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$. Show that the standard troposphere is Schwarzschild-stable, give its polytropic index, and then explain why thunderstorms exist.
Solution
For a plane-parallel ideal-gas atmosphere with lapse rate $\Gamma_{\rm lapse} \equiv -dT/dz$, hydrostatic balance gives $d\ln P/dz = -1/H$ with $H = k_BT/(\mu m_u g)$, so $$ \nabla = \frac{d\ln T}{d\ln P} = \frac{(1/T)(dT/dz)}{-1/H} = \frac{\Gamma_{\rm lapse}\,H}{T} = \frac{\Gamma_{\rm lapse}}{g/c_p}\cdot\frac{k_B}{\mu m_u c_p} = \frac{\Gamma_{\rm lapse}}{g/c_p}\,\nabla_{\rm ad},$$ using $c_p - k_B/(\mu m_u) = c_v$ and $\nabla_{\rm ad} = (\gamma-1)/\gamma = k_B/(\mu m_u c_p)$ from (2.5). So $\nabla/\nabla_{\rm ad}$ is just the ratio of the actual lapse rate to the dry adiabatic one. With $c_p = 1.0047\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ for dry air and $g = 980.665\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}$, the dry adiabatic lapse rate is $g/c_p = 9.761\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$ and the ratio is $6.50/9.761 = 0.6659$. With $\gamma = 7/5$, $\nabla_{\rm ad} = 2/7 = 0.28571$ and $\nabla = 0.19026$. By Definition 5 of Module 3, $n_{\rm eff} = 1/\nabla - 1 = 4.2558$.
So $\nabla \lt \nabla_{\rm ad}$: the standard troposphere is stable by Proposition 1, with $33$ per cent of margin, and yet it convects on any warm afternoon. The premise that fails is (iv), adiabatic change of state. A rising parcel of moist air reaches saturation and then releases latent heat as water condenses, about $2.5\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}}$, which warms it on the way up. Its effective lapse rate is then $5$–$6\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$, below the ambient $6.5$, so the moist parcel arrives warmer than its surroundings and keeps rising. Moist convection is unstable where dry convection is not. The Sun has no comparable latent-heat term in its convection zone, which is why §2 derives the criterion there.
C2. Express the thermally unstable branch of §8 as a range in each of temperature, density and pressure. Why does the asymmetry between those three ranges make a pressure survey the right measurement to make?
Solution
From the turning points of Proposition 8 on the corrected fit: temperature $184.0$ to $5039.4$ K, a factor $27.4$; density $0.994$ to $8.682\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, a factor $8.7$; pressure $1597$ to $5007\ \mathrm{K\,cm^{-3}}$, a factor $3.13$. The pressure range is by far the narrowest, and it is narrow for the reason Proposition 8 gives: $P = n_{\rm eq}T$ runs along a curve on which $n$ and $T$ move in opposite directions, so the product barely changes while each factor changes by an order of magnitude.
That is exactly what makes pressure the useful observable. A measured temperature or a measured density of one cloud says little, because both vary by a large factor within the allowed states; a measured pressure pins the state of the medium to within a factor of three, and any excursion outside that factor is immediately a violation of the static two-phase picture rather than a normal fluctuation. §10 is the measurement, and $29$ per cent of its mass falls outside.

Note also that the excess moves by a factor $4.3$ across the same range, so $\alpha$ is a strong handle on the stratification and a weak one on the velocity — which is why calibrating $\alpha$ on a stellar radius works, and why the radius calibration says little about the surface flow.

D1. Show that for a plane-parallel ideal-gas atmosphere the buoyancy frequency of Proposition 2 can be written $N^2 = (g/T)\,(dT/dz + g/c_p)$, and evaluate it for the isothermal lower stratosphere at $T = 216.65$ K and for the standard troposphere at $T = 288.15$ K.
Solution
From (3.1), $N^2 = (g/H)(\nabla_{\rm ad}-\nabla)$ with $H = k_BT/(\mu m_u g)$. As in C1, $\nabla = -(H/T)\,dT/dz$ and $\nabla_{\rm ad} = k_B/(\mu m_u c_p)$, so $$ N^2 = \frac{g}{H}\left[\frac{k_B}{\mu m_u c_p} + \frac{H}{T}\frac{dT}{dz}\right] = \frac{g\,k_B}{\mu m_u c_p H} + \frac{g}{T}\frac{dT}{dz}.$$ Substituting $H = k_BT/(\mu m_u g)$ in the first term gives $g^2/(c_pT)$, so $N^2 = (g/T)(g/c_p + dT/dz)$ as required. A layer that is Schwarzschild-unstable and Ledoux-stable is semiconvective: the temperature structure drives overturning, the composition structure resists it, and the layer mixes on a thermal rather than a dynamical timescale, because the parcel must first exchange heat with its surroundings before it can move.

D3. Define the cooling length $c_st_{\rm cool}$ and the Field length $\lambda_F$, evaluate both for the cold and the warm phases on the equilibrium curve, and use the result to explain why the warm medium sustains a large departure from thermal equilibrium while the cold medium's excursions below $P_{\min}$ must be transient.
Solution
Take the cooling time as the thermal energy divided by the net radiated power, $t_{\rm cool} = \tfrac32 nk_BT/(n^2\Lambda(T))$, and the Field length as $\lambda_F = \sqrt{\kappa T/(n^2\Lambda)}$, the wavelength at which conduction erases a temperature contrast as fast as cooling builds it, with the neutral-gas conductivity $\kappa = 2.5\times10^{3}\sqrt{T}$ adopted by Koyama & Inutsuka after Parker (1953). The sound speed is the adiabatic $c_s = \sqrt{(5/3)k_BT/(\mu m_u)}$ with $\mu = 1.27$ for the neutral medium. Both phases are taken at the states the observations report: $T = 70$ K for the cold medium, Heiles & Troland's column-weighted median spin temperature, and $T = 6000$ K for the warm medium, a point on the warm branch near $P_{\max}$.

A disturbance smaller than $c_st_{\rm cool}$ is erased by cooling before sound can cross it, so the gas relaxes back onto the equilibrium curve; a disturbance larger than $c_st_{\rm cool}$ outruns the cooling and carries the gas off the curve. The two cooling lengths differ by a factor $794$. For the cold medium $c_st_{\rm cool} = 0.020$ pc, far below any scale a survey resolves or turbulence drives, so cold gas relaxes back onto the curve almost at once and its excursions below $P_{\min}$ must be transient rarefactions rather than a sustained state — which is exactly how Jenkins & Tripp read their own $29$ per cent of cold mass below $P_{\min}$. For the warm medium $c_st_{\rm cool} = 16.29$ pc, the size of a whole molecular cloud, so turbulence driven on tens of parsecs can hold a large part of the warm volume permanently away from equilibrium. That is consistent with Heiles & Troland finding at least $48$ per cent of the warm column at $500$–$5000$ K, although the two published fractions are not the same quantity and are not compared here as one: $48$ per cent is a warm-medium column fraction inside an unstable temperature band, where gas may sit exactly on the unstable branch of the curve, and $29$ per cent is a cold-medium mass fraction below a pressure edge, where the curve has no cold branch at all. What the two cooling lengths explain is the direction and the size of the asymmetry, not a ratio between those two numbers.
The Field lengths set something different: the resolution a simulation needs, since a grid coarser than $\lambda_F$ cannot represent the smallest condensation the instability makes. Koyama & Inutsuka used $140$ au cells on a $2048\times512$ grid over $1.44\times0.36$ pc for exactly this reason.

K3. Is the hot gas of a galaxy cluster thermally unstable? Take $T = 10^{8}$ K, $n_{\rm H} = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $\mu = 0.6$, bremsstrahlung cooling $\Lambda = 2.1\times10^{-27}\sqrt{T}\ \mathrm{erg\,cm^{3}\,s^{-1}}$, a cluster radius of $1$ Mpc, and $n_e = 1.2\,n_{\rm H}$, $n_{\rm tot} = 2.3\,n_{\rm H}$ for a fully ionised cosmic mixture. Compare the cooling time with the sound-crossing time and the Field length with the cluster radius, then repeat for a cool core at $n_{\rm H} = 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $T = 3\times10^{7}$ K, $R = 50$ kpc.
Solution
$\Lambda = 2.1\times10^{-27}\sqrt{10^{8}} = 2.1000\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{erg\,cm^{3}\,s^{-1}}$. The thermal energy density is $\tfrac32 n_{\rm tot}k_BT = 4.7632\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{erg\,cm^{-3}}$ and the radiated power is $n_en_{\rm H}\Lambda = 2.5200\times10^{-29}\ \mathrm{erg\,cm^{-3}\,s^{-1}}$, so $t_{\rm cool} = 1.8902\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{s} = 59.90$ Gyr. The sound speed $\sqrt{(5/3)k_BT/(\mu m_u)} = 1520\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ crosses $1$ Mpc in $2.0304\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{s} = 0.643$ Gyr, so $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm sound} = 93.1$.
For conduction, the Spitzer electron conductivity through Sarazin eq. (5.37), $\kappa = 1.84\times10^{-5}T^{5/2}/\ln\Lambda$ with Module 1's $\ln\Lambda = 37.8$ for the intracluster medium, gives $\kappa = 4.8677\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}\,cm^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ and a Field length $\sqrt{\kappa T/(n_en_{\rm H}\Lambda)} = 4504$ kpc, which is $4.50$ times the cluster radius. Both tests agree: conduction smooths any thermally unstable mode before it can grow, and the gas would not have cooled appreciably in a Hubble time even if it could.
The cool core is a different matter, and the reason is geometry rather than microphysics. At fixed $T$, $t_{\rm cool}\propto 1/n$ and $\lambda_F\propto1/n$, so both shrink together and the dimensionless ratio $\lambda_F/(c_st_{\rm cool})$ does not change with density at all. What changes is the size of the region they are compared with. At $n_{\rm H} = 10^{-2}$ and $T = 3\times10^{7}$ K: $t_{\rm cool} = 3.28$ Gyr, $\lambda_F = 74.0$ kpc, $\lambda_F/R = 1.48$ and $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm sound} = 55.9$. The core cools well inside a Hubble time and its Field length is a sizeable fraction of its radius rather than several times it. Thermal instability in clusters is a cool-core phenomenon.
