Accretion Discs

Angular momentum, not gravity, is what stops gas from falling in — and the molecular viscosity that could carry it away is twelve decades too slow. Four checks, of which two refute. The prescription that replaces molecular viscosity has one free parameter, and the same parameter measured in two classes of disc differs by a factor of ten to forty, while the simulations of the very instability Module 12 derives produce at most a fifth of what the observations need.

Module 9 built spherical accretion and then, in its own closing table, refused the object it had been aimed at. Sagittarius A*'s gas does not fall radially inward: it carries angular momentum, and it circularises near $100\,r_S$ — five decades inside the Bondi radius — where it must then transport that angular momentum outward before any mass can reach the centre. Module 9's words were that this "is Module 11's subject and nothing in this module can address it".

Module 12 then stated the difficulty in one line and left it. A Keplerian disc has $\Omega \propto R^{-3/2}$, so its specific angular momentum $\ell = \Omega R^2 \propto R^{1/2}$ increases outward, which by the Rayleigh criterion makes it stable. A stable disc transports no angular momentum and therefore does not accrete. Module 12 assumed the shear $q = 3/2$ that this statement rests on, and said in print that deriving it needs a disc model, which is this module.

The plan of this module is one problem and one number. The problem is that a disc is too stable. The number is how badly molecular viscosity fails to fix it: twelve decades, which is not a discrepancy to be argued about but a statement that some other physics carries the angular momentum. What replaces molecular viscosity is a parametrisation with one free number in it, and the last two sections are about how big that number is and whether it deserves to be called a constant.

Two things this module does not do, and both are promises a shipped page already made. 1. Gas with angular momentum cannot fall in

Take a parcel of gas at rest far from a mass $M$, and give it a small specific angular momentum $\ell$ about that mass. As it falls, $\ell$ is conserved, and the centrifugal term $\ell^2/2R^2$ in its effective potential grows as $R^{-2}$ while the gravitational term $-GM/R$ grows only as $R^{-1}$. There is therefore a radius at which the two balance and the infall stops:

$$ R_{\rm circ} \;=\; \frac{\ell^2}{GM}. \tag{1.1}$$

Inside $R_{\rm circ}$ the gas cannot go without losing angular momentum. It does not stop moving — it orbits. What was a radial infall becomes a disc, and the question changes from what holds the gas up? to what lets it down?

Definition 1 (a geometrically thin accretion disc). An axisymmetric, vertically hydrostatic, differentially rotating flow around a central mass $M$, described by a surface density $\Sigma(R,t)$ and a scale height $H(R)$ with $H/R \ll 1$, in which the orbital motion is Keplerian to first order in $(H/R)^2$ and mass moves inward on a timescale long compared with the orbital one. The material does not free-fall; it spirals.

Module 9 gave the number that makes this concrete. Sagittarius A*'s gas circularises near $100\,r_S$, where $r_S = 2GM/c^2$ is the Schwarzschild radius. At the GRAVITY Collaboration mass of $4.30\times10^{6}\,M_\odot$ that $r_S$ is $1.2699\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{cm}$, so the circularisation radius is $1.2699\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{cm}$ and the specific angular momentum needed to produce it is $2.6920\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ by (1.1).

Two worked discs carry the rest of this module. They are configurations and not objects, in the sense Module 7 §7.1 and Module 12 §9 use the phrase: each entry is a round choice inside the range its class occupies, and no named system was read.

2. Disc kinematics, and the stability that is the problem

Everything in this section is kinematics. No pressure, no viscosity, no magnetic field: only what a point mass does to a ring of gas.

Proposition 1 (Keplerian rotation and the shear parameter). For a disc whose self-gravity is negligible and whose radial pressure gradient is small compared with gravity, centrifugal balance gives $$ \Omega(R) \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R^{3}}}, \qquad v_K = \Omega R = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R}}, \qquad \ell = \Omega R^{2} = \sqrt{GMR}, \tag{2.1}$$ and therefore the shear parameter $q \equiv -\,\mathrm{d}\ln\Omega/\mathrm{d}\ln R$ takes the value $q = 3/2$ exactly.
Proof. Radial force balance on a ring in steady circular motion is $\Omega^2 R = GM/R^2$, which is (2.1). Taking logarithms, $\ln\Omega = \tfrac12\ln(GM) - \tfrac32\ln R$, so $\mathrm{d}\ln\Omega/\mathrm{d}\ln R = -3/2$ identically, independent of $M$ and of $R$. ∎
The generator differentiates $\Omega$ numerically rather than returning $3/2$, and an assertion requires the answer to be $3/2$ to better than $10^{-6}$; the run prints $1.5000000000$. That is deliberate: Module 12 §9 says in print that its $q = 3/2$ is assumed and not derived, so this module must produce it from something rather than name it.
Proposition 2 (the epicyclic frequency, and the Rayleigh criterion). For $\Omega \propto R^{-q}$ the epicyclic frequency, at which a displaced ring oscillates radially, is $$ \kappa_{\rm ep}^{2} \;=\; \frac{1}{R^{3}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}R}\!\left(R^{4}\Omega^{2}\right) \;=\; 2\,\Omega^{2}\,(2-q). \tag{2.2}$$ The flow is stable to axisymmetric displacements when $\kappa_{\rm ep}^2 \gt 0$, equivalently when $\mathrm{d}(\ell^2)/\mathrm{d}R \gt 0$, equivalently when $q \lt 2$. A Keplerian disc has $q = 3/2$, hence $\kappa_{\rm ep} = \Omega$ exactly and $\mathrm{d}(\ell^2)/\mathrm{d}R = GM$ at every radius.
Proof. Whatever holds the rotation law up, it acts as a central acceleration $g(R)$ directed inward, and circular motion at $R$ requires $g(R) = \Omega^2(R)\,R$. For a point mass $g = GM/R^2$, but the argument below needs only that $g$ depends on $R$ alone. Displace a ring from $R$ to $R+\xi$ at fixed specific angular momentum $\ell(R) = \Omega(R)R^{2}$. Its radial equation of motion is $$ \ddot{\xi} \;=\; \frac{\ell(R)^{2}}{(R+\xi)^{3}} \;-\; g(R+\xi), $$ whose right-hand side vanishes at $\xi = 0$, because $\ell(R)^2/R^3 = \Omega^2R = g(R)$. Expanding to first order in $\xi$, $$ \ddot{\xi} \;=\; -\left[\frac{3\ell(R)^{2}}{R^{4}} + g^{\prime}(R)\right]\xi \;\equiv\; -\,\kappa_{\rm ep}^{2}\,\xi, $$ so the displaced ring oscillates when the bracket is positive and runs away when it is negative. Now $3\ell^2/R^4 = 3\Omega^2$, and for $\Omega \propto R^{-q}$, $g = \Omega^2R \propto R^{1-2q}$ gives $g^{\prime} = (1-2q)\Omega^2$. Hence $$ \kappa_{\rm ep}^{2} \;=\; 3\Omega^{2} + (1-2q)\Omega^{2} \;=\; (4-2q)\,\Omega^{2} \;=\; 2\,\Omega^{2}(2-q), $$ which is the right-hand side of (2.2). The middle expression of (2.2) is the same number written as a derivative: $R^{4}\Omega^{2} = \ell^{2} \propto R^{4-2q}$, so $R^{-3}\mathrm{d}(R^{4}\Omega^{2})/\mathrm{d}R = (4-2q)\Omega^{2}$. Since $\mathrm{d}(\ell^{2})/\mathrm{d}R = R^{3}\kappa_{\rm ep}^{2}$, the two stability statements are one statement, and both hold for $q \lt 2$. Setting $q = 3/2$ gives $\kappa_{\rm ep}^{2} = 2\Omega^{2}(2-3/2) = \Omega^{2}$; substituting Proposition 1’s $\Omega^{2} = GM/R^{3}$ then gives $\mathrm{d}(\ell^{2})/\mathrm{d}R = R^{3}\Omega^{2} = GM$ at every radius. ∎
The trivial limits, both of which the generator asserts. A rigidly rotating fluid has $q = 0$ and (2.2) gives $\kappa_{\rm ep} = 2\Omega$, which is the Coriolis result for a parcel in a rotating frame. At the Rayleigh boundary $q = 2$, (2.2) gives $\kappa_{\rm ep} = 0$ exactly: the restoring force vanishes and the displaced ring neither returns nor runs away. Both are checked to $10^{-14}$ on every run.
The result that sets up the rest of the module, and Fig. 1 is a picture of it. A Keplerian disc is stable to axisymmetric displacements at every radius, because $\kappa_{\rm ep}^{2} = \Omega^{2} \gt 0$ there. Axisymmetric instability would require $q \gt 2$, and a point mass produces $q = 3/2$ and can produce nothing else. Within the physics of this section — no pressure, no viscosity, no field — there is therefore no linear axisymmetric route by which rings exchange angular momentum, and the disc does not accrete. That is the narrowest form of the statement and the only form Proposition 2 proves; whether non-axisymmetric or nonlinear hydrodynamic routes exist is a separate question that this book does not settle, and §11 does not need it, because the mechanism it reaches for is magnetic and not hydrodynamic at all.

Two consequences are worth stating before moving on. First, $\kappa_{\rm ep} = \Omega$ for a Keplerian disc is a degeneracy, not a coincidence: it is why a Keplerian orbit closes on itself and does not precess, and it is why the radial and orbital periods of a planet are the same. Second, the same equality makes the Toomre criterion of §10 simpler than it looks in the galactic-dynamics literature, where $\kappa_{\rm ep}$ and $\Omega$ differ.

3. The anchor: the timescale gap

Module 1 §6 derived the transport coefficients of a collisional gas, and Module 1 §9 — its captures-and-misses table — says of the ideal-flow row that "§6 gives the coefficients; Module 11 needs them for accretion discs". This is where they are needed, and the answer they give is that they are not enough.

Module 1's Proposition 4 gives the dynamic viscosity of a gas of mass density $\rho$, thermal speed $v_{\rm th}$ and mean free path $\lambda$ as $\mu \simeq \tfrac13 \rho v_{\rm th}\lambda$, so the kinematic viscosity is

$$ \nu_{\rm mol} \;=\; \frac{\mu}{\rho} \;=\; \tfrac13\, v_{\rm th}\,\lambda, \tag{3.1}$$

in which the density has cancelled. The coefficient $\tfrac13$ is Module 1's and is not a choice made here.

The thermal speed in (3.1) is the ion's, not the mixture's. Viscosity in a hydrogen plasma is carried by the ions, because at equal temperature and comparable mean free path the ratio of the ion to the electron contribution is $(\rho_i/\rho_e)(v_i/v_e) = \sqrt{m_p/m_e} = 42.9$. Using the mean molecular weight of the mixture, $\mu = 0.6$, would overstate $v_{\rm th}$ and with it $\nu_{\rm mol}$ by a factor of $1.2957$.

For the dwarf-nova row the midplane number density is $1.7524\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, the Coulomb logarithm is $5.0355$, and Module 1's Coulomb mean free path is $2.6770\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{cm}$ — which is $1.1716\times10^{-12}$ of a scale height, so the gas is collisional beyond any doubt. The ion thermal speed is $25.1115\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, and (3.1) gives

$$ \nu_{\rm mol} \;=\; 224.0774\ \mathrm{cm^{2}\,s^{-1}}, \qquad t_\nu \;=\; \frac{R^{2}}{\nu_{\rm mol}} \;=\; 4.4627\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{s} \;=\; 1.4142\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{yr}. \tag{3.2}$$
CHECK 1 — REFUTED. Molecular viscosity, by 12.01 decades. A dwarf nova in outburst decays on a timescale of days; the configuration here takes five days, $4.32\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{s}$. Against the molecular viscous time of (3.2) the ratio is $\mathbf{1.0330\times10^{12}}$, that is 12.01 decades. Equivalently, the $\alpha$ of §6 that $\nu_{\rm mol}$ corresponds to is $4.8098\times10^{-13}$, short of the measured $0.1$–$0.4$ by $11.32$ to $11.92$ decades. The viscous time exceeds the age of the universe; the disc it describes has never accreted anything.

Two quantities in this calculation could be argued about, and both are priced rather than waved at. Using the mixture instead of the ion carrier is worth a factor of $1.2957$. Doubling the Coulomb logarithm from $5.0355$ to $10.0710$ is worth a factor of $0.5$. Together they move the answer by $0.35$ of a decade against a gap of $12.01$. Nothing inside the transport coefficient closes a gap of that size. Fig. 2 puts all five timescales on one axis.

The dwarf-nova parameters are a configuration and not an object. $M = 0.6\,M_\odot$, $R = 10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm}$, $T = 3\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{K}$, $\Sigma = 100\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-2}}$ and a five-day outburst are round choices in the ranges their class occupies, and no named system was read for this module. King, Pringle & Livio's Sect. 2.1 rests on detailed light curves of SS Cyg, VW Hyi, U Gem, WZ Sge and Z Cam; none of those papers was fetched. A named system would change the third significant figure of $12.01$ and not the twelfth decade.

4. The viscous torque

If angular momentum is to move outward, something must exert a torque. In a fluid with kinematic viscosity $\nu$ the stress between neighbouring annuli is a shear stress, and it does exactly that.

Proposition 3 (the viscous torque). Let $G(R)$ be the torque that the disc outside radius $R$ exerts on the disc inside it. In a flow of kinematic viscosity $\nu$ and surface density $\Sigma$, that torque is $$ G(R) \;=\; 2\pi R \cdot \nu\Sigma R \frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{\mathrm{d}R} \cdot R \;=\; 2\pi R^{3}\,\nu\Sigma\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{\mathrm{d}R}. \tag{4.1}$$ For any $q \gt 0$ this is negative: the outer disc takes angular momentum from the inner disc, so angular momentum flows outward.
Proof. The two sides of a cylindrical surface at radius $R$ slide past each other with a velocity difference, per unit radial separation, of $R\,\mathrm{d}\Omega/\mathrm{d}R$: this is the rate of strain, with the rigid-rotation part removed, because a fluid in rigid rotation has no internal stress. The viscous shear stress is $\eta$ times the rate of strain, and integrating the dynamic viscosity $\eta = \rho\nu$ over the disc thickness replaces $\rho$ by $\Sigma$, giving a force per unit length of circumference $\nu\Sigma R\,\mathrm{d}\Omega/\mathrm{d}R$. Multiplying by the circumference $2\pi R$ gives a force and by the moment arm $R$ gives (4.1). For $\Omega \propto R^{-q}$ with $q \gt 0$, $\mathrm{d}\Omega/\mathrm{d}R \lt 0$, so $G \lt 0$. A negative torque on the inner disc slows it; by Newton’s third law the outer disc receives $-G \gt 0$ and is sped up. Angular momentum therefore crosses the surface outward at the rate $|G|$. ∎

The sign is worth dwelling on, because it is the opposite of the intuition that friction between orbits should drag the outer material inward. The inner disc rotates faster, so it is the inner disc that does work on the outer one. Angular momentum moves outward and mass moves inward, and each is what the other requires.

5. How a disc spreads

Proposition 4 (the surface-density diffusion equation). Conservation of mass and of angular momentum for an axisymmetric thin disc with a Keplerian rotation law give $$ \frac{\partial\Sigma}{\partial t} \;=\; \frac{3}{R}\frac{\partial}{\partial R}\!\left[R^{1/2}\frac{\partial}{\partial R}\!\left(\nu\Sigma R^{1/2}\right)\right]. \tag{5.1}$$ This is a diffusion equation for $\Sigma$ with diffusion coefficient of order $\nu$. Whether it is linear depends entirely on the closure: with the $\alpha$ prescription of §6 at fixed temperature, $\nu = \alpha c_T^{2}/\Omega$ carries no $\Sigma$ and (5.1) is linear. It becomes nonlinear the moment the disc’s thermal balance ties the temperature back to $\Sigma$ — which is what the dwarf-nova outburst cycle of §3 is, and which this module does not build.
Proof. Mass conservation for an annulus of width $\mathrm{d}R$ carrying radial velocity $v_R$ is $$ R\frac{\partial\Sigma}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial R}\!\left(R\Sigma v_R\right) = 0. $$ Angular momentum conservation for the same annulus, with the torque of (4.1) acting on each face, is $$ R\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\left(\Sigma R^{2}\Omega\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial R}\!\left(R\Sigma v_R\cdot R^{2}\Omega\right) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\partial G}{\partial R}. $$ Expanding the second equation and using the first to eliminate $\partial\Sigma/\partial t$ leaves $$ R\Sigma v_R \frac{\partial}{\partial R}\!\left(R^{2}\Omega\right) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\partial G}{\partial R}, $$ For $\Omega = \sqrt{GM/R^{3}}$ we have $R^{2}\Omega = \sqrt{GM}\,R^{1/2}$, so $\partial(R^{2}\Omega)/\partial R = \tfrac12\sqrt{GM}\,R^{-1/2}$, and $\mathrm{d}\Omega/\mathrm{d}R = -\tfrac32\sqrt{GM}\,R^{-5/2}$ turns (4.1) into $G = -3\pi\sqrt{GM}\,\nu\Sigma R^{1/2}$. Substituting both, $$ \Sigma v_R \;=\; \frac{2}{\sqrt{GM}\,R^{1/2}}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\partial G}{\partial R} \;=\; -\frac{3}{R^{1/2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial R}\!\left(\nu\Sigma R^{1/2}\right). $$ Substituting this into mass conservation gives $\partial\Sigma/\partial t = -R^{-1}\partial(R\Sigma v_R)/\partial R$, which is (5.1). ∎
The algebra in the last two lines of that proof is the standard one and is not this module's; the derivation is laid out in full in Pringle's 1981 review, which is cited here for the result only and was not fetched. Nothing numerical in this module depends on (5.1): it is here because §6's viscous time is the diffusion time of this equation, and a reader is entitled to see where that time comes from.

6. The α prescription, and what it is not

Section 3 established that molecular viscosity is twelve decades too small. Shakura and Sunyaev's response in 1973 was not to find a bigger viscosity but to parametrise one, and to bound it.

Definition 2 (the α prescription). The disc's effective kinematic viscosity is written $$ \nu \;=\; \alpha\, c_T H, \tag{6.1}$$ with $c_T$ the isothermal sound speed and $H$ the scale height, and $\alpha$ a dimensionless number of order unity or less.

The argument behind the bound is short. Whatever carries the stress is a motion within the disc; if it is turbulence, its eddies cannot be larger than $H$ without leaving the disc, and cannot be faster than $c_T$ without shocking and dissipating. So $\nu \lesssim c_T H$, and $\alpha \lesssim 1$. That is the whole of it: the prescription is not a derivation and this module will not pretend otherwise.

Three things about Shakura and Sunyaev's own α that a reader who opens their paper will meet. Their p. 338 writes it as a sum, $\alpha = v_t/v_s + H^2/(4\pi\rho v_s^2)$, of a turbulent Mach number and a magnetic-to-thermal energy ratio — not as a single stress coefficient. Their $H$ is the magnetic field, in the older convention where $H$ denotes $B$; this book's $H$ is the scale height, and the two collide inside the founding paper of the subject. And their $v_s$ obeys $\rho v_s^2/2 = \tfrac32\rho kT/m_p + \varepsilon_r$, so with radiation neglected $v_s^2 = 3kT/m_p$ — the root-mean-square thermal speed, larger than this book's $c_T$ by $1.34$ at $\mu = 0.6$. Writing (6.1) with $c_T$ is a convention of this book, and $\alpha$ absorbs the factor.

Their own footnote on the same page is worth carrying too: $\alpha$ "is assumed to be constant along the disk in our calculations", and the disc's observational appearance — its spectrum and its surface temperature — "do not strongly depend on the chosen value of $\alpha$". §8 proves the second half of that sentence, and §11 is about what it costs.

7. Vertical structure, and what "thin" means

Proposition 5 (vertical hydrostatic balance). In a disc whose self-gravity is negligible, the vertical component of the central mass's gravity at height $z \ll R$ is $g_z = \Omega^2 z$, and hydrostatic balance with an isothermal equation of state gives $$ \rho(z) \;=\; \rho_0\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2H^{2}}\right), \qquad H \;=\; \frac{c_T}{\Omega}, \qquad \Sigma \;=\; \sqrt{2\pi}\,\rho_0 H. \tag{7.1}$$
Proof. The vertical gravity of a point mass at cylindrical radius $R$ and height $z$ is $g_z = GMz/(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}$, which for $z \ll R$ is $GMz/R^3 = \Omega^2 z$. Vertical hydrostatic balance for an isothermal layer is $c_T^2\,\mathrm{d}\rho/\mathrm{d}z = -\rho\,\Omega^2 z$, whose solution is the Gaussian in (7.1) with $H = c_T/\Omega$. Integrating over all $z$ gives $\int\rho\,\mathrm{d}z = \sqrt{2\pi}\rho_0 H$. ∎
The factor is $\sqrt{2\pi} = 2.5066$ and not $2$. Writing $\Sigma = 2\rho_0 H$ — the constant-density slab — would put the midplane density $25.3$ per cent high. The generator integrates the Gaussian numerically over $\pm 20H$ on every run and asserts that the result is $\Sigma$ to one part in $10^{6}$.

The approximation $g_z \simeq \Omega^2 z$ has a price and it is small. At two scale heights on the dwarf-nova row, $z = 4.5698\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{cm}$ and $z/R = 0.04570$, the thin-disc form overstates the exact vertical gravity by 0.3134 per cent — and $95.45$ per cent of a Gaussian column lies inside $2H$. The error grows as $(z/R)^2$, so it is the aspect ratio and not the height that prices it.

Definition 3 (the thermal time). $t_{\rm th} \equiv 1/(\alpha\Omega)$, the time in which viscous dissipation supplies the disc’s own thermal energy, to within a factor of order unity. The factor is computable and is named here rather than hidden: the thermal energy per unit disc area is $U = \Sigma c_T^{2}/(\gamma-1)$, and the dissipation on both faces is $\tfrac94\nu\Sigma\Omega^{2} = \tfrac94\alpha\Sigma c_T^{2}\Omega$ once $\nu = \alpha c_T H$ and $H = c_T/\Omega$ are put in — a rate that §8 derives from Proposition 3 and that is quoted forward to here. The ratio is $U/(\tfrac94\alpha\Sigma c_T^{2}\Omega) = \tfrac{4}{9(\gamma-1)}(\alpha\Omega)^{-1}$, which is $\tfrac23(\alpha\Omega)^{-1}$ at $\gamma = 5/3$. This module writes $1/(\alpha\Omega)$ and carries the $2/3$ nowhere, because (7.3) uses only the ordering of the three times and no factor of order unity can change it. Every printed value of $t_{\rm th}$ in this module is $1/(\alpha\Omega)$ exactly.
Proposition 6 (the three timescales). With $\nu = \alpha c_T H$ and $H = c_T/\Omega$, the dynamical, thermal and viscous times of a thin disc stand in the ratio $$ t_{\rm dyn} : t_{\rm th} : t_\nu \;=\; 1 \;:\; \frac{1}{\alpha} \;:\; \frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{R}{H}\right)^{2}, \tag{7.3}$$ where $t_{\rm dyn} = 1/\Omega$, $t_{\rm th} = 1/(\alpha\Omega)$ and $t_\nu = R^2/\nu$. For a thin disc with $\alpha \lt 1$ the ordering is strict.
Proof. $t_\nu = R^2/\nu = R^2/(\alpha c_T H)$, and $c_T = \Omega H$ by Proposition 5, so $t_\nu = R^2/(\alpha\Omega H^2) = (R/H)^2/(\alpha\Omega)$. Dividing by $t_{\rm dyn} = 1/\Omega$ gives the third entry, and $t_{\rm th}/t_{\rm dyn} = 1/\alpha$ is immediate. Since $H/R \ll 1$ and $\alpha \lt 1$, each ratio exceeds one. ∎

For the dwarf-nova row at $\alpha = 0.2$ the three are $1.1206\times10^{2}$, $5.6032\times10^{2}$ and $1.0732\times10^{6}$ seconds, with $(R/H)^2 = 1.9154\times10^{3}$. That ordering is what makes a thin disc a well-posed object. It can be treated as vertically hydrostatic and in local thermal balance while it evolves radially, because the radial evolution is the slowest thing happening.

Proposition 7 (what "thin" means, and the answer to Module 3's "no rotation"). Dividing $H = c_T/\Omega$ by $R$ gives $$ \frac{H}{R} \;=\; \frac{c_T}{v_K}. \tag{7.2}$$ A disc is thin exactly when it is cold in units of its own orbital speed.
Proof. $H = c_T/\Omega$ by Proposition 5 and $v_K = \Omega R$ by Proposition 1, so $H/R = c_T/(\Omega R) = c_T/v_K$. ∎

Module 3 §11 listed "no rotation" among its idealisations and said it "excludes centrifugal flattening and the rotational support of discs", pointing here. Equation (7.2) is that repayment, and it is worth saying why it and not something more obvious. A Keplerian disc satisfies $\Omega^2R^3/GM = 1$ exactly at every radius, but that identity is Kepler's law restated and says nothing about a disc; it is true of a single planet. What distinguishes a disc from Module 3's hydrostatic star is the ratio of the two support mechanisms: radially the disc is supported by rotation and by nothing else, vertically by pressure and by nothing else, and the aspect ratio (7.2) is the flattening. A sphere has $H/R = 1$; the dwarf-nova row has $0.02285$ and the protoplanetary row $0.044751$.

8. The steady disc and its temperature

Proposition 8 (the steady thin disc). In a steady state with a constant accretion rate $\dot M$ and a zero-torque inner boundary at $R_{\rm in}$, $$ \nu\Sigma \;=\; \frac{\dot M}{3\pi}\left[1 - \sqrt{\frac{R_{\rm in}}{R}}\right] \;\equiv\; \frac{\dot M}{3\pi}\,f(R). \tag{8.1}$$
Proof. In steady state the results in the proof of Proposition 4 give $\dot M = -2\pi R\Sigma v_R$ constant, and the angular momentum equation integrates once to $$ \dot M\,R^{2}\Omega \;+\; 2\pi R^{3}\nu\Sigma\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{\mathrm{d}R} \;=\; \text{const}. $$ The constant is fixed at the inner edge, and the condition there is $G(R_{\rm in}) = 0$ and not $\mathrm{d}\Omega/\mathrm{d}R = 0$. The Keplerian law (2.1) has $\mathrm{d}\Omega/\mathrm{d}R = -\tfrac32\Omega/R$, which is non-zero at every radius, so no Keplerian disc can satisfy the second condition anywhere. A Keplerian disc meets the first through $\nu\Sigma \to 0$ at $R_{\rm in}$ — the material leaves the disc at a black hole's last stable orbit, or a boundary layer this module does not resolve carries $\Omega$ down to a slowly spinning star's surface value just inside $R_{\rm in}$ and takes $\mathrm{d}\Omega/\mathrm{d}R$ through zero there. With $G(R_{\rm in}) = 0$ the constant is $\dot M R_{\rm in}^{2}\Omega(R_{\rm in}) = \dot M\sqrt{GMR_{\rm in}}$. Substituting the Keplerian $\Omega$ and $G = -3\pi\sqrt{GM}\,\nu\Sigma R^{1/2}$ gives $\dot M\sqrt{GMR} - 3\pi\sqrt{GM}\,\nu\Sigma R^{1/2} = \dot M\sqrt{GMR_{\rm in}}$, and dividing through by $\sqrt{GM}\,R^{1/2}$ gives (8.1). ∎
The zero-torque inner boundary is an assumption and not a theorem. It is exactly right for a disc ending at a surface of vanishing stress and wrong for one threaded by a magnetic field anchored in a spinning central object, where the torque at $R_{\rm in}$ need not vanish and can even be negative. Every number in this section and the next inherits it.
Proposition 9 (the dissipation rate and the temperature profile). The viscous dissipation per unit disc area per side is $$ D(R) \;=\; \frac{9}{8}\nu\Sigma\,\Omega^{2} \;=\; \frac{3GM\dot M}{8\pi R^{3}}\,f(R), \tag{8.2}$$ so if the disc radiates locally as a blackbody, $\sigma T_{\rm eff}^4 = D(R)$ and $$ T_{\rm eff}(R) \;=\; \left[\frac{3GM\dot M}{8\pi\sigma R^{3}}f(R)\right]^{1/4} \;\xrightarrow[R \gg R_{\rm in}]{}\; \propto R^{-3/4}. \tag{8.3}$$ The profile has a maximum at $R = (49/36)\,R_{\rm in}$.
Proof. Take the annulus between $R$ and $R + \mathrm{d}R$. The torques on its two faces do work on it at the rate $\partial(\Omega G)/\partial R\,\mathrm{d}R$, and of that, the part $\Omega\,\partial G/\partial R\,\mathrm{d}R$ is the rotational energy that arrives with the angular momentum the torque delivers. The difference is dissipated: $$ \frac{\partial(\Omega G)}{\partial R} - \Omega\frac{\partial G}{\partial R} \;=\; G\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{\mathrm{d}R} \qquad\text{per unit radius.} $$ Substituting (4.1) gives $2\pi R^{3}\nu\Sigma(\mathrm{d}\Omega/\mathrm{d}R)^{2}$, positive because it is a square, as a dissipation must be. Dividing by the annulus area per unit radius, $2\pi R$, gives the dissipation per unit area of disc on both faces together as $\nu\Sigma\,(R\,\mathrm{d}\Omega/\mathrm{d}R)^{2}$. For the Keplerian law $R\,\mathrm{d}\Omega/\mathrm{d}R = -\tfrac32\Omega$, so the bracket squared is $\tfrac94\Omega^{2}$ and the both-faces rate is $\tfrac94\nu\Sigma\Omega^{2}$. Half emerges from each face, giving $D = \tfrac98\nu\Sigma\Omega^{2}$, and substituting (8.1) and $\Omega^{2} = GM/R^{3}$ gives the second form of (8.2). 9. What a disc radiates, and the price of a Newtonian book

Proposition 10 (the radiative efficiency of a thin disc). Integrating $2D(R)$ over the whole disc gives $$ L \;=\; \frac{GM\dot M}{2R_{\rm in}}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \eta_{\rm rad} \;\equiv\; \frac{L}{\dot M c^{2}} \;=\; \frac{R_g}{2R_{\rm in}}, \qquad R_g \equiv \frac{GM}{c^{2}}. \tag{9.1}$$ At the Schwarzschild innermost stable circular orbit, $R_{\rm in} = 6R_g$, this is exactly $1/12 = 0.083333$.
Proof. $L = \int_{R_{\rm in}}^{\infty} 2D(R)\,2\pi R\,\mathrm{d}R$, where the leading $2$ counts both faces and $2\pi R\,\mathrm{d}R$ is the area of the annulus. Substituting (8.2), $$ L \;=\; \int_{R_{\rm in}}^{\infty} 2\cdot\frac{3GM\dot M}{8\pi R^{3}}f(R)\cdot 2\pi R\,\mathrm{d}R \;=\; \frac{3}{2}\,GM\dot M\int_{R_{\rm in}}^{\infty}\frac{f(R)}{R^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}R. $$ With $x = R/R_{\rm in}$ and $\mathrm{d}R = R_{\rm in}\,\mathrm{d}x$ the integral is $R_{\rm in}^{-1}\int_1^\infty x^{-2}\left(1-x^{-1/2}\right)\mathrm{d}x = R_{\rm in}^{-1}\left[1 - \tfrac23\right] = 1/(3R_{\rm in})$, so $$ L \;=\; \frac{3}{2}\,GM\dot M\cdot\frac{1}{3R_{\rm in}} \;=\; \frac{GM\dot M}{2R_{\rm in}}. $$ Dividing by $\dot M c^{2}$ and using $R_g = GM/c^{2}$ gives (9.1); setting $R_{\rm in} = 6R_g$ gives $\eta_{\rm rad} = 1/12$. ∎

Half the binding energy, and not all of it. The specific binding energy of a circular orbit at $R_{\rm in}$ is $GM/2R_{\rm in}$, not $GM/R_{\rm in}$: the other half is the kinetic energy of the orbit, which the disc still carries when the material crosses the inner boundary and has not radiated.

This book is Newtonian, and six gravitational radii from a black hole is not a place where Newtonian gravity is right. Bardeen, Press and Teukolsky's eq. Neither number is retyped from memory and neither is printed in that paper. The strings "8/9", "0.057" and "0.42" do not occur in Bardeen, Press and Teukolsky; the generator evaluates their formula. 10. When a disc weighs too much

Everything so far has assumed that the disc's own gravity does not matter. Module 5 §8 promised the correction here, listing among its idealisations "no rotation", which "omits the stabilisation of long wavelengths by rotation in a disc".

Proposition 11 (the Toomre criterion for a gas disc). A rotating, isothermal, self-gravitating sheet has the dispersion relation $$ \omega^{2} \;=\; c_T^{2}k^{2} \;-\; 2\pi G\Sigma k \;+\; \kappa_{\rm ep}^{2} \tag{10.1}$$ for axisymmetric disturbances of wavenumber $k$ in the plane, and is stable at every $k$ if and only if $$ Q \;\equiv\; \frac{c_T\,\kappa_{\rm ep}}{\pi G\Sigma} \;\gt \; 1. \tag{10.2}$$
Proof. Equation (10.1) has three terms, and this module supplies one of them and imports the other two. The first two are the dispersion relation of a non-rotating self-gravitating sheet, $\omega^2 = c_T^2k^2 - 2\pi G\Sigma|k|$, which Module 5 carries as its Problem D2 (module05.html:798) and derives there from the sheet potential $\Phi_1 = -(2\pi G\Sigma_1/|k|)\,e^{ikx-|k||z|}$; it is quoted here for $k \gt 0$, and in this module’s $c_T$ in place of Module 5’s $c_s$, which is the same isothermal sound speed. The self-gravity term is linear rather than quadratic in $k$ because the perturbation’s gravity is sourced by a surface density and not by a volume density. The third term is the rotational restoring force: Proposition 2 showed that a ring displaced radially at fixed angular momentum oscillates at $\kappa_{\rm ep}$, and an axisymmetric disturbance in the plane displaces rings radially, so it adds $+\kappa_{\rm ep}^{2}$ to $\omega^{2}$ exactly as a spring added in parallel adds to an oscillator’s squared frequency. That addition is stated and not derived here, because deriving it needs the linearised fluid equations in a rotating frame, which no module of this book writes; it is imported on the same terms as the magnetorotational instability of §11. Given (10.1), the right-hand side is a parabola in $k$ with a minimum at $k_* = \pi G\Sigma/c_T^{2}$, where $$ \omega^{2}(k_*) \;=\; \kappa_{\rm ep}^{2} \;-\; \frac{(\pi G\Sigma)^{2}}{c_T^{2}}. $$ Requiring this to be positive is $c_T\kappa_{\rm ep}/(\pi G\Sigma) \gt 1$, which is (10.2). At $Q = 1$ the minimum is exactly zero and the sheet is marginal at $k_*$ alone. ∎
The trivial limit, which the generator asserts. Set the rotation to zero. Then $\kappa_{\rm ep} = 0$, (10.1) becomes $\omega^2 = c_T^2k^2 - 2\pi G\Sigma k$, and that is the relation Module 5's Problem D2 derives (module05.html:798), unchanged — unstable for every $k \lt 2\pi G\Sigma/c_T^2$ and stable above it, with no wavenumber at which a non-rotating sheet is stable to all disturbances. In the same limit (10.2) gives $Q = 0$, which is $Q < 1$: always unstable. So rotation is the only thing in (10.2) that can stabilise a sheet at long wavelengths, which is exactly what module05.html:764 said it omitted. Setting a new term to zero and recovering the earlier module's result is the check the Module 12 editor pass asked every later dispersion-relation proof to carry; this is Module 11's.
The coefficient $\pi$ is derived above and is NOT Toomre's. Toomre's 1964 paper is about a disc of stars: his p. 1217 says he found it "a legitimate first approximation to ignore the interstellar gas and dust, and to concentrate here on the stability of a thin disk composed only of stars", and sends the gaseous case to a later paper. His own criterion, his eq. (65), is $\sigma_{u,\min} = 3.36\,G\mu/\kappa$ with $\mu$ the projected stellar density — the coefficient is $3.36$ and not $\pi$, a difference of 7 per cent. The criterion carries his name here because that is what it is called; the gas coefficient is this module's algebra, with Safronov's 1960 paper — which Toomre's own reference list carries — named as the precedent and not read.

11. What supplies the stress, and how big α is

Definition 2 named a number and bounded it. It did not say what physical process carries the stress, and for twenty years nobody could. Module 12 §9 is the answer: a Keplerian disc threaded by the weakest imaginable magnetic field is violently unstable, and the magnetorotational instability grows at $\gamma_{\max} = (q/2)\Omega$.

That section assumed $q = 3/2$ and said so. Proposition 1 derives it, so $\gamma_{\max} = 0.7500000000\,\Omega$ is now a result of this book rather than an input to it. The instability itself is not re-derived here: Module 8 §6.3 lists it among what Module 12 builds, and deriving it twice would make one of the two modules redundant.

Proposition 13 (the containment bound). If the magnetorotational instability's saturated field is identified with equipartition at a fraction $\alpha$ of the gas pressure, $B^2 \sim 4\pi\alpha\rho c_T^2$, then the fastest-growing wavelength of Module 12 §9 satisfies $$ \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{2H} \;=\; \frac{4\pi\sqrt{\alpha}}{\sqrt{15}}, \tag{11.1}$$ in which every disc property has cancelled. Proof. $v_A = B/\sqrt{4\pi\rho} = c_T\sqrt{\alpha}$ from the stated identification. The ratios $\kappa_{\rm ep}/\Omega = \sqrt{2(2-q)}$ are $2.000000$, $1.414214$, $1.000000$, $0.447214$ and $0.000000$. C2. For the protoplanetary configuration — $M = 1\,M_\odot$, $R = 10$ au, $T = 50$ K, $\mu = 2.34$, $\Sigma = 10\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-2}}$ — compute $c_T$, $\Omega$, $H$, $H/R$, $\rho_0$, and the viscous time at $\alpha = 0.01$.

Solution. $c_T = \sqrt{kT/\mu m_u} = 0.421497\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and $\Omega = \sqrt{GM/R^3} = 6.296043\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so $H = c_T/\Omega = 6.694628\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{cm} = 0.447508$ au and $H/R = 0.044751$. The midplane density is $\Sigma/\sqrt{2\pi}H = 5.959140\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$. With $\nu = \alpha c_T H = 2.821764\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$, $t_\nu = R^2/\nu = 7.931041\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{s} = 2.513195\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{yr}$. The $\alpha = 0.01$ is Hartmann et al.'s value for T Tauri discs at 10–100 au, quoted through King, Pringle and Livio Sect. 2.3.2, and this configuration sits at 10 au.
C3. Show that $T_{\rm eff}$ of (8.3) is maximised at $R = (49/36)R_{\rm in}$, and evaluate the maximum for the AGN configuration ($M = 10^{8}\,M_\odot$, $R_{\rm in} = 6R_g$, $\dot M = 1.3987\times10^{25}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$).

Solution. With $x = R/R_{\rm in}$, $T_{\rm eff}^4 \propto x^{-3}(1 - x^{-1/2})$. Setting the derivative to zero: $-3x^{-4} + \tfrac72 x^{-9/2} = 0$, so $x^{1/2} = 7/6$ and $x = 49/36 = 1.361111$. In gravitational radii that is $8.166667\,R_g$, where $T_{\rm eff} = 75\,112.74$ K. At $R_{\rm in}$ itself $T_{\rm eff} = 0$ exactly, because $f$ vanishes there.
D1. Derive the steady-state relation (8.1) from Proposition 3, stating where the zero-torque inner boundary enters.

Solution. See the proof of Proposition 8. The boundary condition enters once, in fixing the integration constant: $\mathrm{d}\Omega/\mathrm{d}R = 0$ at $R_{\rm in}$ makes the torque vanish there, so the constant equals $\dot M\sqrt{GMR_{\rm in}}$. Any other inner boundary — a magnetically connected star, for instance — changes that constant and with it the bracket $f$.
D2. Show that far from the inner edge the disc radiates three times the rate at which gravitational energy is released locally, and say where the excess comes from. Find the radius at which the two are equal.

Solution. The specific orbital energy is $-GM/2R$, so material moving inward through an annulus of width $\mathrm{d}R$ releases $GM\dot M\,\mathrm{d}R/(2R^2)$; the annulus has area $2\times 2\pi R\,\mathrm{d}R$ counting both faces, so the local release per unit area per side is $GM\dot M/(8\pi R^3)$. Against (8.2) the ratio is $3f(R)$, which tends to $3$. The ratios at $R/R_{\rm in} = 1.5$, $2$, $10$, $10^2$ and $10^4$ are $0.550510$, $0.878680$, $2.051317$, $2.700000$ and $2.970000$. Setting $3(1 - \sqrt{R_{\rm in}/R}) = 1$ gives $R = \tfrac94 R_{\rm in}$ exactly. Of the three units far out, one is released locally and two are carried outward to that radius by the viscous torque of Proposition 3 from material further in; inside $\tfrac94 R_{\rm in}$ the annuli radiate less than they release, because they are exporting energy rather than importing it.
D3. Derive (10.3) and show that $\Omega$ and $R$ cancel.

Solution. See the proof of Proposition 12. The cancellation happens because $\Sigma \propto \Omega/c_T^2$ in a steady $\alpha$-disc while $Q \propto c_T\Omega/\Sigma$, so the two factors of $\Omega$ divide out and no explicit $R$ survives except inside $f$.
K1. Compute the molecular viscous time of the protoplanetary configuration, and compare it with C2. Warning: the gas is molecular at 50 K, so the Coulomb mean free path of §3 does not apply. Use Module 1's order-of-magnitude neutral cross-section $\sigma \approx 10^{-15}\ \mathrm{cm^2}$.

Solution. With $n = 1.533623\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ the neutral mean free path is $\lambda = 1/(n\sigma) = 6.520507\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{cm}$, and $v_{\rm th}$ at 50 K with $\mu = 2.34$ is $0.672611\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so $\nu_{\rm mol} = \tfrac13 v_{\rm th}\lambda = 1.461922\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $t_\nu = 1.530828\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{s} = 4.850902\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{yr}$. That is $1.930173\times10^{7}$ times C2's answer, or $7.29$ decades. Using the Coulomb formula here would be meaningless: at 50 K in molecular hydrogen there are no free charges to deflect, and the formula's $(kT)^2/n e^4$ describes a process that is not occurring.
K2. Using Module 9's accretion rate and black-hole mass for Sagittarius A*, compute the luminosity a thin disc would produce and compare it with the observed X-ray luminosity. Check that your answer reproduces the efficiency of Proposition 10.

Solution. Module 9's generator gives $\dot M c^2 = 4.543\times10^{41}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$, so $\dot M = 5.054769\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$; its mass is $8.5502\times10^{39}\ \mathrm{g}$, so $R_{\rm in} = 6R_g = 3.809709\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{cm}$. Then $L = GM\dot M/2R_{\rm in} = 3.785833\times10^{40}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$, and $L/\dot Mc^2 = 0.083333 = 1/12$ as Proposition 10 requires. Against the observed $L_X = 2.0\times10^{33}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$ the ratio is $1.892917\times10^{7}$, or $7.28$ decades — the same refutation Module 9 prints as a factor of $2\times10^{7}$, reached by the luminosity route instead of the efficiency route.
K3. Find the value of $\alpha$ at which the magnetorotational instability's fastest-growing wavelength equals the full disc thickness $2H$, under the equipartition identification $B^2 \sim 4\pi\alpha\rho c_T^2$. Compare it with the measured values of §11, and say what the comparison is and is not worth.

Solution. By (11.2) the value is $15/(16\pi^2) = 0.094989$. The dwarf-nova range $0.1$–$0.3$ exceeds it by factors of $1.0528$ to $3.1583$; the protostellar $0.01$ is inside it, at $0.1053$ of it. So the crude identification is self-consistent in cold discs and marginal in hot ones — the same split CHECK 4 measures, which is suggestive and nothing more. Both sides are order-of-magnitude arguments: the saturated stress is not exactly $B^2/4\pi$, and $\alpha$ itself is measured to a factor of a few.
