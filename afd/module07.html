Module 7 — Rayleigh–Taylor and Kelvin–Helmholtz

Two fluids meet at a surface. If the heavy one is on top, the surface overturns; if they slide past each other, the surface rolls up. Both come out of one dispersion relation with four terms, and every section of this module switches one term on or off. The laboratory cases first — an upside-down glass of water, a jet stream — then the astrophysical ones, where the surface tension is gone and the gravity points the wrong way. Two checks: the non-linear mixing law against a laser experiment at the National Ignition Facility, and the linear theory against Kelvin–Helmholtz vortices seen on the flank of a coronal mass ejection.

1. One relation, two instabilities

Modules 5 and 6 asked whether a smoothly stratified fluid would stay where it was put. Gravity against pressure gave the Jeans criterion; buoyancy against an adiabatic gradient gave Schwarzschild's, and cooling against heating gave Field's. In all three the fluid varied continuously, and the question was about a gradient.

This module asks the same question about a surface. Two fluids of different densities meet across a plane, and the properties jump rather than slide. That is not a special case of the earlier work: a jump is where a gradient's length scale goes to zero, and the earlier criteria, which compare one length against another, have nothing left to compare. A separate calculation is needed, and it produces something the earlier ones did not — a growth rate that depends on the wavelength of the disturbance, so the interface picks a scale.

Two mechanisms live on such a surface, and they turn out to be two terms of one formula.

Rayleigh–Taylor. Put the heavy fluid on top. Every parcel of it that sinks and every parcel of the light fluid that rises lowers the potential energy of the pair, so the arrangement is unstable and the interface breaks into descending spikes and ascending bubbles. Nothing is flowing; only gravity and the density difference matter. §4 derives the terrestrial case, where surface tension cuts the instability off below a centimetre or two, and §5 removes the surface tension, which is what happens between two plasmas.

Kelvin–Helmholtz. Now let the two fluids slide past each other. A ripple on the interface forces the streaming fluid to go faster over its crest, which by Bernoulli lowers the pressure there, which lifts the crest further. Gravity is not needed and may be absent; what is needed is a velocity jump. §6 derives it, and finds that gravity, when it does act across the interface, kills the long waves rather than the short ones — the opposite end of the spectrum from surface tension.

The reason to treat them together is that one calculation gives both. §3 derives a single dispersion relation whose four terms are the Kelvin–Helmholtz drive, the Rayleigh–Taylor drive, the capillary restoring force and the magnetic tension. Setting the shear to zero leaves Rayleigh–Taylor; setting gravity to zero leaves Kelvin–Helmholtz; keeping both gives the mixed problem that a real astrophysical interface presents. The five limits of §3 check the relation before it is used on any case that has no closed form, which is the discipline Module 3 applied to its Lane–Emden integrator.

The two halves of the module are tested differently, and the difference is worth stating at the start. The Rayleigh–Taylor half is checked against a laboratory measurement — a laser-driven experiment at the National Ignition Facility — and that is the only place in this module where a standard deviation exists. The Kelvin–Helmholtz half is checked against a solar observation which quotes every input to one significant figure and gives no uncertainty at all. §9 says so in its first paragraph, and then shows what such an observation can still be made to yield: not a confirmation, but a bound on a magnetic field that nobody measured.

2. The interface, and what is perturbed

Everything in this module is a statement about the following configuration, and every hypothesis it carries is listed here once so that the propositions can name them rather than repeat them.

Definition 1 (the plane interface). Two fluids fill the half-spaces above and below the plane $z = 0$. The upper fluid has uniform density $\rho_t$ and streams with uniform velocity $U_t\hat{x}$; the lower has uniform density $\rho_b$ and streams with $U_b\hat{x}$. Write $\Delta U \equiv U_t - U_b$. Gravity is $-g\hat{z}$ with $g \gt 0$, so down is towards the lower fluid. The interface carries an interfacial tension $T_s \ge 0$. Each half-space carries a uniform magnetic field lying in the plane of the interface, of magnitude $B_t$ and $B_b$. Both fluids are incompressible, inviscid and perfectly conducting, and the interface is sharp: the density, the velocity and the field change discontinuously across $z = 0$.

The hypotheses that will later be paid for are worth naming now. Incompressible deletes the sound speed, so nothing derived here can contain a compressible effect — §7 quotes one from the literature precisely because this relation cannot produce it. Inviscid deletes the only dissipation, so the theory has no way to stop short wavelengths growing; §4 estimates where viscosity would take over and says plainly that it does not solve the viscous problem. Semi-infinite deletes every length except the wavelength itself, which is what makes the answer scale-free. Sharp is the assumption §9 finds broken in the observation it uses.

Definition 2 (the Atwood number). $$ A \;\equiv\; \frac{\rho_t-\rho_b}{\rho_t+\rho_b}. \tag{2.1}$$ With the convention of Definition 1 — $\rho_t$ above — $A \gt 0$ means the heavy fluid is on top, which is the Rayleigh–Taylor-unstable ordering, and $-1 \lt A \lt 1$ always. Shimony et al. (2022), whose mixing law §8 tests, write $A = (\rho_2-\rho_1)/(\rho_1+\rho_2)$ with $\rho_2$ the heavier fluid, so their $A$ and this one are the same number.
Definition 3 (the normal mode). Every perturbed quantity is written $$ q_1(\mathbf{x},t) \;=\; \hat q(z)\,\exp\!\big(i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_\perp + \sigma t\big), \tag{2.2}$$ with $\mathbf{k}$ a real horizontal wavevector of magnitude $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ and $\sigma$ complex. The mode grows if $\operatorname{Re}\sigma \gt 0$, and $\operatorname{Im}\sigma/k$ is the speed at which its pattern travels along the interface. Products of perturbations are dropped, which is the linearisation of Module 4 Definition 2 applied here.

Two things follow from Definition 3 alone and are worth having before any algebra. First, $\sigma$ has dimensions of inverse time, so $1/\sigma$ is an e-folding time and a growth is only meaningful against some other clock — the age of a supernova remnant, the duration of an observation. Second, because $\sigma$ will turn out to depend on $k$, the interface does not simply "go unstable": it goes unstable at some wavelengths and not at others, and the one that grows fastest is what an experiment or an image shows.

$\sigma$ is this module's growth rate throughout, and is module-local. From Module 6 a bare $\sigma$ elsewhere in this book is a collision cross-section; no cross-section appears in this module, so there is no collision in practice, and the notation table records the decision. $T_s$, not $T$, is the interfacial tension here, because $T$ is a temperature in §6 and §7 of this same module.

3. The dispersion relation

One proposition carries the module. It is derived once, checked against five limits that are known independently, and then used on cases that have no closed form.

Proposition 1 (the dispersion relation of a plane interface). For the configuration of Definition 1, with the field of each half-space making an angle $\theta$ with $\mathbf{k}$, and with $\mathbf{k}$ parallel to the streaming direction, the normal mode of Definition 3 has $$ \Big(\sigma + i\,\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\overline{\mathbf{U}}\Big)^{2} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{k^{2}\rho_t\rho_b\,\Delta U^{2}}{(\rho_t+\rho_b)^{2}}}_{\text{Kelvin–Helmholtz drive}} \;+\; \underbrace{A\,g\,k\vphantom{\frac{k^2}{(\rho)^2}}}_{\text{Rayleigh–Taylor drive}} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{T_s k^{3}}{\rho_t+\rho_b}}_{\text{capillarity}} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{k^{2}\big(B_t^{2}+B_b^{2}\big)\cos^{2}\theta}{4\pi(\rho_t+\rho_b)}}_{\text{magnetic tension}}, \tag{3.1}$$ where $\overline{\mathbf{U}} \equiv (\rho_t\mathbf{U}_t + \rho_b\mathbf{U}_b)/(\rho_t+\rho_b)$ is the density-weighted mean stream velocity. Writing $\sigma^2$ for the right-hand side, the interface is unstable to the mode $\mathbf{k}$ if and only if $\sigma^2 \gt 0$, and the pattern then travels at $\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\overline{\mathbf{U}}/k$. For an oblique $\mathbf{k}$, the proof below produces $\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\Delta\mathbf{U}$ wherever $k\,\Delta U$ appears, so every $\Delta U$ in (3.1) and in the sections that use it is the projection $\hat k\!\cdot\!\Delta\mathbf{U} = \Delta U\cos\varphi$. That is what §7 relies on, and it is stated here rather than assumed there.
In each half-space the perturbed flow is incompressible and, being started from rest relative to its own stream, irrotational: $\mathbf{u}_1 = \nabla\phi$ with $\nabla^2\phi = 0$. With the $\exp(i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_\perp)$ dependence of Definition 3, Laplace's equation reduces to $\hat\phi'' = k^2\hat\phi$, whose solutions decaying away from the interface are $$ \phi_t = C_t\,e^{-kz}\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_\perp + \sigma t}\ (z\gt0), \qquad \phi_b = C_b\,e^{+kz}\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_\perp + \sigma t}\ (z\lt0). $$ Let the interface sit at $z = \eta(\mathbf{x}_\perp,t) = \hat\eta\exp(i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_\perp+\sigma t)$. The kinematic condition is that a fluid element on the interface stays on it, $\partial_t\eta + \mathbf{U}\!\cdot\!\nabla_\perp\eta = u_z$, which linearises on each side to $$ (\sigma + i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{U}_t)\,\hat\eta = -kC_t, \qquad (\sigma + i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{U}_b)\,\hat\eta = +kC_b. \tag{3.2}$$ The dynamic condition is the pressure jump. Bernoulli's equation in each streaming frame gives the perturbation $-\rho(\partial_t + \mathbf{U}\!\cdot\!\nabla_\perp)\phi$, and the interface is displaced into a background whose pressure varies as $dp_0/dz = -\rho g$, so the total pressure perturbation evaluated at the displaced surface is $$ \delta p = -\rho\big(\partial_t + \mathbf{U}\!\cdot\!\nabla_\perp\big)\phi \;-\; \rho g\eta . \tag{3.3}$$ A crest bulges into the upper fluid and curves with its centre below, so tension raises the pressure on the lower side: $\delta p^{(b)} - \delta p^{(t)} = -T_s\nabla_\perp^2\eta = T_sk^2\hat\eta$. Substituting (3.3) on each side, evaluating $\phi$ at $z=0$ and eliminating $C_t$, $C_b$ with (3.2), $$ -\frac{\rho_b}{k}(\sigma + i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{U}_b)^2 - \frac{\rho_t}{k}(\sigma + i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{U}_t)^2 + (\rho_t-\rho_b)g \;=\; T_sk^2 ,$$ where every term has been divided by $\hat\eta$. Multiplying by $-k$, $$ \rho_t(\sigma + i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{U}_t)^2 + \rho_b(\sigma + i\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{U}_b)^2 \;=\; (\rho_t-\rho_b)gk \;-\; T_sk^3 . \tag{3.4}$$ Write $a_t = \mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{U}_t$, $a_b = \mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{U}_b$ and $\bar a = \mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\overline{\mathbf{U}}$. Expanding both sides of the identity $$ \rho_t(\sigma+ia_t)^2 + \rho_b(\sigma+ia_b)^2 \;=\; (\rho_t+\rho_b)(\sigma+i\bar a)^2 \;-\; \frac{\rho_t\rho_b(a_t-a_b)^2}{\rho_t+\rho_b} $$ shows it holds term by term in $\sigma^2$, $\sigma$ and the constant, the last by $(\rho_t+\rho_b)\bar a^2 - (\rho_ta_t^2+\rho_ba_b^2) = -\rho_t\rho_b(a_t-a_b)^2/(\rho_t+\rho_b)$. Substituting it into (3.4), dividing by $\rho_t+\rho_b$ and using $a_t - a_b = \mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\Delta\mathbf{U}$ gives the first three terms of (3.1), with the middle one $+Agk$ by Definition 2 — positive, and therefore destabilising, exactly when the heavy fluid is on top. For the field, ideal MHD adds the Lorentz force $(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})\times\mathbf{B}/4\pi$. A field lying in the interface and frozen into the fluid is bent by the ripple, and the bending pulls back like a stretched string. With $\mathbf{B}_1 = (\mathbf{B}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\boldsymbol\xi$ for an incompressible displacement $\boldsymbol\xi$, the restoring force per unit volume is $(\mathbf{B}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\mathbf{B}_1/4\pi = -(\mathbf{k}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{B})^2\boldsymbol\xi/4\pi$, and the perturbation occupies a depth $1/k$ on each side of the interface. Integrating over that depth turns the bulk force into a force per unit area of interface, so the field acts as an extra interfacial tension $$ T_{\rm mag} \;=\; \frac{(B_t^2+B_b^2)\cos^2\theta}{4\pi k}, \tag{3.5}$$ with $\theta$ the angle between $\mathbf{k}$ and $\mathbf{B}$. Adding $T_{\rm mag}$ to $T_s$ in (3.4) and dividing through as before contributes $-T_{\rm mag}k^3/(\rho_t+\rho_b)$, which is the fourth term of (3.1). Only the component of $\mathbf{B}$ along $\mathbf{k}$ appears, because a field perpendicular to $\mathbf{k}$ is carried sideways by the ripple without being bent. The step from a force per unit volume to a force per unit area is the one heuristic in this proof — it replaces the exact integral over the exponential profile by its scale height $1/k$ — and it is the step limit 4 of §3.1 checks, by recovering a published form of the magnetic term independently. ∎

Four terms, four physical effects, and each one is a section of this module. The two drives are positive and destabilising; the two restoring terms are negative. Their $k$-dependences are all different — $k^2$, $k$, $k^3$, $k^2$ — and that is the whole story of which wavelengths grow:

  • The Rayleigh–Taylor drive $Agk$ grows only linearly in $k$, so it is beaten at large $k$ by anything that rises faster.
  • Capillarity rises as $k^3$, so it always wins at short wavelength. It sets a short-wavelength cutoff. §4.
  • The Kelvin–Helmholtz drive rises as $k^2$, so it beats gravity at short wavelength and loses to it at long. Gravity therefore sets a long-wavelength cutoff on Kelvin–Helmholtz. §6.
  • Magnetic tension rises as $k^2$ and not as $k^3$, because by (3.5) the equivalent surface tension it supplies is itself proportional to $1/k$: the field is bent through a depth $1/k$, not confined to the surface. That puts it at exactly the same power of $k$ as the Kelvin–Helmholtz drive, and two terms with the same power of $k$ do not produce a cutoff at all. They produce a threshold on the amplitudes, one that either holds at every wavelength or at none. That is why §9 can turn the mere existence of the instability into a bound on the field, with no wavelength entering.

4. Rayleigh–Taylor in a glass of water

Switch off the shear and the field. Equation (3.1) becomes $$ \sigma^2 \;=\; Agk \;-\; \frac{T_sk^3}{\rho_t+\rho_b}, \tag{4.1}$$ which is the whole of terrestrial Rayleigh–Taylor. Two terms with different powers of $k$ must cross, and where they cross is the answer to a question anyone can ask in a kitchen: fill a glass with water, put a card over it, turn it upside down and take the card away. The water falls out. Put the same water in a drinking straw and it does not.

Proposition 2 (the capillary cutoff and the fastest mode). Under (4.1) with $A \gt 0$ and $T_s \gt 0$, write $\Delta\rho \equiv \rho_t-\rho_b$. Then $\sigma^2 \gt 0$ if and only if $k \lt k_c$, where $$ k_c \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{g\,\Delta\rho}{T_s}}, \qquad \lambda_c \;=\; 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{T_s}{g\,\Delta\rho}}. \tag{4.2}$$ The growth rate is largest at $k_{\max} = k_c/\sqrt3$, so that $$ \lambda_{\max} \;=\; \sqrt3\,\lambda_c \tag{4.3}$$ exactly, independently of $g$, $T_s$ and both densities.
Instability needs $Agk \gt T_sk^3/(\rho_t+\rho_b)$. Using $A(\rho_t+\rho_b) = \Delta\rho$ from Definition 2, this is $g\Delta\rho \gt T_sk^2$, which is (4.2); the $k$ on each side has cancelled, so the cutoff is where the two slopes balance, not where the rates do. For the maximum, differentiate: $d\sigma^2/dk = Ag - 3T_sk^2/(\rho_t+\rho_b) = 0$ gives $k_{\max}^2 = Ag(\rho_t+\rho_b)/(3T_s) = g\Delta\rho/(3T_s) = k_c^2/3$. Since $\lambda \propto 1/k$, $\lambda_{\max} = \sqrt3\,\lambda_c$. The factor $\sqrt3$ is the ratio of the two powers of $k$ in (4.1), $3$ and $1$, and nothing else enters. ∎

Water over air at $20\ ^\circ$C, with $\rho_t = 0.998207\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $\rho_b = 1.2041\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $T_s = 72.75\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-1}}$, gives $A = 0.997590$ — as close to the maximum $1$ as makes no difference, which is why the air's inertia can be ignored in every hand calculation of this problem. The capillary length is $\sqrt{T_s/(g\Delta\rho)} = 2.728$ mm, and

Key result (the upside-down glass). $\lambda_c = 1.7139$ cm and $\lambda_{\max} = 2.9686$ cm, at which $\sigma_{\max} = 37.15\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, an e-folding time of $26.9$ ms. A water surface held above air selects a $3.0$ cm wave and turns it over in $27$ milliseconds. Nothing shorter than $1.71$ cm grows at all.

That is the straw. A tube much narrower than $\lambda_c = 1.71$ cm admits no unstable mode, because the longest wave that fits across it is already inside the capillary cutoff, so the water stays in. The comparison is an order-of-magnitude one and is not exact: Definition 1's interface is unbounded, and a tube imposes a boundary condition at its wall whose lowest admissible wavelength is $\lambda_c$ only up to a geometrical factor of order unity that this module does not compute. What is exact is that the cutoff is a length, fixed by three material constants, and that it is of order a centimetre for water — which is why the demonstration works with a drinking straw and fails with a tumbler.

4.1 Where viscosity would take over, and why it does not here

Equation (4.1) has no dissipation in it, so it is silent about whether the fastest mode it predicts is the one a real fluid shows. A crude but sufficient test compares the inviscid growth rate against the rate at which viscosity damps a surface wave.

Proposition 3 (the viscous crossover, as an estimate). A free-surface wave of wavenumber $k$ in a fluid of kinematic viscosity $\nu$ is damped at the rate $2\nu k^2$. Equating that with the inviscid growth rate $\sqrt{Agk}$ gives the crossover $$ k_\nu \;=\; \left(\frac{Ag}{4\nu^2}\right)^{1/3}, \qquad \lambda_\nu = 2\pi/k_\nu. \tag{4.4}$$ Above $\lambda_\nu$ viscosity is a correction; below it, the inviscid rate is not the answer. This is a crossover estimate and not a solution. The viscous Rayleigh–Taylor problem is a quartic eigenvalue problem (Chandrasekhar 1961, ch. X) and is not solved in this module; (4.4) says only where the neglected term becomes comparable to the retained one.
The damping rate $2\nu k^2$ for a free surface is quoted, not derived: it follows from the viscous dissipation of an irrotational wave whose velocity field decays as $e^{-k|z|}$, and its derivation needs the viscous stress conditions this module's Definition 1 discards. Setting $\sqrt{Agk} = 2\nu k^2$ and solving for $k$ gives $k^3 = Ag/(4\nu^2)$, which is (4.4). ∎

For water, $\nu = 1.0034\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ gives $\lambda_\nu = 0.467$ mm against $\lambda_{\max} = 29.7$ mm, a factor $63.5$. Viscosity is irrelevant to the kitchen experiment, and capillarity alone chooses the pattern. Problem K1 does the same calculation for glycerol, where $\nu$ is a thousand times larger, $\lambda_\nu = 5.207$ cm exceeds $\lambda_{\max} = 2.458$ cm by $2.12$, and the ordering reverses: the same instability, with the opposite cut-off in charge.

4.2 Two fluids that mix: no capillary cutoff at all

Proposition 2 assumed $T_s \gt 0$. Interfacial tension exists only between fluids that do not mix. Pour salt water gently on top of fresh and there is a density difference but no skin: the two are miscible, molecules cross the boundary freely, and $T_s$ is essentially zero.

Three per cent NaCl by mass at $20\ ^\circ$C has $\rho = 1.0210\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ against fresh water's $0.998207$, giving $A = 0.011288$, some $88$ times smaller than water over air. With $T_s = 0$ equation (4.1) reduces to $\sigma = \sqrt{Agk}$, which has no cutoff and no maximum: every wavelength grows, and shorter ones grow faster.

The miscible case is the one that matters for the rest of the module, because two plasmas are always miscible.

5. Rayleigh–Taylor with no skin: the astrophysical case

Between two regions of ionised gas there is no interfacial tension. Setting $T_s = 0$ and $B = 0$ in (3.1) leaves the barest possible statement.

Proposition 4 (no cutoff, and no fastest mode). For two ideal fluids with $A \gt 0$, $T_s = 0$, $B = 0$ and no shear, $$ \sigma \;=\; \sqrt{A\,g\,k}, \tag{5.1}$$ which is positive for every $k \gt 0$ and increases without bound as $k$ increases. The linear problem therefore has no fastest-growing wavelength, and the shortest scale present grows first.
Immediate from (3.1) with three of its four terms set to zero. That $\sigma$ has no maximum follows because $\sqrt{Agk}$ is monotone in $k$ and the only term that could turn it over, $-T_sk^3/(\rho_t+\rho_b)$, has been discarded by hypothesis. ∎

Proposition 4 is a short statement with a long consequence, and it is worth spelling out because it is a standing hazard in this subject rather than an elegant result.

A simulation of this problem has no converged answer. The growth rate rises as $\sqrt{k}$, so whatever the smallest resolved scale is, that is the scale that goes unstable first, and refining the grid changes the answer rather than improving it. The cut-off must come from somewhere the ideal equations do not contain: from viscosity, as in (4.4); from a finite interface thickness, which §9 shows is the realistic case for an astrophysical boundary; or from the magnetic tension term of (3.1), which Module 12 takes up. What Proposition 4 establishes is that something must supply it, and that the ideal fluid model of Modules 2–11 does not.

5.1 A decelerating shell: where $g$ comes from when there is no star

Equation (5.1) needs a $g$. For a supernova remnant there is no external gravity worth the name at the relevant radius; the acceleration that matters is the deceleration of the ejecta by the material they sweep up. The ejecta are dense and the ambient medium ahead of them is thin, so in the frame of the decelerating shell the effective gravity points from the dense fluid towards the light one — which is the unstable ordering.

Proposition 5 (the effective gravity of a decelerating shell). Let a shell expand with $R(t) \propto t^{\,m}$. In its instantaneous rest frame the effective gravity across its outer face has magnitude $$ g \;=\; |\ddot R| \;=\; m(1-m)\,\frac{R}{t^{2}}, \tag{5.2}$$ which is positive — that is, the shell is decelerating — for every $0 \lt m \lt 1$, and vanishes at $m = 0$ and $m = 1$.
$R = R_0(t/t_0)^m$ gives $\dot R = mR/t$ and $\ddot R = m(m-1)R/t^2$. For $0 \lt m \lt 1$ the factor $m(m-1)$ is negative, so $\ddot R \lt 0$ and $|\ddot R| = m(1-m)R/t^2$. At $m = 1$ the expansion is free and unaccelerated; at $m = 0$ the shell has stopped. The maximum of $m(1-m)$ is at $m = 1/2$. ∎

Two standard cases bracket the range. The Sedov–Taylor blast wave of Module 8 has $m = 2/5$; a young remnant still driven by its own ejecta has $m \simeq 0.7$. Both are evaluated below, and the point of putting them side by side is that the answer barely moves.

6. Kelvin–Helmholtz, and the two ways gravity stops it

Now switch the shear on. With $T_s = 0$ and $B = 0$, (3.1) reads $$ \sigma^2 \;=\; \frac{k^2\rho_t\rho_b\,\Delta U^2}{(\rho_t+\rho_b)^2} \;+\; A\,g\,k. \tag{6.1}$$ If the light fluid is on top, $A \lt 0$ and the two terms compete. The first rises as $k^2$ and the second only as $k$, so the shear wins at short wavelength and gravity wins at long: the cutoff is at the opposite end of the spectrum from the capillary one.

Proposition 6 (the gravitational cutoff of Kelvin–Helmholtz). For two fluids with the light fluid above, $\rho_t \lt \rho_b$, no tension and no field, the mode $\mathbf{k}$ parallel to the shear is unstable if and only if $$ \Delta U^2 \;\gt\; \frac{g\,(\rho_b^2-\rho_t^2)}{k\,\rho_t\rho_b}, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad k \;\gt\; k_{\rm cut} \;=\; \frac{g\,(\rho_b^2-\rho_t^2)}{\rho_t\rho_b\,\Delta U^2}. \tag{6.2}$$ Every wavelength longer than $2\pi/k_{\rm cut}$ is stabilised. As $\rho_t \to \rho_b$ the cutoff wavenumber goes to zero and every wavelength is unstable at every shear, recovering limit 2 of §3.1.
With the light fluid above, $A = -(\rho_b-\rho_t)/(\rho_t+\rho_b)$, so (6.1) gives instability when $$ \frac{k^2\rho_t\rho_b\Delta U^2}{(\rho_t+\rho_b)^2} \;\gt\; \frac{(\rho_b-\rho_t)gk}{\rho_t+\rho_b}. $$ Dividing by $k$ and by $\rho_t\rho_b/(\rho_t+\rho_b)^2$ gives $k\Delta U^2 \gt g(\rho_b-\rho_t)(\rho_t+\rho_b)/(\rho_t\rho_b) = g(\rho_b^2-\rho_t^2)/(\rho_t\rho_b)$, which is (6.2). Setting $\rho_t = \rho_b$ makes the numerator vanish, so $k_{\rm cut} = 0$. ∎

The physical reading is worth keeping, because the sign of the effect is counter-intuitive the first time. A long wave displaces a large volume of fluid; a short wave displaces a small one. Gravity charges by volume and the shear pays by area, so the longer the wave, the worse the bargain. Long waves have too much heavy fluid to lift.

6.1 Clear-air turbulence: the Richardson number

Proposition 6 is a statement about a discontinuity. Real shear layers are continuous, and the continuous problem has a famous answer that is not a special case of Proposition 6 and must not be run together with it.

Quoted, not derived, and about a different flow. Miles (1961) and Howard (1961) proved that for a continuously stratified, inviscid, parallel shear flow, the condition $$ \mathrm{Ri} \;\equiv\; \frac{N^2}{(dU/dz)^2} \;\gt\; \frac14 \quad\text{at every height} \tag{6.3}$$ is sufficient for stability. Neither paper is in this folder and neither theorem is proved here. Two things follow from the logical form alone and are the traps. First, $\mathrm{Ri} \gt 1/4$ everywhere is sufficient for stability, so $\mathrm{Ri} \lt 1/4$ somewhere is necessary for instability and not sufficient for it: a flow with $\mathrm{Ri} \lt 1/4$ is permitted to be unstable, not compelled. Second, this is a theorem about a smooth profile, whereas Proposition 6 is about a jump; the two are different statements and this module does not translate between them.

$N$ in (6.3) is the Brunt–Väisälä frequency of Module 6 Proposition 2, unchanged, and $\mathrm{Ri}$ compares the energy it takes to lift a parcel against the energy available in the shear. The atmospheric case is the one everybody has met.

Near the jet stream, at $T = 220$ K with an environmental lapse rate of $6.0\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$, Module 6's expression $N^2 = (g/T)(dT/dz + g/c_p)$ for dry air gives $N^2 = 1.6764\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$, so $N = 0.01295\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and the buoyancy period $2\pi/N$ is $8.1$ minutes.

7. The compressible limit, quoted not derived

Definition 1 made the fluids incompressible, which deleted the sound speed. Nothing derived from (3.1) can therefore contain a compressible effect, and there is one that matters: a fast enough shear layer is stabilised by compressibility, and (3.1) cannot see it.

Quoted, not derived. Landau (1944) and Miles (1958) showed that a plane vortex sheet between two identical ideal gases is stable to two-dimensional perturbations once $$ \Delta U \;\gt\; 2\sqrt2\,c_s, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad M \equiv \Delta U/c_s \;\gt\; 2.8284. \tag{7.1}$$ Neither paper is in this folder; both are cited for this result alone, and no number is taken from either. The mechanism is that above that speed the disturbance radiates sound away from the interface faster than the shear can feed it. This module's (3.1) has no $c_s$ in it and therefore cannot contain (7.1). That is not a failure of the derivation; it is the hypothesis "incompressible" being paid for.

The sheet is not absolutely stable above $M = 2.8284$, and the escape is geometric. A mode whose wavevector makes an angle $\varphi$ with the flow direction sees only the projected shear $\Delta U\cos\varphi$, so its effective Mach number is $M\cos\varphi$. Oblique modes with

$$ \cos\varphi \;\lt\; \frac{2\sqrt2}{M}, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \varphi \;\gt\; \arccos\!\big(2\sqrt2/M\big), \tag{7.2}$$

therefore remain unstable at every Mach number (Miles 1958; Fejer & Miles 1963). The window narrows but never closes.

A jet of radius $R = 100$ pc moving at Mach $5$ through an ambient medium at $10^7$ K is a standard configuration, and it shows what (7.1) costs. With $\mu = 0.60$ and $\gamma = 5/3$ the ambient sound speed is $c_s = 480.6\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so $\Delta U = 5c_s = 2403\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. $$ \sigma \;=\; 7.7102\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}, \qquad 1/\sigma \;=\; 4.110\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{yr}. $$

The jet crosses $1$ kpc in $4.069\times10^{5}$ yr, so the incompressible theory says the interface has $0.99$ e-folding times in which to roll up over that length — marginal, and just interesting enough to believe.

It cannot be reached. $M = 5$ exceeds $2\sqrt2$, so by (7.1) the two-dimensional mode that $0.99$ e-foldings was computed for is stable, and the incompressible rate is an upper limit the flow does not achieve. What survives is the oblique modes beyond $\varphi = 55.6^\circ$, which see a projected shear of at most $\Delta U\cos\varphi \lt 1359\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and therefore grow more slowly still. Problem K3 walks a student into this and back out of it. The jet numbers are a configuration, not an object: $R = 100$ pc, $M = 5$, $\eta = 0.010$ and $T = 10^7$ K are round choices in the range these sources occupy, and no measured jet is being described. Nothing in this module is checked against them. The extragalactic jet is not taken up by any later module of this book; Module 10 treats turbulence, and Module 11 accretion discs.

8. After the linear theory: the mixing law, and the laboratory check

§5.1 ended with a count: a young supernova remnant is three to twelve e-folding times old at every wavelength from a hundredth of its radius to a tenth of it. Linear theory has therefore been exhausted long before anything is observable, and the quantity an image can measure — how far the two fluids have interpenetrated — is not something (5.1) predicts. A separate claim is needed for the non-linear stage, and it is a claim of a different kind: not a growth rate for each wavelength, but a single width for the whole mixing layer.

Definition 4 (the mixing width). $h_B$ is the distance the leading bubbles of light fluid have penetrated into the heavy fluid, measured from the unperturbed interface; $h_S$ is the corresponding penetration of the heavy spikes into the light fluid. The two are different numbers, and at large Atwood number $h_S$ is the larger, because a dense spike falling through a light fluid is less retarded than a light bubble rising through a dense one. This module uses $h_B$.
Proposition 7 (the self-similar mixing law). Assume (i) a narrowband multimode initial perturbation, (ii) a constant acceleration $g$ across the interface, and (iii) that the mixing layer has forgotten its initial condition — that is, that the only length in the problem is the width $h_B$ itself, so the dominant bubble wavelength satisfies $\langle\lambda\rangle = h_B/b$ for a pure number $b$. Then $$ h_B \;=\; \alpha_B\,A\,g\,t^{2}, \tag{8.1}$$ with $\alpha_B$ determined by $b$, by the drag and added-mass coefficients $C_d$ and $C_a$, and by the density ratio — and by nothing about how the layer was started, and nothing about $g$ or $t$.
Equation (8.4) below makes the dependence on the density ratio explicit, and it is a real dependence, not a pure number: with $C_a = 1$ and $C_d = 2\pi$, the three-dimensional values Shimony et al. adopt, (8.4) gives $\alpha_B = 1/(2 + 8\pi b)$ in the limit $\rho_1\ll\rho_2$ and $1/(2 + 4\pi b)$ at equal densities. Every comparison made in this section is between experiments and models at high Atwood number, so the comparison is consistent, but $\alpha_B$ is not a universal constant even inside the model that defines it.
A bubble of size $\langle\lambda\rangle$ rising at speed $u_B$ through the heavy fluid feels a buoyancy force per unit volume $(\rho_2-\rho_1)g$, a drag $C_d\rho_2u_B^2/\langle\lambda\rangle$ and an added-mass inertia $(\rho_1 + C_a\rho_2)\,du_B/dt$. The balance is $$ (\rho_1 + C_a\rho_2)\frac{du_B}{dt} \;=\; (\rho_2-\rho_1)g \;-\; C_d\rho_2\frac{u_B^2}{\langle\lambda\rangle}. \tag{8.2}$$ Hypothesis (iii) sets $\langle\lambda\rangle = h_B/b$ with $b$ constant, and $u_B = dh_B/dt$. Try $h_B = \alpha_B Agt^2$. Then $$ u_B = 2\alpha_BAgt, \qquad \frac{du_B}{dt} = 2\alpha_BAg, \qquad \frac{u_B^2}{\langle\lambda\rangle} = \frac{4\alpha_B^2A^2g^2t^2}{\alpha_BAgt^2/b} = 4b\,\alpha_B A g, $$ and every power of $t$ has cancelled from both sides. Substituting into (8.2), dividing by $(\rho_1+\rho_2)g$ and using $(\rho_2-\rho_1)/(\rho_1+\rho_2) = A$ from Definition 2, the common factor $A$ cancels and leaves $$ \alpha_B \;=\; \frac{\rho_1+\rho_2}{2(\rho_1 + C_a\rho_2) + 4b\,C_d\,\rho_2}, \tag{8.4}$$ which contains neither $g$, nor $t$, nor anything about the initial perturbation. It is the only power law that works: putting $h_B \propto t^{\,p}$ into (8.2) gives $du_B/dt \propto t^{\,p-2}$ and $u_B^2/\langle\lambda\rangle \propto t^{\,p-2}$, and both must balance a term independent of $t$, which forces $p = 2$. ∎

Hypothesis (iii) is the whole content of the proposition, and it is not a technical convenience. It is the assertion that a mixing layer eventually stops remembering the ripple it grew from — that after enough bubble mergers, the width alone sets the scale. Shimony et al. state it in those words: "We assumed that at the self-similar stage, the initial conditions are forgotten." Everything that follows tests it.

When $g$ varies in time, (8.1) generalises by replacing $Agt^2$ with the Read integral $$ x_{\rm Read} \;\equiv\; A\left(\int\!\sqrt{g(t)}\,dt\right)^{2}, \tag{8.3}$$ which for constant $g$ is $Agt^2$ exactly — a consistency the numbers script verifies to ten decimal places. Plotting a mix width against $x_{\rm Read}$ rather than against time is what allows experiments driven by different acceleration histories to be compared on one axis.

(8.3) is quoted, not derived. It is Read's (1984) prescription, reached here through Shimony et al.'s equation (1); Read's paper is not in this folder and no number is taken from it. What Proposition 7 derives is (8.1), at constant $g$, and the buoyancy–drag balance (8.2) has no power-law solution for a general $g(t)$. The one thing checked here is that (8.3) reduces to (8.1) when $g$ is constant.

8.2 Confirmed: the form of the law

What is confirmed is a functional form, not a number, and it is worth being exact about how far the evidence goes. The experiment fielded four initial conditions: a two-dimensional seed at $\langle\lambda\rangle = 30\ \mu$m, the same with a thinner ablator, a three-dimensional seed at $30\ \mu$m, and a two-dimensional seed at $15\ \mu$m. Each gives a mix-width history in time. Plotted against the Read integral (8.3), each becomes linear at late times once the Richtmyer–Meshkov and decompression contributions are removed, and the slope of that line is $\alpha_S + \alpha_B$. Linearity in $x_{\rm Read}$ is exactly (8.1); a layer still governed by its seed would not be linear in it at all.

The two seeds do not lie on one line, and this module does not say they do. The paper reports that the $15\ \mu$m seed "exhibit[s] the largest initial growth rate" and reaches the largest widths, that the $30\ \mu$m data "show a similar slope to the model result for $\alpha_B \sim 0.03$", and that the $15\ \mu$m case "offers a better constraint" and gives $0.038\pm0.008$. Its Fig. 4 draws separate model curves for the two seeds, with different initial amplitudes. So the two slopes are two measurements, $1.00\,\sigma$ apart, not one.
Confirmed. The form $h_B = \alpha_B A g t^2$, in the generalised version (8.3): four initial conditions, differing by a factor $2$ in seed wavelength and by dimensionality, each give a mix width linear in $x_{\rm Read}$ at late time. The form is what is confirmed; the coefficient is what is measured, and the two seeds measure it $1.00\,\sigma$ apart.

9. The anchor: Kelvin–Helmholtz on the flank of a CME

On 8 April 2010, beginning at 02:34 UT, SDO/AIA observed a flare and coronal mass ejection from an active region at $16^\circ$E, $29^\circ$N. The flare peaked at 03:25 UT at GOES class B3.7 and STEREO/SECCHI measured the ejection at about $500\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Along the boundary of one of the dimming regions left behind by the evacuated plasma, Ofman & Thompson (2011) identified a chain of vortex-shaped features, several to ten arcseconds across, travelling at $6$–$14\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ — the first reported observation of the Kelvin–Helmholtz instability in the solar corona in the extreme ultraviolet.

Say the weakness first, because it governs everything below. Every input this paper supplies is quoted to one significant figure with no uncertainty: the wavelength is "$\sim$7000 km", the interface is "1–3 pixels", the growth time is "on the order of 14 minutes", and the driving shear is "$\sim$20 km/s" and is explicitly an upper limit. No standard deviation can be computed from this source and none is claimed anywhere in this section. Every ratio below is a factor-of-two statement. This is a weaker anchor than Module 2's independently fitted exponents or Module 3's helioseismic boundary, and it is used because it is the readable observation that exists: Foullon et al. (2011), which does quote uncertainties, is not on arXiv and could not be read, and a number taken from a paper this folder does not hold is the rule that was broken in Module 2.

What such an observation can still be made to yield is the subject of this section, and the answer is stated in advance so that no check below has to carry more weight than it can.

What this anchor does and does not deliver. It delivers no confirmation of Proposition 1 against a measured number. 9.5 The sharp result: what the instability measures about the field

Their p. 8 states an assumption plainly: "For simplicity, we have assumed that the magnetic field has no parallel component to the interface in Equation 1." Undo it, and the observation stops being a consistency check and becomes a measurement — because for the vortices to have been seen at all, the magnetic term in (3.1) must have lost to the Kelvin–Helmholtz drive.

Proposition 9 (an observed Kelvin–Helmholtz instability bounds the field along $\mathbf{k}$). Under Definition 1 with $g = T_s = 0$, the mode $\mathbf{k}$ is unstable if and only if $$ (B_t^2+B_b^2)\cos^2\theta \;\lt\; \frac{4\pi\,\rho_t\rho_b\,\Delta U^{2}}{\rho_t+\rho_b}. \tag{9.2}$$ The wavenumber $k$ has cancelled: both competing terms of (3.1) go as $k^2$, so the condition holds at every wavelength or at none. In particular, with equal field magnitudes on both sides, $B_t = B_b = B$, $$ B\cos\theta \;\lt\; \Delta U\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\,\rho_t\rho_b}{\rho_t+\rho_b}}, \tag{9.3}$$ and with the field on one side only the same inequality holds with $4\pi$ in place of $2\pi$, giving a bound larger by $\sqrt2$.
Set $g = T_s = 0$ in (3.1) and require $\sigma^2 \gt 0$: $$ \frac{k^2\rho_t\rho_b\Delta U^2}{(\rho_t+\rho_b)^2} \;\gt\; \frac{k^2(B_t^2+B_b^2)\cos^2\theta}{4\pi(\rho_t+\rho_b)} .$$ Both sides carry $k^2$, which cancels; multiplying by $4\pi(\rho_t+\rho_b)$ gives (9.2). Putting $B_t = B_b = B$ makes the left side $2B^2\cos^2\theta$, and taking the square root gives (9.3); putting $B_b = 0$ makes it $B_t^2\cos^2\theta$ and replaces the $2\pi$ by $4\pi$. ∎

The hypothesis about where the field is matters at the $\sqrt2$ level, so both cases are printed rather than one. Ofman & Thompson's model puts a field on both sides, so (9.3) is the case matching their configuration; the one-sided value is the weaker and therefore the safer statement.

The instability existing at all bounds the field along $\mathbf{k}$. $B\cos\theta \lt 0.0675$ G for equal fields on both sides, or $\lt 0.0954$ G for a one-sided field, at $n_e = 3\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. The bound scales as $\sqrt{n_e}$: it is $0.1232$ G at $10^{9}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $0.0390$ G at $10^{8}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. Both are upper bounds twice over, because $\Delta U$ is itself an upper limit and the bound is linear in $\Delta U$.

9.6 Refuted: the vortex-sheet assumption

Their p. 7 justifies treating the velocity jump as a discontinuity because the layer is "much smaller than the wavelength": $a/\lambda = 800/7000 = 0.1143$, a factor $8.75$. But the parameter that decides whether a layer may be treated as a sheet is not $a/\lambda$; it is $ka$, because $k$ is the inverse depth over which the perturbation decays and $a$ is the depth over which the shear is spread. Those are the two lengths that must be compared.

$ka = 0.7181$, and over their own "1–3 pixels" it runs from $0.3906$ to $1.1718$. It is of order unity, not small. The vortex-sheet growth rate is therefore an upper limit, not an estimate: a finite layer both lowers the rate and closes the unstable band above $ka$ of order $1$.

9.7 A counterfactual, not a refutation: the geometry is load-bearing

Their p. 8 says the model is run "without gravity (since it is perpendicular to the plane)". That is correct and it is also the most consequential sentence in the setup. Suppose it did. 10. What this model captures, and what it misses

Each idealisation, and where it is repaid if it is repaid at all.

  • The Rayleigh–Taylor half of this module is never tested against an astronomical measurement. This is the largest gap and it is named first. The terrestrial result is derived in §4, the regime is changed in §5, and the non-linear law is checked in §8 — against a laser experiment. Nothing in this module compares a Rayleigh–Taylor prediction with a measured astronomical number. Foullon et al. (2011) and Hester (1996) were sought for that purpose and neither is readable in full from this folder, and a comparison built on a number this folder does not hold is the rule that was broken once in Module 2 and is not broken here. The nearest honest substitute considered was the mixing width of (8.1) against the gap between the contact discontinuity and the forward shock in a young remnant; it was rejected, because (8.1) is proved for a planar interface at constant $g$ and that gap is a spherical, thin-shell, time-varying configuration whose measured quantity is a position and not a mixing width. It is not repaid in Modules 1–10. Module 8 derives the expansion index $m = 2/5$ that §5.1 here borrows as a number, and checks it against a measured blast wave — but that measurement is Taylor's Trinity table, a terrestrial explosion, so it does not supply the astronomical Rayleigh–Taylor check this module lacks.
  • Incompressible. Definition 1 deletes the sound speed, so §7's stabilisation at $\Delta U \gt 2\sqrt2\,c_s$ has to be quoted from Landau and Miles rather than derived. Module 8's shock jump conditions are the compressible fluid, but the compressible stability problem is not treated anywhere in Modules 1–10.
  • Inviscid. There is no dissipation in (3.1), so Proposition 4's growth rate rises without bound and the linear problem has no smallest scale. §4.1 estimates where viscosity would take over and says explicitly that it does not solve the viscous problem; Chandrasekhar's viscous quartic is not solved in this book.
  • The interface is sharp. §9.6 finds this assumption broken in the one observation this module uses, at $ka = 0.72$, and declines to solve the finite-layer eigenproblem because the layer thickness is uncertain by a factor $3$. A finite layer lowers the rate and closes the unstable band at short wavelength; neither correction is computed here.
  • Linear stability only, for everything except §8. Propositions 1 to 6 and 8 to 9 say that a perturbation grows and at what rate. They do not say what it grows into. §5.1 shows the cost directly: a young remnant is already $3$ to $12$ e-folding times old at every scale of interest, so the linear theory has expired before anything is observable. Proposition 7 is this module's only non-linear statement, and it is a scaling law with a coefficient known to a factor $2$.
  • No magnetic field, except as a term that is switched off. The fourth term of (3.1) is derived and then set to zero everywhere but §9.5, where it is used backwards, to bound a field rather than to compute a rate. The consequence is stated as sharply as this module can state it: a hydrodynamic calculation of a coronal instability needs the wavevector to lie within a degree of perpendicular to a field the paper never measured. Module 12 is where that is repaid.
  • Uniform density on each side. Definition 1's half-spaces are homogeneous, so the only length in the problem is $1/k$. Real interfaces sit inside stratifications with their own scale heights, and when the scale height is comparable to $1/k$ the half-space solutions $e^{\mp kz}$ are wrong. Not treated anywhere in Modules 1–10.
  • Two dimensions of the three matter, and the third is only sampled. §7's oblique modes are the one place a genuinely three-dimensional wavevector appears, and there only through the projection $\cos\varphi$. The full three-dimensional problem, in which modes at different angles interact, is not treated.
  • The coefficient $\alpha_B$ is measured, not derived. Equation (8.4) expresses it in terms of $b$, $C_d$ and $C_a$, none of which this module computes; they are taken from the buoyancy–drag literature through Shimony et al. The factor $2$ in §8.4 is the price, and it is the module's closing number.
  • One absolute density is an input, not a measurement. The coronal $n_e = 3\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ of §9.1 comes from Module 1's census, because the observation gives only a ratio. Every quantity built on it carries its $\sqrt{n_e}$ scaling in print, which is the most this module can do about it.

Conceptual ↑ contents

C1. Water will not fall out of a drinking straw held vertically with its top sealed, but the same water falls out of a tumbler turned upside down. Explain the difference using Proposition 2, and give the diameter at which the behaviour changes. Then explain why the argument fails for mercury at a diameter of $1.20$ cm rather than $1.71$ cm.
Solution
By Proposition 2 the interface is unstable only to wavelengths longer than $\lambda_c = 2\pi\sqrt{T_s/(g\Delta\rho)}$. A tube of internal width $d$ cannot support a disturbance of wavelength longer than about $d$ — the wall pins the interface — so if $d \lt \lambda_c$ there is no unstable mode available and the surface is stable. For water over air at $20\ ^\circ$C, $\lambda_c = 1.7139$ cm. A straw is a few millimetres across and holds its water; a tumbler is several centimetres across, admits wavelengths beyond $\lambda_c$, and does not.
The number is material-specific because all three of $T_s$, $g$ and $\Delta\rho$ enter. For mercury, $T_s = 487\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-1}}$ and $\rho = 13.55\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, so $\lambda_c = 2\pi\sqrt{487/(980.665\times13.55)} = 1.2029$ cm and $\lambda_{\max} = \sqrt3\lambda_c = 2.0835$ cm. Mercury has a much larger surface tension than water, which would raise $\lambda_c$, but it is also $13.6$ times denser, which lowers it; the density wins, and the critical tube is narrower for mercury than for water. The general statement is that $\lambda_c \propto \sqrt{T_s/\Delta\rho}$, so it is the ratio and not either quantity that sets the scale.
C2. Three per cent salt water is poured carefully on top of fresh water. A student computes the cutoff wavelength from Proposition 2 using the surface tension of water, $72.75\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-1}}$, and concludes that nothing below $11.3$ cm can grow. What is wrong, and what actually cuts off the short wavelengths?
Solution
The error is the existence of $T_s$ at all. Interfacial tension is the energy cost of an interface between two fluids that do not mix; salt water and fresh water are miscible, molecules cross freely, and there is no such cost. The $72.75\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-1}}$ the student used is the tension of water against air, a different pair. For salt against fresh, $T_s$ is essentially zero, Proposition 2 does not apply, and (4.1) reduces to Proposition 4: $\sigma = \sqrt{Agk}$, with no cutoff and no fastest mode.
The arithmetic itself is right: with $\Delta\rho = 0.022793\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $T_s = 72.75\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-1}}$, equation (4.2) does give $\lambda_c = 11.336$ cm. Its wrongness is visible before any computation, because a capillary cutoff of $11$ cm would mean salt water could be held above fresh water in any beaker narrower than a dinner plate, which nobody has observed.
What actually cuts off the small scales is dissipation. With $A = 0.011288$ and $\nu = 1.0034\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$, the crossover (4.4) is $\lambda_\nu = 2.08$ mm. Molecular diffusion of salt smooths the interface as well, which the model of Definition 1 does not contain at all. Above $\lambda_\nu$ the growth rates are those of §4.2: $8.34\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ at $1$ cm and $2.64\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ at $10$ cm.
C3. A pilot is told that clear-air turbulence occurs when the Richardson number falls below $1/4$. Using the result quoted at (6.3), state precisely what is and is not implied, and explain why turbulence forecasts for a given flight are nevertheless poor even when the shear and stratification are known.
Solution
Miles (1961) and Howard (1961) proved that $\mathrm{Ri} \gt 1/4$ everywhere is sufficient for stability. Reading the contrapositive: if a flow is unstable, then $\mathrm{Ri} \lt 1/4$ somewhere. So $\mathrm{Ri} \lt 1/4$ is a necessary condition for instability and not a sufficient one. A layer with $\mathrm{Ri} = 0.186$ is permitted to be unstable; nothing in the theorem says it is.
Two further gaps separate the criterion from the forecast. First, the theorem is about a smooth parallel shear flow of an inviscid, continuously stratified fluid, and says nothing about when, where, or at what amplitude an instability that is permitted will actually appear — that depends on the disturbances present, which are not part of the theorem. Second, the numbers are small and the measurements are coarse. At $T = 220$ K with a $6.0\ \mathrm{K\,km^{-1}}$ lapse rate, $N = 0.01295\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\mathrm{Ri} = 1/4$ is reached at a shear of $25.9\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ per kilometre, or $2.6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ per $100$ m of altitude. Near a jet stream that is an ordinary value, met routinely and over large volumes; a criterion that is satisfied most of the time in the region of interest, and that only permits rather than compels, cannot localise a patch of turbulence a few kilometres across.
The honest summary for the pilot is that $\mathrm{Ri} \lt 1/4$ marks where turbulence is possible, and that the possibility is widespread.

Derivational ↑ contents

D1. From (4.1), derive the cutoff wavenumber and the fastest-growing wavenumber, and show that $\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_c = \sqrt3$ exactly. Then show that the same relation holds for any dispersion relation of the form $\sigma^2 = \alpha k^p - \beta k^q$ with $q \gt p \gt 0$, and give the general ratio.
Solution
The first part is Proposition 2: $\sigma^2 = 0$ at $Ag = T_sk^2/(\rho_t+\rho_b)$, giving $k_c = \sqrt{g\Delta\rho/T_s}$; and $d\sigma^2/dk = Ag - 3T_sk^2/(\rho_t+\rho_b) = 0$ gives $k_{\max} = k_c/\sqrt3$, so $\lambda_{\max} = \sqrt3\lambda_c = 2.9686$ cm against $\lambda_c = 1.7139$ cm.
In general, $\sigma^2 = \alpha k^p - \beta k^q$ vanishes at $k_c^{q-p} = \alpha/\beta$ and is stationary where $p\alpha k^{p-1} = q\beta k^{q-1}$, that is at $k_{\max}^{q-p} = p\alpha/(q\beta)$. Dividing, $$ \left(\frac{k_{\max}}{k_c}\right)^{q-p} = \frac{p}{q}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_c} = \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{1/(q-p)}.$$ With $p = 1$ and $q = 3$ this is $3^{1/2} = \sqrt3$, as required. The ratio depends only on the two powers of $k$, never on $\alpha$ or $\beta$, which is why $\sqrt3$ survives every change of fluid, gravity and tension. It also shows what to expect elsewhere: for the magnetic case of (3.1), where the competing powers are $p = 1$ and $q = 2$, the ratio would be $2$.
D2. Derive the Kelvin–Helmholtz gravitational cutoff (6.2) from Proposition 1. The derivation is the proof of Proposition 6. Setting $T_s = B = 0$ in (3.1) and requiring $\sigma^2 \gt 0$ with the light fluid above gives $k\Delta U^2 \gt g(\rho_b^2-\rho_t^2)/(\rho_t\rho_b)$, so $k_{\rm cut} = g(\rho_b^2-\rho_t^2)/(\rho_t\rho_b\Delta U^2)$ and every longer wave is stabilised.
The prominence as described is the wrong way up for Proposition 6. The gravitational cutoff does not apply at all: the interface is unstable outright, at every wavelength, whether or not there is any shear. At $\lambda = 1000$ km, $\sigma = \sqrt{Agk} = 0.04109\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so $1/\sigma = 24.3$ s, that is $0.41$ minutes. Recognising the sign before reaching for a formula is the whole of the problem.

K3. An extragalactic jet of radius $R = 100$ pc moves at Mach $5$ through an ambient medium at $10^{7}$ K with $\mu = 0.60$, with a jet-to-ambient density ratio $\eta = 0.010$. Using (6.1) with $g = 0$ and $k = 1/R$, compute the growth time and the number of e-foldings over $1$ kpc of travel. Then decide whether that number is attainable.
Solution
The ambient sound speed is $c_s = \sqrt{\gamma k_BT/(\mu m_u)} = 480.6\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ with $\gamma = 5/3$, so $\Delta U = 5c_s = 2403\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. The prefactor of (6.1) is $\sqrt{\rho_j\rho_a}/(\rho_j+\rho_a) = \sqrt\eta/(1+\eta) = 0.09901$, and $k = 1/R = 3.2408\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. Hence $$\sigma = k\,\frac{\sqrt\eta}{1+\eta}\,\Delta U = 7.7102\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}, \qquad 1/\sigma = 4.110\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{yr}.$$ The jet covers $1$ kpc in $1000\,\mathrm{pc}/\Delta U = 4.069\times10^{5}$ yr, so the e-folding count over that length is $4.069/4.110 = 0.99$.
It is not attainable. The calculation used (6.1), which comes from (3.1), which assumes incompressible fluids. At $M = 5$ the condition (7.1) for compressible stabilisation, $M \gt 2\sqrt2 = 2.8284$, is met with room to spare, so the two-dimensional mode whose rate was just computed is stable. The $0.99$ e-foldings is an upper limit the flow does not reach.
What survives is the oblique modes of (7.2): those with $\varphi \gt \arccos(2\sqrt2/5) = 55.6^\circ$, which see a projected shear of at most $\Delta U\cos(55.6^\circ) = 1359\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and therefore grow at most $0.57$ times as fast, giving at most $0.56$ e-foldings over the kiloparsec. The lesson is the one §7 states: a relation with no sound speed in it cannot tell you when the sound speed matters, and the check has to be made from outside it.
