Every module of this book so far has treated light as something a fluid emits and never as something that pushes on it. Module 1 §9 put that in its closing table in one line — "Photons neither carry momentum nor dominate the energy budget" — and named this module as the repayment. The destination is the Eddington limit, in §9. 1. Photons carry momentum

A photon of energy $E$ carries momentum $E/c$. A beam that delivers energy at a rate $F$ per unit area therefore delivers momentum at a rate $F/c$ per unit area, and a body that absorbs the beam feels a pressure $F/c$. For sunlight on the Earth that pressure is negligible. Inside a star the same accounting is not, and near a luminous compact object it decides whether gas can fall in at all. To do the accounting one needs to describe radiation that travels in every direction at once.

Definition 1 (the specific intensity and its moments). The specific intensity $I_\nu(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{n},t)$ is the energy carried in direction $\mathbf{n}$ per unit time, per unit area normal to $\mathbf{n}$, per unit solid angle and per unit frequency, in $\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}\,cm^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,Hz^{-1}}$. With $\theta$ the angle between $\mathbf{n}$ and a chosen axis $z$ and $\mathrm{d}\Omega$ the element of solid angle, its first three angular moments are $$ u_\nu = \frac{1}{c}\oint I_\nu\,\mathrm{d}\Omega, \qquad F_\nu = \oint I_\nu\cos\theta\,\mathrm{d}\Omega, \qquad P_\nu = \frac{1}{c}\oint I_\nu\cos^{2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\Omega, \tag{1.1}$$ the radiation energy density per unit frequency, the flux along $z$, and the flux of $z$-momentum across a surface normal to $z$ — the radiation pressure. The mean intensity is $J_\nu = (1/4\pi)\oint I_\nu\,\mathrm{d}\Omega = c\,u_\nu/4\pi$. Quantities integrated over frequency drop the subscript: $u_{\rm rad} = \int u_\nu\,\mathrm{d}\nu$, $F = \int F_\nu\,\mathrm{d}\nu$, $P_{\rm rad} = \int P_\nu\,\mathrm{d}\nu$.

The second moment is written $P_\nu$ and never $K_\nu$, which is what most of the transfer literature writes, because $K$ is Module 3's polytropic constant. That $P_\nu$ is a pressure is the whole point of the first proposition.

Proposition 1 (the radiation pressure of an isotropic field). If $I_\nu$ does not depend on direction, then $P_\nu = u_\nu/3$ and $F_\nu = 0$. In thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$ the integrated energy density is $u_{\rm rad} = a_{\rm rad}T^{4}$, so $$ P_{\rm rad} \;=\; \frac{u_{\rm rad}}{3} \;=\; \frac{a_{\rm rad}T^{4}}{3}, \qquad a_{\rm rad} \;=\; \frac{4\sigma_{\rm SB}}{c}. \tag{1.2}$$ The factor $1/3$ is derived; the law $u_{\rm rad} = a_{\rm rad}T^4$ is stated.
Write $\mathrm{d}\Omega = \sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi = -\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\phi$ with $\mu = \cos\theta$. For isotropic $I_\nu$ the three integrals in (1.1) are $\oint\mathrm{d}\Omega = 2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mu = 4\pi$, $\oint\mu\,\mathrm{d}\Omega = 2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\mu = 0$ and $\oint\mu^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega = 2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}\mu^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu = 4\pi/3$. So $u_\nu = 4\pi I_\nu/c$, $F_\nu = 0$ and $P_\nu = 4\pi I_\nu/3c = u_\nu/3$. Integrating over $\nu$ gives $P_{\rm rad} = u_{\rm rad}/3$. What is stated and not derived is the Stefan–Boltzmann law for black-body radiation, $\int B_\nu(T)\,\mathrm{d}\nu = \sigma_{\rm SB}T^{4}/\pi$, where $B_\nu(T)$ is the Planck function; this book does no statistical mechanics of photons. Given it, the energy crossing a plane from one side of an isotropic black-body field is $\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1 B_\nu\,\mu\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\mathrm{d}\phi = \pi B_\nu$ per unit frequency, which integrates to $\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4$, while $u_{\rm rad} = (4\pi/c)\int B_\nu\,\mathrm{d}\nu = 4\sigma_{\rm SB}T^{4}/c$. That is $u_{\rm rad} = a_{\rm rad}T^4$ with $a_{\rm rad} = 4\sigma_{\rm SB}/c$. ∎

The run evaluates $a_{\rm rad} = 7.565733\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{erg\,cm^{-3}\,K^{-4}}$ from the exact SI value of $\sigma_{\rm SB}$, which is the constant Module 3's notation table fixes with its subscript. The factor $1/3$ is a statement about geometry and not about photons. Its trivial limit is a parallel beam, $I_\nu \propto \delta(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{n}_0)$ along $z$: then $\cos\theta = 1$ wherever the intensity is non-zero, the three integrals in (1.1) are equal, and $P_\nu = u_\nu$. A beam pushes three times harder than an isotropic field of the same energy density. Problem C1 asks for both cases and for a third, a field that fills one hemisphere uniformly and is empty in the other, which also has $P_\nu = u_\nu/3$ — a fact that §5 uses at the surface of a star.

2. The transfer equation

$\nu$ in this module is a frequency, and never the kinematic viscosity. A reader who has just finished Module 11 will read $\nu$ as a viscosity, because Module 7, Module 10 and Module 11 all write it so. In this module the subscript in $I_\nu$, $\kappa_\nu$, $j_\nu$, $B_\nu$ and the bare $\nu$ in $\nu_{\rm max}$ are a frequency, in Hz, as in Module 4 §4's $\nu_{\rm max}$ and $\nu_{\rm ac}$. No viscosity occurs anywhere in this module.
Definition 2 (opacity, emissivity and the source function). A ray crossing a path length $\mathrm{d}s$ of gas of density $\rho$ loses the fraction $\kappa_\nu\rho\,\mathrm{d}s$ of its intensity to absorption and scattering together; $\kappa_\nu$ is the mass extinction coefficient, or opacity, in $\mathrm{cm^{2}\,g^{-1}}$. The same gas adds $j_\nu\rho\,\mathrm{d}s$, where $j_\nu$ is the emissivity, in $\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}\,g^{-1}\,sr^{-1}\,Hz^{-1}}$. Their ratio $S_\nu \equiv j_\nu/\kappa_\nu$ is the source function. In thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$, $S_\nu = B_\nu(T)$.

Opacity is never written bare $\kappa$ here. The bare glyph already has three meanings in this book — Module 1's thermal conductivity, Module 4's evanescent decay rate, and Module 12's $\kappa_\parallel$, $\kappa_\perp$ — so this module writes $\kappa_\nu$ at one frequency, $\kappa_{\rm R}$ for the Rosseland mean of §4, $\kappa_{\rm es}$ for electron scattering and $\kappa_{\rm F}$ for the flux mean of §9. And the emissivity is $j_\nu$ and not the $\eta_\nu$ of many texts, because module12.html:927 refuses a new bare meaning of $\eta$.

Proposition 2 (the transfer equation and its formal solution). In a static medium, along a ray with path length $s$, $$ \frac{\mathrm{d}I_\nu}{\mathrm{d}s} \;=\; \rho\left(j_\nu - \kappa_\nu I_\nu\right), \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}I_\nu}{\mathrm{d}\tau_\nu} \;=\; S_\nu - I_\nu, \tag{2.1}$$ where $\mathrm{d}\tau_\nu = \kappa_\nu\rho\,\mathrm{d}s$ is accumulated in the direction of travel from the point where the ray enters, $\tau_\nu = 0$. Its solution is $$ I_\nu(\tau_\nu) \;=\; I_\nu(0)\,e^{-\tau_\nu} \;+\; \int_0^{\tau_\nu} S_\nu(t)\,e^{-(\tau_\nu - t)}\,\mathrm{d}t. \tag{2.2}$$ The first form of (2.1) is stated; (2.2) is derived.
What is stated. The first form of (2.1) is energy conservation for the photons in a thin cylinder along the ray: what leaves one end equals what entered the other, less what the gas removed, plus what it emitted. It omits the term $(1/c)\partial I_\nu/\partial t$, which is negligible whenever the light-crossing time is short against the time on which the medium changes, and it assumes the gas is at rest. What is derived. Dividing the first form by $\kappa_\nu\rho$ and using $\mathrm{d}\tau_\nu = \kappa_\nu\rho\,\mathrm{d}s$ and $S_\nu = j_\nu/\kappa_\nu$ gives the second. Multiply it by $e^{\tau_\nu}$: the left side becomes $e^{\tau_\nu}(\mathrm{d}I_\nu/\mathrm{d}\tau_\nu + I_\nu) = \mathrm{d}(I_\nu e^{\tau_\nu})/\mathrm{d}\tau_\nu$, so $\mathrm{d}(I_\nu e^{\tau_\nu})/\mathrm{d}\tau_\nu = S_\nu e^{\tau_\nu}$. Integrating from $0$ to $\tau_\nu$ gives $I_\nu(\tau_\nu)e^{\tau_\nu} - I_\nu(0) = \int_0^{\tau_\nu}S_\nu(t)e^{t}\,\mathrm{d}t$, and multiplying by $e^{-\tau_\nu}$ gives (2.2). ∎

The trivial limit. For a uniform source function $S_\nu$, (2.2) integrates to $I_\nu = I_\nu(0)e^{-\tau_\nu} + S_\nu(1 - e^{-\tau_\nu})$. At $\tau_\nu \ll 1$ this is $I_\nu(0) + (S_\nu - I_\nu(0))\tau_\nu$: the gas barely changes the ray. At $\tau_\nu \gg 1$ it is $S_\nu$, whatever came in: a thick enough slab forgets the light that entered it and shows only its own source function, which is $B_\nu(T)$ in thermal equilibrium. That is why a star looks like a black body of roughly its surface temperature. When the extinction includes scattering, $S_\nu$ contains the scattered light and depends on $I_\nu$ itself, so (2.2) is then an integral equation and not a solution; §5 meets exactly that.

3. Optical depth

Definition 3 (optical depth and the photon mean free path). The optical depth is $\mathrm{d}\tau_\nu = \kappa_\nu\rho\,\mathrm{d}s$. In an atmosphere it is measured inward from the observer, with $\tau_\nu = 0$ at the top; along a ray in Proposition 2 the same increment is accumulated in the direction of travel. The photon mean free path is $\lambda_{\rm ph} = 1/(\kappa\rho)$, for whichever opacity $\kappa$ is in use.

The glyph $\tau$ is already the optical depth in this book: Module 6's notation table claims it "in $\tau = 2/3$ only", and this module takes it over in full. By (2.2) with no source, a ray survives an optical depth $\tau$ with probability $e^{-\tau}$, so the mean optical depth a photon travels before an interaction is $\int_0^\infty\tau e^{-\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau = 1$ and the mean distance is $\lambda_{\rm ph}$. In the ATLAS9 solar model of §6, at $\tau_{\rm R} = 2/3$, the photon mean free path is $84.2$ km.

Proposition 3 (the random walk). A photon that is scattered, or absorbed and re-emitted, in a random direction at every step, and that must cross a slab of optical depth $\tau \gg 1$, takes of order $$ N \;\approx\; \tau^{2} \tag{3.1}$$ steps to do it. This is an order of magnitude and is printed as one.
Let the photon take $N$ steps $\boldsymbol{\ell}_i$, each of length $\lambda_{\rm ph}$ on average and in an independent random direction. The net displacement is $\mathbf{d} = \sum_i\boldsymbol{\ell}_i$, and $\langle|\mathbf{d}|^{2}\rangle = \sum_i\langle|\boldsymbol{\ell}_i|^{2}\rangle + \sum_{i\ne j}\langle\boldsymbol{\ell}_i\cdot\boldsymbol{\ell}_j\rangle = N\lambda_{\rm ph}^{2}$, because independent random directions make every cross term average to zero. The slab has thickness $L = \tau\lambda_{\rm ph}$. Setting the root-mean-square displacement $\lambda_{\rm ph}\sqrt{N}$ equal to $L$ gives $N = \tau^{2}$. The factors of order unity that this sets aside — the spread of step lengths, whether the displacement is measured in one dimension or three, and the absorbing boundary — are what §4 fixes. ∎

The consequence is that a star is opaque in time as well as in intensity. The photon travels a total path $N\lambda_{\rm ph} = \tau^{2}\lambda_{\rm ph} = \tau L$, which is $\tau$ times longer than the straight line across. §4 turns this into a diffusion coefficient with its factor fixed.

4. The diffusion limit

Deep inside a star, where $\tau \gg 1$ over any distance on which the temperature changes, the radiation field is almost isotropic and almost black-body. The flux is then the small anisotropy that a temperature gradient forces on it, and it obeys a diffusion law. The one freedom is which average of $\kappa_\nu$ over frequency appears in it, and that is settled by a definition.

Definition 4 (the Rosseland mean opacity). $$ \frac{1}{\kappa_{\rm R}} \;\equiv\; \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{\kappa_\nu}\,\frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T}\,\mathrm{d}\nu}{\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T}\,\mathrm{d}\nu}. \tag{4.1}$$ It is defined so that Proposition 4 holds with a single opacity. It is a harmonic mean, so frequencies where the gas is transparent dominate it, and it is weighted by $\partial B_\nu/\partial T$, which is where the diffusing flux is carried.
Proposition 4 (radiative diffusion). In a plane-parallel, static medium with $\tau \gg 1$ over a temperature scale height, the frequency-integrated flux along $z$ is $$ F \;=\; -\,\frac{c}{3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\!\left(a_{\rm rad}T^{4}\right) \;=\; -\,\frac{16\sigma_{\rm SB}T^{3}}{3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}z}. \tag{4.2}$$ The assumption that carries the proof is the Eddington closure, $P_\nu = u_\nu/3$, which Proposition 1 made exact for an isotropic field and which is here applied to one that is only nearly isotropic.
With $\mu = \cos\theta$ and $z$ the coordinate normal to the layers, $\mathrm{d}s = \mathrm{d}z/\mu$, so the first form of (2.1) is $\mu\,\partial I_\nu/\partial z = \rho(j_\nu - \kappa_\nu I_\nu)$. Multiply by $\mu$ and integrate over solid angle. On the left, $\oint\mu^{2}\,\partial I_\nu/\partial z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega = c\,\mathrm{d}P_\nu/\mathrm{d}z$ by (1.1). On the right, the emission of a gas at rest is isotropic, so $\oint j_\nu\mu\,\mathrm{d}\Omega = 0$, and $\oint\kappa_\nu I_\nu\mu\,\mathrm{d}\Omega = \kappa_\nu F_\nu$. The first moment of the transfer equation is therefore exact: $$ c\,\frac{\mathrm{d}P_\nu}{\mathrm{d}z} \;=\; -\,\kappa_\nu\rho\,F_\nu. $$ Now apply the closure $P_\nu = u_\nu/3$, and use that at $\tau\gg1$ the field is black-body to leading order, $u_\nu = 4\pi B_\nu(T)/c$, so that $\mathrm{d}P_\nu/\mathrm{d}z = (4\pi/3c)(\partial B_\nu/\partial T)\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}z$. Then $$ F_\nu \;=\; -\,\frac{4\pi}{3\kappa_\nu\rho}\,\frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}z}. $$ The hinge is the order of the two operations. Integrating over frequency, $\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}z$ and $\rho$ come outside, and what is left inside is $\int(1/\kappa_\nu)(\partial B_\nu/\partial T)\,\mathrm{d}\nu$, which by (4.1) is $(1/\kappa_{\rm R})\int(\partial B_\nu/\partial T)\,\mathrm{d}\nu$. The remaining integral is $\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}T$ of $\int B_\nu\,\mathrm{d}\nu = \sigma_{\rm SB}T^4/\pi$, that is $4\sigma_{\rm SB}T^{3}/\pi$. Hence $F = -(4\pi/3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho)(4\sigma_{\rm SB}T^{3}/\pi)\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}z = -(16\sigma_{\rm SB}T^3/3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho)\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}z$, the second form of (4.2). Since $a_{\rm rad} = 4\sigma_{\rm SB}/c$, $\mathrm{d}(a_{\rm rad}T^4)/\mathrm{d}z = (16\sigma_{\rm SB}T^{3}/c)\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}z$, which turns the second form into the first. ∎

The trivial limit is an isothermal slab. With $\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}z = 0$, (4.2) gives $F = 0$ at any opacity: a region at one temperature is filled with isotropic black-body radiation, and by Proposition 1 an isotropic field carries no flux. The other check is on the weighting. Had one averaged $\kappa_\nu$ directly instead of $1/\kappa_\nu$, a single opaque line would dominate the mean and choke the flux, which is the opposite of what happens: radiation leaks out through the transparent windows, and the harmonic mean (4.1) says so.

Proposition 5 (the two diffusivities). Written as $F = -D\,\mathrm{d}u_{\rm rad}/\mathrm{d}z$, (4.2) has photon diffusivity $$ D \;=\; \frac{c\,\lambda_{\rm ph}}{3}, \qquad \lambda_{\rm ph} = \frac{1}{\kappa_{\rm R}\rho}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad t_{\rm diff} \;\sim\; \frac{R^{2}}{D} \;=\; \frac{3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho R^{2}}{c}. \tag{4.3}$$ Written as a heat flux in a gas of specific heat $c_P$ at constant pressure, the same law is a diffusion of temperature with diffusivity $$ \chi \;=\; \frac{16\sigma_{\rm SB}T^{3}}{3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho^{2}c_P}. \tag{4.4}$$ The time $R^2/D$ is an order of magnitude and is printed as one.
With $u_{\rm rad} = a_{\rm rad}T^4$, the first form of (4.2) reads $F = -(c/3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho)\,\mathrm{d}u_{\rm rad}/\mathrm{d}z$, so $D = c/(3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho) = c\lambda_{\rm ph}/3$. For a diffusion equation $\partial u/\partial t = D\,\partial^{2}u/\partial z^{2}$, a disturbance of size $R$ decays on a time of order $R^{2}/D$; the exact coefficient depends on the shape of the disturbance, which is why this is an order of magnitude. Substituting $D$ gives $3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho R^{2}/c$. For (4.4), write the second form of (4.2) as $F = -K_{\rm rad}\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}z$ with conductivity $K_{\rm rad} = 16\sigma_{\rm SB}T^3/(3\kappa_{\rm R}\rho)$. Energy conservation in a gas heated only by the divergence of this flux at constant pressure is $\rho c_P\,\partial T/\partial t = -\partial F/\partial z = \partial(K_{\rm rad}\,\partial T/\partial z)/\partial z$, which for locally uniform $K_{\rm rad}$ is a diffusion equation for $T$ with $\chi = K_{\rm rad}/(\rho c_P)$, which is (4.4). ∎

The factor $3$ in $t_{\rm diff}$ is the $1/3$ of Proposition 4, and it is the same $1/3$ as in Module 1 §6's kinetic-theory transport coefficients, $\mu \simeq \tfrac13\rho v\lambda$ (module01.html:363): a flux carried by carriers of speed $v$ and free path $\lambda$ has diffusivity $\tfrac13 v\lambda$, and for photons $v = c$. Proposition 3's random walk had no such factor, because it did not fix the geometry; this is the factor it set aside. The step-1 version of this module's generator carried the $3$ as "the standard three-dimensional correction", with no source; Gate D ruled that it be derived rather than sourced, and it is derived here.

$\chi$ is the diffusivity that Module 4 §7 named "the radiative diffusivity of the stellar gas" and did not evaluate. §7 evaluates it.

5. The grey atmosphere

At the surface of a star the diffusion law must fail, because $\tau$ goes to zero and the radiation field becomes one-sided. The simplest model that carries the radiation from the diffusion regime out to the surface is a grey atmosphere, in which $\kappa_\nu$ does not depend on frequency, in radiative equilibrium, in which every layer emits exactly the energy it absorbs so that the flux is constant with depth. That model has a closed-form temperature profile under the Eddington closure.

Proposition 6 (Eddington's grey atmosphere). A plane-parallel grey atmosphere in radiative equilibrium and local thermal equilibrium, under the Eddington closure $P_{\rm rad} = u_{\rm rad}/3$ and with no radiation incident from outside, carrying the flux $F = \sigma_{\rm SB}T_{\rm eff}^{4}$, has $$ T^{4}(\tau) \;=\; \tfrac34\,T_{\rm eff}^{4}\left(\tau + \tfrac23\right), \tag{5.1}$$ so that $T = T_{\rm eff}$ at $\tau = 2/3$. The exact grey solution, without the closure, replaces $2/3$ by the Hopf function $q_{\rm H}(\tau)$; that replacement is stated and not derived.
Let $\tau$ increase inward, $\mathrm{d}\tau = -\kappa\rho\,\mathrm{d}z$ with $z$ outward, and $\mu = \cos\theta$ from the outward normal. The transfer equation (2.1) reads $\mu\,\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}\tau = I - S$, frequency-integrated because $\kappa$ is grey. Integrate over solid angle: $\mathrm{d}F/\mathrm{d}\tau = 4\pi(J - S)$. Radiative equilibrium says $F$ is constant, so $J = S$ at every depth. Multiply by $\mu$ and integrate: $c\,\mathrm{d}P_{\rm rad}/\mathrm{d}\tau = F$, so $P_{\rm rad} = F\tau/c + C$. The closure $P_{\rm rad} = u_{\rm rad}/3 = 4\pi J/3c$ converts this into $J(\tau) = (3F/4\pi)\tau + 3cC/4\pi$. The hinge is the surface condition, which fixes $C$. At $\tau = 0$ nothing comes in, and the Eddington approximation takes the outgoing intensity as uniform over the outward hemisphere, $I = I_0$ for $\mu \gt 0$ and $I = 0$ for $\mu \lt 0$. Then $J(0) = I_0/2$ and $F = 2\pi\int_0^1 I_0\mu\,\mathrm{d}\mu = \pi I_0$, so $J(0) = F/2\pi$. (Such a hemispheric field also has $P_{\rm rad} = u_{\rm rad}/3$, by Problem C1, so the surface condition does not contradict the closure.) Hence $J(\tau) = (3F/4\pi)(\tau + 2/3)$. In local thermal equilibrium $S = \int B_\nu\,\mathrm{d}\nu = \sigma_{\rm SB}T^{4}/\pi$, and $J = S$ gives $\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4/\pi = (3\sigma_{\rm SB}T_{\rm eff}^4/4\pi)(\tau + 2/3)$, which is (5.1). At $\tau = 2/3$ the bracket is $4/3$ and $T = T_{\rm eff}$. What is stated: the exact solution of $J = \Lambda[J]$, the integral equation that (2.2) becomes when $S = J$, is $J = (3F/4\pi)(\tau + q_{\rm H}(\tau))$; this module computes $q_{\rm H}$ numerically and does not derive it. ∎

The trivial limit is $\tau \to \infty$, and it closes a loop. Deep in the atmosphere (5.1) gives $\mathrm{d}T^4/\mathrm{d}\tau = \tfrac34T_{\rm eff}^4$. Put that into Proposition 4 in its optical-depth form, $F = (c/3)\,\mathrm{d}(a_{\rm rad}T^4)/\mathrm{d}\tau$: the flux is $(ca_{\rm rad}/3)(\tfrac34T_{\rm eff}^4) = (ca_{\rm rad}/4)T_{\rm eff}^4 = \sigma_{\rm SB}T_{\rm eff}^4$ exactly, because $ca_{\rm rad} = 4\sigma_{\rm SB}$. The grey atmosphere at depth is the diffusion law carrying its own flux. The run evaluates the ratio as $1.000000$ (Problem D2).

The Hopf function differs from $2/3$ by little. The run solves the exact grey problem and gets $q_{\rm H}(0) = 0.57730$ against the known $1/\sqrt3 = 0.57735$, and $q_{\rm H}(2/3) = 0.68876$, which moves $T$ at $\tau = 2/3$ by $0.412$ per cent. The closure is a good approximation everywhere except the very top.

6. The anchor: the opacity of the solar photosphere

Module 6 §6 built the solar photosphere from convection: a mixing length, the Schwarzschild criterion, and the flux the Sun must carry. It computed a pressure scale height $H = 142.9$ km and a density $\rho = 3.0631\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and never wrote down an opacity. Yet the photosphere is defined by one: it is the layer at optical depth $2/3$. Those two numbers therefore imply an opacity, and the implication can be checked against a published model that neither module was built from.

The inference. In an isothermal layer the density falls as $e^{-z/H}$, so the mass column above a level of density $\rho$ is $\int\rho\,\mathrm{d}z = \rho H$ exactly. If $\kappa$ is the same all the way up, the optical depth of that column is $\tau = \kappa\rho H$, and demanding $\tau = 2/3$ gives

$$ \kappa \;=\; \frac{2/3}{\rho H} \;=\; 0.15231\ \mathrm{cm^{2}\,g^{-1}}. \tag{6.1}$$

Why the photosphere is not electron-scattering

Electron scattering is the one opacity with no atomic physics in it: every free electron scatters with the Thomson cross-section $\sigma_{\rm T}$, whatever its energy (for $kT \ll m_{\rm e}c^2$). A fully ionised gas of hydrogen mass fraction $X$, the rest helium, has $n_{\rm e} = (\rho/2m_{\rm p})(1+X)$ electrons per unit volume — one per hydrogen nucleus, two per helium nucleus of mass $4m_{\rm p}$ — so

$$ \kappa_{\rm es} \;=\; \frac{\sigma_{\rm T}n_{\rm e}}{\rho} \;=\; \frac{\sigma_{\rm T}}{2m_{\rm p}}(1+X). \tag{6.2}$$

At $X = 1$ this is $\sigma_{\rm T}/m_{\rm p} = 0.39773\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$, and at the Sun's present surface composition, $X = 0.7583$, it is $0.34966$. (The round formula $0.2(1+X)$ gives $0.3517$, which differs by $0.572$ per cent.) Heavier elements, fully ionised, contribute about one electron per two nucleon masses, like helium, so (6.2) holds for them too.

The photosphere is not fully ionised, and that, not the size of its opacity, is why electron scattering does not set it. At $5910$ K hydrogen is almost entirely neutral, and ATLAS9's own electron density at $\tau_{\rm R} = 2/3$ is $2.4815\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, supplied mostly by easily ionised metals. With those electrons, $\sigma_{\rm T}n_{\rm e}/\rho = 6.1780\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$ — $5659.8$ times below the fully ionised value, and $7198.5$ times below ATLAS9's $\kappa_{\rm R}$. The opacity that fills that gap is bound–free and free–free absorption by the negative hydrogen ion, H⁻, a hydrogen atom that has captured one of those scarce electrons. This book does no atomic physics, and H⁻ is refused here: its cross-sections are what ATLAS9 contains and what this module borrows.

That argument does not survive CHECK 2, because ATLAS9's $\kappa_{\rm R} = 0.4447$ lies above $0.34966$. The electron-density argument above replaces it; the run prints both and says which stands.

7. Heat that leaks out of a sound wave

Module 4 §7 derived the Sun's acoustic modes on the assumption that every compression is adiabatic, and then wrote: "By (3.4) it requires $\omega\chi/c^2\ll1$, with $\chi$ now the radiative diffusivity of the stellar gas. That criterion is not evaluated for the Sun in this module … the size of the failure is a subject of Module 13" (module04.html:429). It is the book's one explicitly deferred computation. In Module 4's criterion $c$ is the sound speed; in this module $c$ is the speed of light, so the sound speed is written $c_{\rm s}$ and the criterion is $\omega\chi/c_{\rm s}^2 \ll 1$.

Proposition 7 (the criterion is a ratio of two times). For a sound wave of cyclic frequency $\nu$, angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi\nu$, speed $c_{\rm s}$ and wavelength $c_{\rm s}/\nu$, in a gas of thermal diffusivity $\chi$, $$ \frac{\omega\chi}{c_{\rm s}^{2}} \;=\; \frac{2\pi}{t_{\rm diff}/\mathcal{P}}, \tag{7.1}$$ where $t_{\rm diff} = (c_{\rm s}/\nu)^2/\chi$ is the time for heat to diffuse across one wavelength and $\mathcal{P} = 1/\nu$ is the period. Adiabatic means $t_{\rm diff}/\mathcal{P} \gg 2\pi$.
$t_{\rm diff}/\mathcal{P} = [(c_{\rm s}/\nu)^2/\chi]\,\nu = c_{\rm s}^{2}/(\nu\chi)$. Then $2\pi/(t_{\rm diff}/\mathcal{P}) = 2\pi\nu\chi/c_{\rm s}^{2} = \omega\chi/c_{\rm s}^2$. ∎

So the criterion is not about a scale height at all. It asks whether heat has time to diffuse across a wavelength during one oscillation. If it does not, the compressions keep their heat and the wave is adiabatic; if it does, temperature differences smooth out within a cycle and the wave approaches isothermal. At order unity it is neither, and the wave loses energy to the radiation every cycle. The run checks (7.1) numerically to $10^{-9}$.

The inputs are $\nu_{\rm max} = 3090\ \mu$Hz, so $\omega = 1.941504\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$; Module 4's photospheric sound speed $c_{\rm s} = 8.0810\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$; Module 6's $c_P = 1.6968\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$; and $\kappa_{\rm R}$, $\rho$ and $T$ from ATLAS9 at two levels. By (4.4), $\chi = 1.5027\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$ at $T = T_{\rm eff}$ and $1.1587\times10^{13}$ at $\tau_{\rm R} = 2/3$.

8. A gas that contains radiation

Module 2 §11 listed $\gamma = 5/3$ among its idealisations and said it is wrong "for radiation-dominated interiors where $\gamma\to4/3$" (module02.html:688). This section pays that debt with the general statement: the adiabatic index of a mixture of an ideal monatomic gas and black-body radiation, at any gas fraction.

Let the total pressure be $P = P_{\rm gas} + P_{\rm rad}$ with $P_{\rm gas} = \rho k_BT/(\mu m_u)$ and $P_{\rm rad} = a_{\rm rad}T^{4}/3$, and let $\beta \equiv P_{\rm gas}/P$, Module 3's meaning exactly. The specific internal energy is written $e$, as in Module 2, and it is the gas's $\tfrac32P_{\rm gas}/\rho$ plus the radiation's $u_{\rm rad}/\rho$.

Proposition 8 (the first adiabatic index of a gas-plus-radiation mixture). With $e = 3P_{\rm gas}/(2\rho) + a_{\rm rad}T^{4}/\rho$, the adiabatic index $\Gamma_1 \equiv (\partial\ln P/\partial\ln\rho)_{\rm ad}$ is $$ \Gamma_1 \;=\; \beta \;+\; \frac{(4-3\beta)^{2}(\gamma-1)}{\beta + 12(\gamma-1)(1-\beta)}, \qquad \gamma = \tfrac53, \tag{8.1}$$ which is $5/3$ at $\beta = 1$ and $4/3$ at $\beta = 0$.
Work per unit mass with $v = 1/\rho$ and write $\mathcal{R} = k_B/(\mu m_u)$, so $P_{\rm gas} = \mathcal{R}T/v$ and $e = \tfrac32\mathcal{R}T + a_{\rm rad}T^{4}v$. The first law for an adiabatic change is $\mathrm{d}e + P\,\mathrm{d}v = 0$. Differentiating $e$, $$ \left(\tfrac32\mathcal{R} + 4a_{\rm rad}T^{3}v\right)\mathrm{d}T \;+\; \left(a_{\rm rad}T^{4} + \frac{\mathcal{R}T}{v} + \frac{a_{\rm rad}T^{4}}{3}\right)\mathrm{d}v \;=\; 0. $$ Express every term through $P$ and $\beta$: $\mathcal{R}T/v = \beta P$ and $a_{\rm rad}T^4 = 3(1-\beta)P$. The first bracket times $\mathrm{d}T$ is $Pv\,(\mathrm{d}T/T)\left[\tfrac32\beta + 12(1-\beta)\right]$, and the second bracket is $P\left[3(1-\beta) + \beta + (1-\beta)\right] = P(4 - 3\beta)$. With $\mathrm{d}v/v = -\mathrm{d}\ln\rho$, $$ \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln T}{\mathrm{d}\ln\rho}\right|_{\rm ad} \;=\; \frac{4-3\beta}{\tfrac32\beta + 12(1-\beta)} \;=\; \frac{(4-3\beta)(\gamma-1)}{\beta + 12(\gamma-1)(1-\beta)}, $$ the second form multiplying top and bottom by $\gamma - 1 = 2/3$. Now differentiate the pressure itself: $\mathrm{d}P = P_{\rm gas}(\mathrm{d}\ln\rho + \mathrm{d}\ln T) + 4P_{\rm rad}\,\mathrm{d}\ln T$, so $$ \mathrm{d}\ln P \;=\; \beta\,\mathrm{d}\ln\rho \;+\; (4 - 3\beta)\,\mathrm{d}\ln T. $$ Dividing by $\mathrm{d}\ln\rho$ along the adiabat and substituting the previous line gives (8.1). Both limits. At $\beta = 1$ the fraction is $1\cdot(\gamma-1)/1$, so $\Gamma_1 = 1 + (\gamma - 1) = \gamma = 5/3$. At $\beta = 0$ it is $16(\gamma-1)/[12(\gamma-1)] = 4/3$, and $\Gamma_1 = 4/3$. ∎

(8.1) is Chandrasekhar's eq. (131), p. 57, of his 1939 Introduction to the Study of Stellar Structure, and he states both limits under it himself. His Table 1, p. 59, tabulates $\Gamma_1$ for $\gamma = 5/3$ at $1 - \beta = 0, 0.1, \ldots, 1$ to three decimals, and the run reproduces all eleven rows to $3.44\times10^{-4}$, inside the $5\times10^{-4}$ that three printed decimals allow. Selected values: $\Gamma_1 = 1.650997$ at $\beta = 0.99$, $1.562745$ at $0.9$, $1.425926$ at $0.5$ and $1.350228$ at $0.1$. Radiation lowers $\Gamma_1$ quickly: a radiation share of one per cent, $\beta = 0.99$, already takes it from $1.666667$ to $1.650997$.

Proposition 9 (where radiation pressure equals gas pressure). At temperature $T$, $P_{\rm rad} = P_{\rm gas}$ at the density $$ \rho_{\rm eq}(T) \;=\; \frac{a_{\rm rad}\,\mu m_u\,T^{3}}{3k_B}, \tag{8.2}$$ and radiation dominates the pressure where $\rho \lt \rho_{\rm eq}(T)$. At any point, $\rho/\rho_{\rm eq}(T) = \beta/(1-\beta)$.
Setting $\rho k_BT/(\mu m_u) = a_{\rm rad}T^4/3$ and solving for $\rho$ gives (8.2). Finally $P_{\rm gas}/P_{\rm rad} = \rho/\rho_{\rm eq}$ by the same proportionality, and $P_{\rm gas}/P_{\rm rad} = \beta/(1-\beta)$ by the definition of $\beta$. ∎

9. The Eddington limit

Now the destination. A source of luminosity $L$ and mass $M$ sits at the centre, and ionised gas surrounds it. Gravity pulls each gram inward. The radiation streaming outward pushes each gram outward, by the momentum it deposits. Both forces fall as $1/r^2$.

Proposition 10 (the Eddington luminosity). Let gas at radius $r$ have flux-mean opacity $\kappa_{\rm F} \equiv (1/F)\int\kappa_\nu F_\nu\,\mathrm{d}\nu$, and let the source radiate $L$ isotropically. Gravity holds the gas only if $$ L \;\lt\; L_{\rm Edd} \;\equiv\; \frac{4\pi GMc}{\kappa_{\rm F}}, \tag{9.1}$$ independent of $r$. For fully ionised gas the opacity is electron scattering, $\kappa_{\rm F} = \kappa_{\rm es} = \sigma_{\rm T}(1+X)/(2m_{\rm p})$, and at $X = 1$ this is $$ L_{\rm Edd} \;=\; \frac{4\pi GMm_{\rm p}c}{\sigma_{\rm T}}, \tag{9.2}$$ which is the formula module09.html:944 states.
The flux at radius $r$ is $F = L/(4\pi r^2)$, and it carries momentum $F/c$ per unit area per unit time. By Proposition 2, a layer of thickness $\mathrm{d}s$ removes the fraction $\kappa_\nu\rho\,\mathrm{d}s$ of the flux at each frequency. Absorbed photons deposit their momentum; photons scattered symmetrically forward and backward, as Thomson scattering does, deposit it on average too. The momentum removed per unit volume per unit time is therefore $\rho\int\kappa_\nu F_\nu\,\mathrm{d}\nu/c = \kappa_{\rm F}\rho F/c$, and per unit mass the outward force is $\kappa_{\rm F}F/c = \kappa_{\rm F}L/(4\pi r^{2}c)$. Gravity per unit mass is $GM/r^2$ inward. The gas is held when $$ \frac{\kappa_{\rm F}L}{4\pi r^{2}c} \;\lt\; \frac{GM}{r^{2}}, $$ and the $r^2$ cancels, leaving (9.1). For electron scattering, $\sigma_{\rm T}$ does not depend on frequency, so $\kappa_{\rm F} = \kappa_{\rm es}$ whatever the spectrum, and (6.2) gives $\kappa_{\rm es}$. At $X = 1$, $\kappa_{\rm es} = \sigma_{\rm T}/m_{\rm p}$, and (9.1) becomes (9.2). The radiation pushes on the electrons and gravity pulls mainly on the protons; the electric field that holds a plasma neutral couples the two, which is why the force balance is taken per unit mass of plasma. ∎

The trivial limit is $\kappa_{\rm F} \to 0$, and (9.1) gives $L_{\rm Edd}\to\infty$: a gas that does not interact with the radiation feels no force from it, and there is no limit. The cancellation of $r$ is the content of the proposition. Without it there would be a critical radius for a given luminosity; with it there is a critical luminosity for a given mass, the same at every radius.

Eddington's own form

Eddington did not state a limiting luminosity, and he did not use electron scattering. In his Sect. 82, p. 115, of The Internal Constitution of the Stars he derives "an upper limit to the opacity $k$ in any star (perfect gas or not) for which $L$ and $M$ are known by observation", and gives its physical reading on the same page: "the radiation observed to be emitted must work its way through the star, and if there were too much obstruction it would blow up the star".

Proposition 11 (Eddington's bound on the opacity). In a star in hydrostatic equilibrium, if the gas pressure $p_{\rm G}$ increases inward, $\mathrm{d}p_{\rm G} \gt 0$ for an inward step, and if the luminosity per unit mass $L_r/M_r$ inside radius $r$ is no smaller than its surface value $L/M$, then the flux-mean opacity satisfies $$ k \;\lt\; \frac{4\pi cGM}{L}. \tag{9.3}$$ This is (9.1) read as a bound on the opacity of a star of known $L$ and $M$.
Hydrostatic balance for an inward step $\mathrm{d}r \lt 0$ gives the increase of total pressure $\mathrm{d}P = -\rho(GM_r/r^{2})\,\mathrm{d}r$. The frequency-integrated first moment from the proof of Proposition 4, $c\,\mathrm{d}P_{\rm rad} = -k\rho F\,\mathrm{d}r$ with $F = L_r/4\pi r^2$, gives the increase of radiation pressure. Their ratio is $\mathrm{d}P_{\rm rad}/\mathrm{d}P = kL_r/(4\pi cGM_r)$. Since $\mathrm{d}P = \mathrm{d}p_{\rm G} + \mathrm{d}P_{\rm rad}$ and $\mathrm{d}p_{\rm G} \gt 0$, $\mathrm{d}P_{\rm rad} \lt \mathrm{d}P$, so $kL_r/(4\pi cGM_r) \lt 1$. With $L_r/M_r \ge L/M$, $kL/(4\pi cGM) \le kL_r/(4\pi cGM_r) \lt 1$, which is (9.3). At the surface, $L_r = L$ and $M_r = M$, and (9.3) is (9.1) with $k$ for $\kappa_{\rm F}$: the same inequality. ∎

Eddington writes the ratio $(L_r/M_r)/(L/M)$ as a single symbol and argues that it exceeds one because energy generation is concentrated toward the centre; the symbol is not used here, because module12.html:927 refuses a new bare meaning of the glyph he chose. His "If both temperature and density increase the material pressure $p_G$ must increase" is the condition $\mathrm{d}p_{\rm G} \gt 0$. The name "Eddington limit" is kept because it is what the literature calls (9.1), with the qualification that Eddington stated it as a bound on $k$ and not with $\kappa_{\rm es}$. For the Sun (9.3) gives $k \lt 13061\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$ with this book's constants; he gives $13{,}200$.

The limit's assumptions, each of which can fail. It assumes spherical symmetry, so that the flux at $r$ is $L/4\pi r^2$; a disc or a beam puts the flux where the gas is not. It assumes the opacity is electron scattering; any additional absorption lowers $L_{\rm Edd}$. And it assumes a steady state; a transient can exceed it while it blows the gas away. This module names no observed violation, because none was read.

10. Accretion at the limit

An accreting black hole radiates $L = \eta_{\rm rad}\dot Mc^{2}$, where the radiative efficiency $\eta_{\rm rad}$ is Module 9's notation. Module 9 defines the Eddington accretion rate as $\dot M_{\rm Edd} = L_{\rm Edd}/(\eta_{\rm rad}c^2)$ at $\eta_{\rm rad} = 0.1$ (module09.html:944).

Proposition 12 (growth at the Eddington rate). A hole that accretes at $\dot M_{\rm Edd}$ and radiates $\eta_{\rm rad}\dot Mc^2$ keeps $(1-\eta_{\rm rad})\dot M$, and its mass grows as $M \propto e^{t/t_{\rm e}}$ with $$ t_{\rm e} \;=\; \frac{\eta_{\rm rad}\,\kappa_{\rm es}\,c}{4\pi G\,(1-\eta_{\rm rad})}, \tag{10.1}$$ which does not depend on $M$.
The energy radiated, $\eta_{\rm rad}\dot Mc^2$ per unit time, leaves as photons and with it the rest mass $\eta_{\rm rad}\dot M$, so the hole gains $\mathrm{d}M/\mathrm{d}t = (1-\eta_{\rm rad})\dot M$. At $\dot M = \dot M_{\rm Edd} = L_{\rm Edd}/(\eta_{\rm rad}c^{2})$ with (9.1), $$ \frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}t} \;=\; (1-\eta_{\rm rad})\,\frac{4\pi GMc}{\kappa_{\rm es}\,\eta_{\rm rad}c^{2}} \;=\; \frac{M}{t_{\rm e}}, $$ with $t_{\rm e}$ as in (10.1). The right side is linear in $M$, so $M = M_0e^{t/t_{\rm e}}$; $M$ cancelled out of $t_{\rm e}$. ∎

11. Where radiation matters, and where it does not

Proposition 9 draws the boundary between gas-pressure and radiation-pressure support as a line $\rho_{\rm eq}\propto T^{3}$, which is a straight line of slope $3$ in the logarithms. Fig. 5 draws it with two points from this book.

At the Sun's centre the line lies at $9.3219\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ for $\mu = 0.82851$, against the actual $150.50$: the centre is $1614.5$ times denser than the crossover, and by Proposition 9 that ratio is $\beta/(1-\beta)$, the same statement as $1 - \beta = 6.190\times10^{-4}$. Why radiation matters more in more massive stars follows from the slope. Along a sequence of stars of the same structure and composition, $\rho_c \propto M/R^3$, and the hydrostatic central pressure $P_c \propto GM^2/R^4$ with $P_c \propto \rho_cT_c$ gives $T_c \propto M/R$. Then $\rho_c/\rho_{\rm eq}(T_c) \propto (M/R^3)/(M/R)^3 = M^{-2}$: the radius cancels, and each factor of ten in mass moves the centre two decades toward the line. This is the scaling inside Eddington's quartic, whose $(1-\beta)/\beta^4 \propto M^2$ Module 3 solves in its Problem K3.

The Sun as a whole sits at $L_\odot/L_{\rm Edd} = 2.6772\times10^{-5}$, with $\kappa_{\rm es}$ at $X = 0.7583$ (Problem D3). That is the fraction of the Sun's gravity that radiation pressure carries in the outer layers where the opacity is electron scattering, and it is the size of what Module 3 §11 dropped when it wrote "Ideal gas (3.2), with radiation only in Proposition 6" (module03.html:795). The centre's share, $1 - \beta_c = 6.1940\times10^{-4}$, is Module 3 §9's (module03.html:628). At the photosphere, $P_{\rm rad}/P = 2.9454\times10^{-5}$. Radiation pressure is a correction of a few parts in $10^{4}$ or $10^{5}$ everywhere in the Sun; radiation transport, which sets the temperature gradient through (4.2), is not a correction at all.

The radiating shock. Module 8 §3 (module08.html:336) computed that a $10\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ shock in molecular gas at $10$ K that radiates away its heat compresses the gas by a factor of $2802$, against $4$ for an adiabatic shock. That is radiation's other role in fluid dynamics — as an energy sink rather than a force — and it is Module 8's result, cited here and not re-derived.

12. What this module captures, and what it misses

The module captures radiation as a momentum carrier and as a heat carrier, in the two limits where each has a closed form: the optically thick diffusion law and the grey atmosphere, and the force balance on optically thin gas. What it misses are frequency dependence beyond the Rosseland mean, the time-dependent and moving-medium terms of the transfer equation, and all the atomic physics that sets real opacities.

13. Problems

C1. Show that a radiation field whose intensity $I_\nu$ does not depend on direction has $P_\nu = u_\nu/3$. Then compute $P_\nu/u_\nu$ for a parallel beam, $I_\nu \propto \delta(\mathbf{n} - \mathbf{n}_0)$ with $\mathbf{n}_0$ along $z$, and for a field that is uniform over the hemisphere $\cos\theta \gt 0$ and zero over the other.
Solution
For isotropic $I_\nu$, $P_\nu/u_\nu = \oint\cos^2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\Omega/\oint\mathrm{d}\Omega = (4\pi/3)/4\pi = 0.333333$; see the proof of Proposition 1. For the beam, all the intensity has $\cos\theta = 1$, so $P_\nu/u_\nu = 1.000000$: three times the isotropic value. For the hemisphere, $\int_0^1\mu^2\,\mathrm{d}\mu/\int_0^1\mathrm{d}\mu = 1/3$, so $P_\nu/u_\nu = 0.333333$ again, although its flux is not zero. The $1/3$ needs only that $\langle\cos^{2}\theta\rangle = 1/3$, not isotropy; this is why the surface condition of Proposition 6 does not contradict its closure.
C2. Compute $L_{\rm Edd}$ and $\dot M_{\rm Edd}$ for a neutron star of $M = 1.4\,M_\odot$, with $M_\odot = 1.9884\times10^{33}$ g, for fully ionised gas of hydrogen mass fraction $X = 1$ and $X = 0.7261$, the rest helium. Use the book's convention $\eta_{\rm rad} = 0.1$ in $\dot M_{\rm Edd} = L_{\rm Edd}/(\eta_{\rm rad}c^2)$.
Solution
$M = 2.783774\times10^{33}$ g. By (9.1) with (6.2), $L_{\rm Edd} = 1.759891\times10^{38}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$ at $X = 1$ and $2.039153\times10^{38}$ at $X = 0.7261$, a ratio of $1.158681 = 2/(1+X)$. The rates are $\dot M_{\rm Edd} = 1.958143\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$ and $2.268864\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$. The composition moves the limit by the factor $2/(1+X)$ and nothing else.
C3. In Eddington's grey atmosphere (5.1) with $T_{\rm eff} = 5772$ K, compute $T$ at $\tau = 0$, $2/3$ and $2$. The model is grey: $\kappa_\nu$ does not depend on frequency.
Solution
$T(0) = T_{\rm eff}(1/2)^{1/4} = 0.840896\,T_{\rm eff} = 4853.7$ K; $T(2/3) = 5772.0$ K by construction; $T(2) = T_{\rm eff}2^{1/4} = 6864.1$ K. The surface is not at zero temperature: it sees radiation from below and is heated by it. The exact grey solution lowers $T(0)$, because $q_{\rm H}(0) = 1/\sqrt3$ is less than $2/3$; the surface temperature falls by the factor $(q_{\rm H}(0)/(2/3))^{1/4}$.
D1. Derive (8.1) from the first law, $\mathrm{d}e + P\,\mathrm{d}(1/\rho) = 0$, with $e = 3P_{\rm gas}/(2\rho) + a_{\rm rad}T^{4}/\rho$ for a monatomic gas, $\gamma = 5/3$, and check both limits. Evaluate $\Gamma_1$ at $\beta = 1/2$.
Solution
See the proof of Proposition 8. The adiabatic temperature exponent $\mathrm{d}\ln T/\mathrm{d}\ln\rho$ is $0.666667 = \gamma - 1$ at $\beta = 1$ and $0.333333$ at $\beta = 0$, and $\Gamma_1 = \beta + (4 - 3\beta)\,\mathrm{d}\ln T/\mathrm{d}\ln\rho$ gives $1.666667$ and $1.333333$. At $\beta = 1/2$, $\Gamma_1 = 1.425926$; Chandrasekhar's Table 1 prints $1.426$ at $1 - \beta = 0.5$.
D2. Derive (4.2) from the first angular moment of the transfer equation, stating the Eddington closure $P_\nu = u_\nu/3$ as the assumption it is. Then apply the result to the grey atmosphere (5.1) at large $\tau$ and show that it carries exactly the flux $\sigma_{\rm SB}T_{\rm eff}^4$.
Solution
See the proof of Proposition 4; the closure enters once, in replacing $P_\nu$ by $u_\nu/3$, and it is exact only for $\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle = 1/3$. With $z$ outward and $\tau$ inward, $\mathrm{d}\tau = -\kappa\rho\,\mathrm{d}z$, and (4.2) reads $F = (c/3)\,\mathrm{d}(a_{\rm rad}T^{4})/\mathrm{d}\tau$. From (5.1), $\mathrm{d}T^{4}/\mathrm{d}\tau = \tfrac34T_{\rm eff}^{4}$ at every depth, so $F = (ca_{\rm rad}/4)T_{\rm eff}^4 = \sigma_{\rm SB}T_{\rm eff}^4$. The run evaluates the ratio as $1.000000$.
D3. Show that Propositions 10 and 11 are one inequality. Use Proposition 11's condition that the gas pressure increases inward, $\mathrm{d}p_{\rm G} \gt 0$ for an inward step. Then evaluate Eddington's bound for the Sun, with $L_\odot = 3.828\times10^{33}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$ and $GM_\odot = 1.3271244\times10^{26}\ \mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-2}}$, and divide the Sun's surface electron-scattering opacity at $X = 0.7583$ by it.
Solution
At the surface, $L_r = L$ and $M_r = M$, and the proof of Proposition 11 gives $kL/(4\pi cGM) \lt 1$, which rearranges to $L \lt 4\pi GMc/k$: (9.1) with $\kappa_{\rm F} = k$. Proposition 10 reads the inequality as a bound on $L$ at given opacity; Proposition 11 reads it as a bound on the opacity at given $L$. For the Sun, $4\pi cGM_\odot/L_\odot = 13060.8\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$. With $\kappa_{\rm es} = 0.34966$ the ratio is $2.6772\times10^{-5}$, which is $L_\odot/L_{\rm Edd}$: the two readings give the same number.
K1. A $10\,M_\odot$ black hole accretes at exactly the Eddington rate, with $\eta_{\rm rad} = 0.1$ and $\kappa_{\rm es} = \sigma_{\rm T}/m_{\rm p}$. The hole keeps the fraction $(1-\eta_{\rm rad})$ of what falls in. How long does it take to reach $10^{9}\,M_\odot$?
Solution
By (10.1), $t_{\rm e} = 5.0054\times10^{7}$ yr. The growth factor is $10^{8}$, which is $\ln 10^{8} = 18.420681$ e-folds, so the time is $9.2204\times10^{8}$ yr. Dropping the $(1-\eta_{\rm rad})$ — using the Salpeter time $4.5049\times10^{7}$ yr as the e-folding time — gives $8.2983\times10^{8}$ yr, 10 per cent short. The time does not depend on the seed's mass except through the logarithm of the growth factor.
K2. Module 9 §10's Bondi hole has $M = 10\,M_\odot$, with $M_\odot = 1.9884\times10^{33}$ g, and accretes $\dot M = 5.124\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$ (module09.html:913). $\dot M_{\rm Edd}$ is defined at $\eta_{\rm rad} = 0.1$ and $L_{\rm Edd}$ by (9.2). What luminosity would the hole radiate at $\eta_{\rm rad} = 0.1$, how many times $L_{\rm Edd}$ is it, and at what $\eta_{\rm rad}$ would it sit exactly at $L_{\rm Edd}$?
Solution
$L = 0.1\,\dot Mc^2 = 4.6052\times10^{42}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$, which is $3663.5$ times $L_{\rm Edd} = 1.257065\times10^{39}$ — the same number as $\dot M/\dot M_{\rm Edd}$, because $\dot M_{\rm Edd}$ is defined at the same $0.1$. At $L = L_{\rm Edd}$ the efficiency is $L_{\rm Edd}/\dot Mc^2 = 2.7297\times10^{-5}$. A flow that radiates that inefficiently escapes the limit: it can accrete at the Bondi rate without radiation pressure stopping it. That is the regime Module 9 §9 puts Sgr A* in, at $\eta_{\rm rad} \approx 4\times10^{-9}$.
K3. Evaluate the adiabaticity parameter $\omega\chi/c_{\rm s}^2$ at the solar acoustic cutoff, $\nu_{\rm ac} = 4497\ \mu$Hz, with ATLAS9's values at $\tau_{\rm R} = 2/3$: $\kappa_{\rm R} = 0.4447\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$, $\rho = 2.6721\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $T = 5910.1$ K, $c_P = 1.6968\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ and $c_{\rm s} = 8.081\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$.
Solution
By (4.4), $\chi = 1.1588\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$, and with $\omega = 2\pi\nu_{\rm ac}$ the parameter is $0.5014$. By (7.1) the diffusion time across a wavelength is $12.532$ periods. The parameter is linear in $\omega$, so it is $1.4554 = 4497/3090$ times its value at $\nu_{\rm max}$. At the cutoff, the highest frequency the atmosphere traps, the leakage is half a radian of heat per radian of oscillation.
