8. The astrophysical check: Bondi accretion onto Sgr A*

Proposition 5 needs three numbers: a mass, a gas density and a sound speed at the accretion radius. For the black hole at the centre of the Galaxy all three have been measured, which is rare, and a fourth quantity — the actual accretion rate — has been bounded independently, which is rarer. The comparison is therefore possible, and it is the largest ratio this book has produced.

The mass is the GRAVITY Collaboration's, from the orbits of individual stars: $M = 4.30\times10^{6}\,M_\odot$ to about $\pm0.25$ per cent, that is $8.5502\times10^{39}$ g. The gas is Chandra's, from Baganoff et al. (2003), their Sect. 11.1.2: $n_{\rm e} \approx 130\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $kT_{\rm e} \approx 2$ keV $= 2.321\times10^{7}$ K, with the flow taken as adiabatic, $\gamma = 5/3$, and the gas fully ionised at twice solar abundance, $\mu \approx 0.70$.

Baganoff et al.'s density convention is $\rho = n_{\rm e}\mu m_{\rm H}$ — mass per particle multiplied by the electron density — and it is reproduced here deliberately, so that the comparison in §8.1 tests the same quantity they computed. Their $\mu \approx 0.70$ is quoted against their own $m_{\rm H}$ and is the one mean molecular weight in this module not converted to the $m_{\rm u}$ convention of (2.1), because converting a number inside a quoted formula would misrepresent the source. The consequences of the convention are computed and shown to be immaterial at the end of §8.1.

Those inputs give $\rho = 1.5221\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $c_\infty = \sqrt{\gamma kT/(\mu m_{\rm H})} = 675.4\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, against the $670\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ the paper quotes — a ratio of $1.0080$, the difference being rounding in their printed inputs. Then $R_B = 2GM/c_\infty^2 = 0.0811$ pc. By (5.2) at $\gamma = 5/3$ the critical radius is zero: the adiabatic flow has no interior critical point, which is exactly why $\lambda_c = 1/4$ enters as a limit. Proposition 5 then gives

$$ \dot M_{\rm Bondi} \;=\; 5.0549\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}} \;=\; 8.022\times10^{-6}\ M_\odot\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}. \tag{8.1}$$

8.1 First, a check that is not a test of nature

Baganoff et al. computed the Bondi rate from their own numbers and got $\dot M_B \approx 3\times10^{-6}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ with $\lambda = 1/4$. They used the black-hole mass standard in 2001, which their own paper states as $2.6\times10^{6}\,M_\odot$ and which this module also recovers, as $2.61\times10^{6}\,M_\odot$, by back-solving their quoted $R_B \approx 0.05$ pc and $c_\infty \approx 670\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Since $\dot M \propto M^2$, rescaling to the GRAVITY mass gives $8.15\times10^{-6}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$.

Check 9 — a pipeline check, not a test. Ratio $8.15/8.02 = 1.016$. Baganoff et al. computed their number from their own $n_{\rm e}$ and $kT$ with this same formula, so agreement here confirms only that the formula has been implemented identically to theirs. It says nothing about the Galactic Centre. The test is the next subsection.

The density convention can be varied to see whether it matters. Taking instead $\rho = n_{\rm e}m_{\rm p}(1+4y)/(1+2y)$ at $y = 0.1$ — mass per electron, for twice-solar helium — gives $2.537\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, a factor $1.67$ larger, and $\dot M = 1.34\times10^{-5}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$. Every conclusion below moves by less than a factor of two and none changes.

A second consistency check, also not a test: Baganoff et al.'s Sect. 11.3 gives a local diffuse plasma at $kT \approx 1.3$ keV and $n_{\rm e} \approx 26\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and quotes $\dot M_{B,\rm local} \sim 1\times10^{-6}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$; this module's formula at their mass gives $1.13\times10^{-6}$, a ratio of $1.13$. (Their $26\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ is a root-mean-square electron density at unity filling factor, from their Sect. 7.)

8.2 The comparison, and the condition attached to it

Marrone et al. (2007) detected Faraday rotation toward Sgr A*, with a ten-epoch mean rotation measure $\mathrm{RM} = (-5.6\pm0.7)\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{rad\,m^{-2}}$ — $8.0\sigma$ from zero. The rotation measure integrates $n_{\rm e}B_\parallel$ along the line of sight through the accretion flow, so it bounds how much plasma can be there. The bound depends on how far in the rotating medium extends:

Carry the condition with the number. The upper limits assume a magnetic field near equipartition strength, ordered, and largely radial, and Marrone et al. say in their Sect. 4 that this "cannot be justified observationally": "Magnetic fields that are a fraction $\epsilon$ of the equipartition strength will raise the accretion rate limits by $\epsilon^{-2/3}$ (a factor of 10 for $\epsilon = 3\%$)." Applying their own scaling: $\epsilon = 1$ gives an upper limit of $2.0\times10^{-7}$ and a ratio of $40$; $\epsilon = 0.10$ gives $9.3\times10^{-7}$ and a ratio of $9$; $\epsilon = 0.03$ gives $2.1\times10^{-6}$ and a ratio of 4. They add that if the field is reversed with a small bias, "the accretion rate upper limits derived from our RM detection would no longer hold" at all. The headline factor of 40 is the equipartition number and must never be printed without that clause.

Two further comparisons do not depend on the field at all, and the refutation rests on them.

The lower limits. Marrone et al. state explicitly that these "are not subject to the above caveats since the uncertainties act to raise the minimum accretion rate". The Bondi rate exceeds them by factors of $535$ to $2674$. An upper limit that might be soft is one thing; a rate that exceeds even the minimum plasma content the rotation measure demands, by three orders of magnitude, is another.

The radiative efficiency. Baganoff et al.'s absorption-corrected 2–10 keV luminosity is $\approx 2\times10^{33}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$. If the Bondi rate were actually accreting, the available power would be $\dot M c^2 = 4.543\times10^{41}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$, so the implied efficiency is

$$ \eta \;=\; \frac{L_X}{\dot M_{\rm Bondi}c^2} \;\approx\; 4\times10^{-9}. \tag{8.2}$$ One significant figure and no more. Baganoff et al. give $L_X = (2.4^{+3.0}_{-0.6})\times10^{33}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$ and state that the 2–10 keV luminosity is "known only to within a factor of two"; they then round to $\approx 2\times10^{33}$ throughout their discussion, which is the figure used here. The interval on $L_X$ alone spans $\eta = 4\times10^{-9}$ to $1\times10^{-8}$. Quoting three digits for a quantity whose numerator is uncertain by a factor of two would be a false precision, and the conclusion needs none.
Check 11 — REFUTED, independently of the magnetic field. A geometrically thin accretion disc radiates with $\eta \approx 0.1$. The implied $\eta \approx 4\times10^{-9}$ is smaller by a factor of $\mathbf{2\times10^{7}}$. Either the gas is not accreting at the Bondi rate, or it is accreting and radiating almost nothing — and both readings refute the model as stated, because the model assumes radiation is dynamically unimportant and that the mass arrives.

Fig. 5 places all of these on one logarithmic axis, which is the only fair way to show a ratio of $2674$ and a ratio of $1.016$ in the same picture.

8.3 What is refuted, stated exactly

Not the algebra. §5.1 verified (5.3) against a direct assembly from the critical point to eight figures and against Bondi's own table to every legible digit. And note that the algebra was never doing as much work as is often claimed: Bondi proves $\lambda \le \lambda_c$, so even a perfect implementation does not fix the coefficient. A refutation resting on "the algebra is right, so the physics must be wrong" would be resting on a theorem that does not exist.

What fails are the three physical premises of Proposition 5, each of which Sgr A* violates in a way that is separately documented:

  1. No angular momentum. The gas has some. It circularises near $100\,r_S$ — five decades inside $R_B$, marked on Fig. 4 — rather than falling radially in, and a circularised flow must transport angular momentum outward before any mass reaches the centre. That is Module 11's subject and nothing in this module can address it.
  2. No outflow. Bondi's solution has $\dot M$ independent of radius by construction. The observed density profile near Sgr A* is shallower than the $r^{-3/2}$ that free-fall requires, which means $\dot M$ falls with decreasing radius: most of the gas captured at $R_B$ never arrives.
  3. Adiabatic and radiatively unimportant. (8.2) shows the flow radiates $2\times10^{7}$ times less efficiently than a thin disc. That is the defining property of a radiatively inefficient accretion flow, and the literature Baganoff et al. review in their Sect. 11 exists because of it.

Each premise, on its own, would reduce the rate. All three act in the same direction, and together they span the factor of 40 — or 4, or 535, depending on which leg is being used — without difficulty. The value of the calculation is not that it predicts the rate. It is that it predicts a rate which is definitely wrong, by a definite amount, in a definite direction, and the size of the discrepancy is the measure of how much physics the spherical picture omits.

One limitation belongs to the checks rather than to the equations. Both anchors compare a steady, spherically symmetric, single-stream calculation against measurements that are ensemble averages over a structured flow: the Helios fits average slow and fast streams, and the Chandra measurement averages over a region five decades larger than the one where the accretion is decided. §7.1 states the self-similarity condition under which the first of these is legitimate. For the second there is no such condition, and the comparison in §8 should be read as a statement about orders of magnitude — which is all it needs to be, given that the smallest ratio in it is $4$ and the largest is $2\times10^{7}$.
