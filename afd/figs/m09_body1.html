1. One equation, two flows

Module 3 put a star in hydrostatic balance and left it there. Module 2 derived the equation that governs what happens when the balance fails, and applied it to a flow whose radial structure was measured rather than predicted. This module does the thing neither of them did: it takes the steady spherical flow seriously as an equation to be solved, discovers that the equation has a singular point, and finds that almost everything worth knowing about stellar winds and about accretion onto compact objects follows from the behaviour of solutions near that point.

The claim in the title is literal and is worth stating before any algebra. Write continuity and Euler for a steady, spherically symmetric flow. Eliminate the density. What is left is one ordinary differential equation for the speed $v(r)$, and that equation contains no information about which way the gas is going: $v$ enters it through $v^2$ and through $\mathrm{d}\ln v/\mathrm{d}\ln r$, and a solution with $v \gt 0$ everywhere maps onto a solution with $v \lt 0$ everywhere. Take the outward branch and it is E. N. Parker's solar wind, published in 1958. Take the inward branch and it is H. Bondi's spherical accretion flow, published in 1952. They are the same calculation. Fig. 1 is that sentence drawn.

The singular point is the reason this is not a routine exercise. At one particular radius the coefficient of $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}r$ vanishes, and unless the right-hand side vanishes there too, the solution has infinite gradient and stops existing. That is a strong constraint, and §3 shows it selects exactly one value of an integration constant out of a continuum. The solution that satisfies it is the only one that starts subsonic at a stellar surface and ends supersonic at infinity — and a wind that does not end supersonic decelerates as $v \propto r^{-2}$, which is a qualitatively different object with a qualitatively different signature in the data. §7 uses exactly that: the sign of a measured velocity exponent separates the two, with no fitting.

The module then does what this book always does, and asks whether the calculation describes anything. It asks twice, at the two extremes of the mass range, and gets two different kinds of answer.

The anchor is the solar wind, §7. Venzmer & Bothmer (2018) fitted four power laws to the combined Helios 1 and 2 data between $0.29$ and $0.98$ au, each exponent fitted independently of the others and with no fluid equation imposed on the fit. Those four numbers confirm two predictions of this module and refute a third, and the sharpest of them is not a confirmation or a refutation at all but a measured shortfall: steady Euler, fed the measured pressure gradient and given no wind model whatever, accounts for about one third of the acceleration the wind is observed to have at 1 au. The same table was used in Module 2 §10 to confirm an exact conservation law and refute the adiabatic closure. §7.1 says in print what is new here and what is not, because a reader is entitled to ask whether the book is checking one table twice.

The astrophysical check is Sagittarius A*, §8, and it produces the largest ratio in the book so far. The Bondi rate, computed from a measured black-hole mass and Chandra's measured gas density and temperature, exceeds the rate that Faraday rotation permits by a factor of $40$ — and the module then spends as much space on the conditions attached to that $40$ as on the number itself, because one of them cuts it to $4$. Two further comparisons, which do not depend on those conditions, survive at factors of $535$ to $2674$ and $2\times10^{7}$. The refutation is of the physical premises, not of the algebra.

One thing must be said at the outset, because the module's first draft got it wrong and the correction is instructive. It is often written that the steady equations determine the accretion rate, and that the coefficient Bondi computed is an eigenvalue forced by the mathematics. Bondi says the opposite, in his own summary, and §5 quotes him. The steady equations bound the rate; choosing the transonic solution is a physical argument about which flow nature realises, and it is his argument rather than a theorem. That distinction does not weaken §8 — two of its three legs never touch the coefficient — but a refutation built on a misattributed theorem would not be worth having.
