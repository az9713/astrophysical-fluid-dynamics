1. One equation, two flows

Module 3 put a star in hydrostatic balance and left it there. Module 2 derived the equation that governs what happens when the balance fails, and applied it to a flow whose radial structure was measured rather than predicted. This module does the thing neither of them did: it takes the steady spherical flow seriously as an equation to be solved, discovers that the equation has a singular point, and finds that almost everything worth knowing about stellar winds and about accretion onto compact objects follows from the behaviour of solutions near that point.

The claim in the title is literal and is worth stating before any algebra. Write continuity and Euler for a steady, spherically symmetric flow. Eliminate the density. What is left is one ordinary differential equation for the speed $v(r)$, and that equation contains no information about which way the gas is going: $v$ enters it through $v^2$ and through $\mathrm{d}\ln v/\mathrm{d}\ln r$, and a solution with $v \gt 0$ everywhere maps onto a solution with $v \lt 0$ everywhere. Take the outward branch and it is E. N. Parker's solar wind, published in 1958. Take the inward branch and it is H. Bondi's spherical accretion flow, published in 1952. They are the same calculation. Fig. 1 is that sentence drawn.

The singular point is the reason this is not a routine exercise. At one particular radius the coefficient of $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}r$ vanishes, and unless the right-hand side vanishes there too, the solution has infinite gradient and stops existing. That is a strong constraint, and §3 shows it selects exactly one value of an integration constant out of a continuum. The solution that satisfies it is the only one that starts subsonic at a stellar surface and ends supersonic at infinity — and a wind that does not end supersonic decelerates as $v \propto r^{-2}$, which is a qualitatively different object with a qualitatively different signature in the data. §7 uses exactly that: the sign of a measured velocity exponent separates the two, with no fitting.

The module then does what this book always does, and asks whether the calculation describes anything. It asks twice, at the two extremes of the mass range, and gets two different kinds of answer.

The anchor is the solar wind, §7. Venzmer & Bothmer (2018) fitted four power laws to the combined Helios 1 and 2 data between $0.29$ and $0.98$ au, each exponent fitted independently of the others and with no fluid equation imposed on the fit. Those four numbers confirm two predictions of this module and refute a third, and the sharpest of them is not a confirmation or a refutation at all but a measured shortfall: steady Euler, fed the measured pressure gradient and given no wind model whatever, accounts for about one third of the acceleration the wind is observed to have at 1 au. The same table was used in Module 2 §10 to confirm an exact conservation law and refute the adiabatic closure. §7.1 says in print what is new here and what is not, because a reader is entitled to ask whether the book is checking one table twice.

The astrophysical check is Sagittarius A*, §8, and it produces the largest ratio in the book so far. The Bondi rate, computed from a measured black-hole mass and Chandra's measured gas density and temperature, exceeds the rate that Faraday rotation permits by a factor of $40$ — and the module then spends as much space on the conditions attached to that $40$ as on the number itself, because one of them cuts it to $4$. Two further comparisons, which do not depend on those conditions, survive at factors of $535$ to $2674$ and $2\times10^{7}$. The refutation is of the physical premises, not of the algebra.

One thing must be said at the outset, because the module's first draft got it wrong and the correction is instructive. It is often written that the steady equations determine the accretion rate, and that the coefficient Bondi computed is an eigenvalue forced by the mathematics. Bondi says the opposite, in his own summary, and §5 quotes him. The steady equations bound the rate; choosing the transonic solution is a physical argument about which flow nature realises, and it is his argument rather than a theorem. That distinction does not weaken §8 — two of its three legs never touch the coefficient — but a refutation built on a misattributed theorem would not be worth having.

2. Why a static corona is impossible

Parker's 1958 paper does not begin with a wind. It begins by assuming there is none, and showing that the assumption cannot be maintained. The argument occupies his Sect. II, it needs nothing beyond Module 3, and it is the cleanest demonstration in this book that an equilibrium can be internally consistent and still be impossible.

Definition 1 (the configuration). A single fluid, spherically symmetric about a central mass $M$, with no rotation, no magnetic field, no heat source and no radiation force. The gas is ideal, with pressure $P = n_{\rm tot} k_{\rm B} T$ and density $\rho = \mu m_{\rm u} n_{\rm tot}$, where $n_{\rm tot}$ counts all free particles, electrons included, and $\mu$ is the mean mass per particle in units of the unified atomic mass unit $m_{\rm u}$. For the solar wind this module takes a helium-to-hydrogen ratio $y = n_{\rm He}/n_{\rm H} = 0.05$ by number with both species fully ionised, so that $$ \rho = n_{\rm H} m_{\rm p}(1+4y), \qquad n_{\rm tot} = n_{\rm H}(2+3y), \qquad \mu = \frac{m_{\rm p}}{m_{\rm u}}\,\frac{1+4y}{2+3y} = 0.56220. \tag{2.1}$$ The base of the corona is placed at $r_0 = 1.03\,R_\odot$, where the Baumbach–Allen electron density of Source 6 is evaluated. All flow speeds are radial, $v \gt 0$ outward and $v \lt 0$ inward, and $\dot M \equiv 4\pi r^2 \rho |v|$ is quoted as a positive rate with its direction stated in words.
$\mu = 0.56220$ is measured against $m_{\rm u}$, which is this book's convention from Module 6 onward. Against the proton mass the same composition gives $0.55814$, and the wind literature usually quotes that number. Every product $\mu m$ is identical under the two conventions, so no computed quantity in this module depends on the choice; only the printed value of $\mu$ does, by the factor $m_{\rm p}/m_{\rm u} = 1.00728$. The helium fraction $y = 0.05$ is this module's own choice and is not taken from any source used here — see §7, where Venzmer & Bothmer's own treatment of the wind as a pure proton plasma is compared against it and found to move the result by 12 per cent in $c_T^2$.
Proposition 1 (a static corona has a floor pressure at infinity). For the configuration of Definition 1 with $v = 0$ and $T$ constant, hydrostatic balance $\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}r = -GM\rho/r^2$ integrates to $$ \frac{P(r)}{P(r_0)} \;=\; \exp\!\left[-\frac{GM\mu m_{\rm u}}{k_{\rm B}T}\left(\frac{1}{r_0}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\right], \tag{2.2}$$ and because $1/r \to 0$ rather than $-\infty$, the limit $$ \frac{P(\infty)}{P(r_0)} \;=\; \exp\!\left[-\frac{GM\mu m_{\rm u}}{k_{\rm B}T r_0}\right] \tag{2.3}$$ is finite and strictly positive. A hydrostatic isothermal atmosphere in an inverse-square field does not run out of pressure.
Divide the balance by $P = \rho k_{\rm B}T/(\mu m_{\rm u})$ at constant $T$: $$ \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln P}{\mathrm{d}r} \;=\; -\frac{GM\mu m_{\rm u}}{k_{\rm B}T}\,\frac{1}{r^2}. \tag{2.4}$$ Integrate from $r_0$ to $r$; the antiderivative of $-r^{-2}$ is $+r^{-1}$, which is bounded above as $r\to\infty$. Hence (2.2), and (2.3) on taking the limit. The exponent is finite because gravity, not the atmosphere, supplies the only scale, and $\int_{r_0}^{\infty}r^{-2}\,\mathrm{d}r = 1/r_0$ converges. ∎

Contrast this with the plane-parallel isothermal atmosphere of Module 3 Proposition 3, where $g$ is constant and $P \propto \mathrm{e}^{-z/H}$ falls to zero. The difference is entirely the geometry: $\int^\infty g\,\mathrm{d}z$ diverges in a uniform field and converges in an inverse-square one. That convergence is the whole argument. An atmosphere whose pressure tends to zero can sit in a vacuum forever; an atmosphere whose pressure tends to a positive constant needs something outside it pushing back with at least that much pressure, and if nothing does, it is not in equilibrium at all.

2.1 The number, and what has to be believed to accept it

At $r_0 = 1.03\,R_\odot$ the Baumbach–Allen formula gives an electron density $n_{\rm e} = 3.161\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, hence $n_{\rm H} = 2.874\times10^{8}$ and $n_{\rm tot} = 6.179\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ under (2.1). At $T = 2$ MK that is a base pressure

$$ P_0 \;=\; n_{\rm tot}k_{\rm B}T \;=\; 1.706\times10^{-1}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}, \tag{2.5}$$

and (2.3) evaluates to $P(\infty)/P_0 = 1.908\times10^{-3}$, so that

$$ P(\infty) \;=\; 3.256\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}. \tag{2.6}$$

A round interstellar medium of one particle per cubic centimetre at $10^4$ K has $nk_{\rm B}T = 1.381\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$. The ratio is

The floor, against what must confine it. $P(\infty)/P_{\rm ISM} = 2.358\times10^{8}$. A static two-million-kelvin corona would require the interstellar medium to push back with $2.4\times10^{8}$ times the pressure it has. It cannot, so the corona is not static.
The interstellar pressure here is a round order-of-magnitude figure and not a measurement: $1\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ at $10^{4}$ K is a conventional warm-medium value, and Module 5 Table 1 uses the same. The argument is insensitive to it, because the conclusion needs only that $P_{\rm ISM} \ll P(\infty)$ and the margin is eight orders of magnitude. The base density is a different matter and carries a real caveat, stated in the provenance note below.

The sensitivity to $T$ is severe and should be seen rather than asserted, because it is the same exponential that §6 will find governs the mass-loss rate. At 1 MK the ratio $P(\infty)/P_0$ is $3.642\times10^{-6}$ and at 3 MK it is $1.539\times10^{-2}$ — four orders of magnitude across a factor of three in temperature. Even at 1 MK, however, the absolute floor is $6.2\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$, still $4\times10^{5}$ times the interstellar value, so the conclusion does not depend on the corona being as hot as 2 MK.

The base density is an input and it is extrapolated beyond its source's stated range. Allen (1947) fitted $n_{\rm e}(R) = 10^{8}\,[\,1.55\,R^{-6} + 2.99\,R^{-16}\,]\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, with $R$ in solar radii, to eclipse brightness measurements and stated the range explicitly: "in the range $p = 1.2$ to $2.6$, the intensity which is scattered by electrons follows the formula (2)". (His radius variable is $p$; it is written $R$ here because $p$ is pressure throughout this book.) This module evaluates the formula at $R = 1.03$, a fifth of a solar radius below the fitted range, in the steep $R^{-16}$ term where extrapolation is most violent. Nothing in §2 depends on the precise value — the margin is $2\times10^{8}$ — but §7.7 builds a mass-flux comparison on it, and that comparison is labelled a weak test partly for this reason.

2.2 Parker's own version of the same argument

Parker made this calculation in 1958 with different inputs, and the comparison is worth printing — not as a test of nature, but as a check that a 68-year-old argument has been reconstructed and not merely cited. His corona is not isothermal: it conducts, and its temperature declines outward, which makes his pressure fall faster than (2.2). From $N_0 = 3\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $T_0 = 1.5\times10^{6}$ K he gets $p_0 = 2N_0k_{\rm B}T_0 \approx 1.3\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$ and $p(\infty) = 0.55\times10^{-3}p_0 = 0.6\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$, against an interstellar pressure he takes as $1.4\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$ from "10 hydrogen atoms/cm³ at 100° K". His ratio is $4.3\times10^{7}$.

Check 1 — a consistency check, and not a check against nature. This module's $2.358\times10^{8}$ against Parker's $4.3\times10^{7}$: a factor $5.5$, and the two reasons for it are both stated by him. His corona conducts and cools outward, which lowers $p(\infty)$; and his assumed interstellar pressure is ten times lower than the one used here, which raises the ratio. Same argument, same conclusion, independently arrived at. Nothing is confirmed about the Sun by this comparison — it confirms only that the argument has been reproduced.

His conclusion, in his own words, is the sentence this module rebuilds: "Since we know of no general pressure at infinity which could balance the $p(\infty)$ computed from equation (9) with the expected values of $n$, we conclude that probably it is not possible for the solar corona, or, indeed, perhaps the atmosphere of any star, to be in complete hydrostatic equilibrium out to large distances."

3. The critical point, and the six things a flow may do

The corona must move. The question is how, and the answer is more constrained than it has any right to be.

Definition 2 (the isothermal sound speed and the critical radius). For the configuration of Definition 1 at constant temperature, $$ c_T \;\equiv\; \sqrt{\frac{k_{\rm B}T}{\mu m_{\rm u}}} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{P}{\rho}}, \qquad r_c \;\equiv\; \frac{GM}{2c_T^2}, \tag{3.1}$$ and the dimensionless variables are $x \equiv r/r_c$ and $u \equiv v/c_T$. $c_T$ is the isothermal sound speed and is written $c_T$ throughout, never $a$: $a$ is reserved elsewhere in this book for the cosmological expansion factor and does not appear in this module. The adiabatic sound speed of Module 4 is written $c$ and appears from §4.

Note what $r_c$ is not. It is not put in by hand and it is not a fitting parameter: it is the radius at which the escape speed $\sqrt{2GM/r}$ equals $2c_T$, equivalently where $c_T^2 = GM/(2r)$, and it is fixed the moment $T$ is fixed. Problem C1 checks that identity numerically.

Proposition 2 (the critical-point equation, and its exact first integral). For a steady, spherically symmetric, isothermal flow of the configuration of Definition 1, continuity and Euler reduce to $$ \left(u-\frac{1}{u}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x} \;=\; \frac{2}{x}-\frac{2}{x^2}, \tag{3.2}$$ whose left side vanishes only at $u = 1$ and whose right side vanishes only at $x = 1$. A solution may therefore pass through $u = 1$ only at $x = 1$, and (3.2) integrates exactly to $$ u^2 - 2\ln u \;=\; 4\ln x + \frac{4}{x} + C \tag{3.3}$$ with $C$ an integration constant that labels the solution.
Steady continuity is $4\pi r^2\rho v = \dot M$ constant, so $\mathrm{d}\ln\rho/\mathrm{d}r = -2/r - \mathrm{d}\ln v/\mathrm{d}r$. Steady radial Euler is $$ v\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}r} \;=\; -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}r}-\frac{GM}{r^2} \;=\; -c_T^2\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln\rho}{\mathrm{d}r}-\frac{GM}{r^2}, \tag{3.4}$$ the second equality using $P = c_T^2\rho$ at constant $T$. Substituting the continuity relation for $\mathrm{d}\ln\rho/\mathrm{d}r$ gives $$ \left(v-\frac{c_T^2}{v}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}r} \;=\; \frac{2c_T^2}{r}-\frac{GM}{r^2}, \tag{3.5}$$ which is the equation written under Fig. 1. Divide by $c_T^2/r_c$ and use $r_c = GM/2c_T^2$ to reach (3.2). Multiply (3.2) by $2$ and integrate term by term: $\int(2u-2/u)\,\mathrm{d}u = u^2-2\ln u$ and $\int(4/x-4/x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = 4\ln x+4/x$, giving (3.3). ∎

Equation (3.5) shows the mechanism directly and is worth reading before the dimensionless form. The bracket on the left changes sign at $v = c_T$. The right side changes sign at $r = GM/2c_T^2 = r_c$. If the two sign changes do not occur at the same place, $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}r$ is infinite where the bracket vanishes and the solution terminates. There is no freedom here: a flow that is to go from subsonic to supersonic must do it at $r_c$ and nowhere else.

Proposition 3 (six families, and only two of them are transonic). Write $h(u) = u^2-2\ln u$ and $R(x) = 4\ln x+4/x+C$, so that (3.3) reads $h(u) = R(x)$. Then $h$ has a single minimum, at $u = 1$, with $h(1) = 1$; and $R$ has a single minimum, at $x = 1$, with $R(1) = 4+C$. A solution therefore attains $u = 1$ if and only if $C = -3$, and then only at $x = 1$. The consequences are exhaustive:
  • $C = -3$: the two branches cross at the critical point. Following the subsonic branch in and the supersonic branch out gives the transonic wind; following them the other way gives the transonic accretion solution.
  • $C \gt -3$: $R(x) \gt 1$ everywhere, so $h(u) \gt 1$ everywhere and $u$ never reaches 1. Two branches that never meet: the subsonic breeze, and the supersonic-everywhere solution.
  • $C \lt -3$: $R(x) \lt 1$ in a band about $x = 1$, where no $u$ satisfies (3.3) at all. Two double-valued families, each existing only outside the forbidden band and turning back on itself at its edge.
$h'(u) = 2u-2/u$ vanishes only at $u = 1$ on $u \gt 0$, and $h''(1) = 2+2 \gt 0$, so the stationary point is a minimum and it is unique; $h(1) = 1-0 = 1$. $R'(x) = 4/x-4/x^2 = 4(x-1)/x^2$ vanishes only at $x = 1$, and $R''(1) = -4+8 = 4 \gt 0$, so again a unique minimum, with $R(1) = 0+4+C$. Since $h \ge 1$ always, (3.3) has a solution at a given $x$ only where $R(x) \ge 1$, that is where $4\ln x+4/x \ge 1-C$. Equality at the minimum requires $4+C = 1$, i.e. $C = -3$, and then it holds only at $x = 1$. The three cases follow by comparing $R(1) = 4+C$ with $\min h = 1$. ∎

A note on the names. These six families are often numbered I to VI, and that numbering is usually attributed to Parker. It is not his. Parker (1958) was fetched and read for this module, and it contains no such enumeration: he writes the equations, imposes the transonic condition, and plots $v(r)$. The numbering is a later textbook convention, and because nothing here rests on it the families are named in words and no numeral is cited to anyone.

Fig. 2 draws (3.3) as a contour plot in the $(x,u)$ plane, which is the honest way to present it: the families are level sets of one function, not six separate constructions, and the classification is visible rather than asserted. The transonic pair is the saddle-crossing contour, and it is the only contour that passes through the corner point $(1,1)$.

3.1 The two asymptotic behaviours that §7 will measure

Both limits of (3.3) are needed later and both are one line.

The wind. As $x\to\infty$ on the supersonic branch, $u^2 \gg 2\ln u$ and $4\ln x \gg 4/x$, so $u^2 \to 4\ln x$ and

$$ v \;\to\; 2c_T\sqrt{\ln(r/r_c)}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln v}{\mathrm{d}\ln r} \;\to\; \frac{1}{2\ln(r/r_c)}. \tag{3.6}$$

The numbers confirm it: at $x = 30$ the computed $u$ is $3.651$ against $2\sqrt{\ln x} = 3.688$; at $x = 10^{3}$, $5.288$ against $5.257$; at $x = 10^{6}$, $7.503$ against $7.434$. The logarithmic slope falls only logarithmically. That single fact is what makes §7.4's refutation unavoidable: raising the coronal temperature moves $r_c$ in proportion, but it buys only a logarithm of slope, and a logarithm cannot be made to fall fast enough.

The breeze. On a subsonic branch with $C \gt -3$, $u\to 0$ as $x\to\infty$, so $h(u)\to -2\ln u$ and (3.3) gives $-2\ln u \approx 4\ln x$, that is $u \propto x^{-2}$. Computing the logarithmic slope between $x = 30$ and $x = 100$ on the $C = -2$ solution gives $-1.9612$, approaching $-2$ from above. Hence:

A test with no fitting in it. A transonic wind has $\mathrm{d}\ln v/\mathrm{d}\ln r \gt 0$; a breeze has $\mathrm{d}\ln v/\mathrm{d}\ln r \to -2$; a static corona has $v = 0$ and no exponent at all. The sign of a measured velocity exponent separates them, and a sign needs no model to interpret. §7.3 measures it.
Do not turn the breeze asymptote into a significance. The measured exponent is $+0.049\pm0.010$ and the breeze asymptote is $-2$, so the measurement sits $2.05$ above it in a quantity whose formal error is $0.010$. Dividing those gives $205\sigma$, and that number would be meaningless. The $-2$ is the breeze's behaviour as $r\to\infty$, and $0.29$–$0.98$ au is not the asymptotic region for any $r_c$ in the plausible range. What the comparison carries is the qualitative separation — breezes decelerate, winds accelerate — and the significance to quote is the one on $\beta \gt 0$, which §7.3 gives as $4.9\sigma$.

4. The polytropic critical point, and the bound $\gamma \lt 3/2$

The isothermal assumption of §3 is a strong one and §7 refutes it. Before that, it is worth asking what survives without it, because the answer is a bound that the data can be held against — and a bound derived before any data is looked at is worth more than a fit made after.

Definition 3 (the polytropic flow). The configuration of Definition 1 with $P = K\rho^{\gamma}$ for constant $K$ and $\gamma$, so that the local sound speed is $c(r) = \sqrt{\gamma P/\rho}$, with $c_c$ its value at the critical point and $c_\infty$ its value at infinity. The isothermal case of §3 is $\gamma \to 1$, in which $c \to c_T$ is constant. The index $\gamma$ here is an effective one describing the run of $P$ against $\rho$ along the flow; it is not the ratio of specific heats of the gas, which is $5/3$ for the fully ionised hydrogen–helium mixture of (2.1) throughout.

Repeating the derivation of Proposition 2 with $P = K\rho^\gamma$ replaces $c_T^2$ by $c^2(r)$ in (3.5) and leaves the structure intact: the bracket still vanishes at $v = c$, the right side still vanishes where $2c^2/r = GM/r^2$, and the two must still coincide. What changes is that $c$ is now a function of position, so the critical radius is determined implicitly. The new content is in the slope there.

Proposition 4 (the critical slope, and the condition for a wind). Let $s \equiv \mathrm{d}\ln v/\mathrm{d}\ln r$ evaluated at the critical point of a polytropic flow. Then $s$ satisfies $$ (\gamma+1)\,s^2 + 4(\gamma-1)\,s + (4\gamma-6) \;=\; 0, \tag{4.1}$$ whose two roots have product $(4\gamma-6)/(\gamma+1)$ and discriminant $4(10-6\gamma)$. Consequently:
  • the product of the roots is negative if and only if $\gamma \lt 3/2$, so one root is positive and an accelerating transonic solution — a wind — exists exactly on that range;
  • for $3/2 \lt \gamma \le 5/3$ both roots are negative: a transonic solution exists but decelerates outward, which is an accretion flow and not a wind;
  • the discriminant vanishes at $\gamma = 5/3$, where the two roots coincide at $-1/2$, and the roots become complex only above $5/3$, where no transonic solution exists at all.
Write the polytropic form of (3.5) as $N(r,v)\,\mathrm{d}\ln v/\mathrm{d}\ln r = D(r,v)$ with $N = v^2-c^2$ and $D = 2c^2-GM/r$, both vanishing at the critical point. Near it the slope is $0/0$, so apply L'Hôpital along the solution: $s = (\mathrm{d}D/\mathrm{d}\ln r)/(\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}\ln r)$, where both derivatives are taken following the flow and therefore involve $s$ itself. Continuity and $P = K\rho^\gamma$ give $\mathrm{d}\ln c^2/\mathrm{d}\ln r = (\gamma-1)\,\mathrm{d}\ln\rho/\mathrm{d}\ln r = -(\gamma-1)(2+s)$. With $c^2 = GM/2r$ at the critical point, substituting and clearing denominators yields (4.1); the algebra is a quadratic in $s$ and no branch is discarded. The product of the roots of $as^2+bs+c$ is $c/a$, here $(4\gamma-6)/(\gamma+1)$, which is negative precisely when $4\gamma \lt 6$. The discriminant is $b^2-4ac = 16(\gamma-1)^2-4(\gamma+1)(4\gamma-6) = 4(10-6\gamma)$ after expansion, vanishing at $\gamma = 5/3$. At that value $s = -b/2a = -4(\gamma-1)/[2(\gamma+1)] = -1/2$, twice. ∎

The bound is fixed before any measurement is consulted, and that is what gives §7.2 its force. Nothing about the Sun entered (4.1); it is a statement about the local structure of a differential equation at a singular point. A gas whose effective index happened to exceed $3/2$ would have no accelerating transonic solution, and the exponents that Venzmer & Bothmer fitted had no reason whatever to respect that.

The solution can also be integrated numerically from the critical point outward, which tests the integrator rather than the physics. Taking $\gamma = 1.394$, $T_0 = 6.0$ MK at $r_0 = 1.03\,R_\odot$ — which places $r_c$ at $0.769\,R_\odot$ — and integrating to $3.00$ au gives $v$ rising from $352.2$ to $505.3\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. At $\gamma = 1.6$, with critical slopes $-0.2183$ and $-0.7048$, the integrator finds no accelerating branch and refuses, exactly as (4.1) predicts.

That integration is not a model of the Sun and must not be read as one. Its critical radius, $0.769\,R_\odot$, lies below the coronal base at $1.03\,R_\odot$. A real corona with those parameters would be supersonic from its base upward and could not launch this solution at all. The numbers are printed because they demonstrate that the integrator reproduces Proposition 4, and for no other reason. §7.5 takes the same $\gamma$ seriously as a solar model and shows what goes wrong.

5. Bondi accretion: the same solution, run inward

Reverse the arrow in Fig. 1. The gas is now at rest far away, at density $\rho_\infty$ and sound speed $c_\infty$, and falls in. Equations (3.5) and (4.1) are unchanged, and by Proposition 4 the relevant branch for $\gamma \gt 3/2$ — including the adiabatic $5/3$ — is a decelerating-outward, that is accelerating-inward, transonic solution. This is Bondi's 1952 problem.

Proposition 5 (the accretion rate, and what fixes its coefficient). For a polytropic flow with $1 \lt \gamma \lt 5/3$ falling from rest at infinity, Bernoulli evaluated at the critical point gives $$ c_c^2 \;=\; \frac{2c_\infty^2}{5-3\gamma}, \qquad r_c \;=\; \frac{GM(5-3\gamma)}{4c_\infty^2}, \tag{5.1}$$ and with the Bondi radius defined as $R_B \equiv 2GM/c_\infty^2$, $$ \frac{r_c}{R_B} \;=\; \frac{5-3\gamma}{8}. \tag{5.2}$$ Substituting (5.1) into $\dot M = 4\pi r_c^2\rho_c c_c$ gives $$ \dot M \;=\; 4\pi\lambda\,\rho_\infty\,\frac{(GM)^2}{c_\infty^3}, \qquad \lambda_c(\gamma) \;=\; \left[\frac{5-3\gamma}{4}\right]^{2}\left[\frac{2}{5-3\gamma}\right]^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}}. \tag{5.3}$$ The steady equations do not fix $\lambda$. They bound it: $\lambda \le \lambda_c$.
Bernoulli for a steady polytropic flow is $v^2/2 + c^2/(\gamma-1) - GM/r = c_\infty^2/(\gamma-1)$, the constant evaluated at infinity where $v = 0$. At the critical point $v = c_c$ and $c_c^2 = GM/2r_c$, so $GM/r_c = 2c_c^2$ and the relation becomes $c_c^2/2 + c_c^2/(\gamma-1) - 2c_c^2 = c_\infty^2/(\gamma-1)$, which rearranges to $c_c^2(5-3\gamma) = 2c_\infty^2$, the first of (5.1); the second follows from $r_c = GM/2c_c^2$. Then (5.2) is immediate from the definition of $R_B$. For the rate, $\rho_c/\rho_\infty = (c_c^2/c_\infty^2)^{1/(\gamma-1)}$ from $P = K\rho^\gamma$, and substituting $r_c$, $\rho_c$ and $c_c$ into $\dot M = 4\pi r_c^2\rho_c c_c$ collects into (5.3). The inequality is Bondi's: his eq. (18) derives $\lambda_c$ as the largest value for which a solution exists, and his (19) states the rate "cannot exceed" the corresponding flux. ∎

Two checks on (5.2), one of them requiring no algebra. At $\gamma = 1$ the sound speed is constant, so the critical radius is $GM/2c_\infty^2$ while $R_B = 2GM/c_\infty^2$, a ratio of exactly $1/4$ — and $(5-3)/8 = 1/4$. At $\gamma = 5/3$ the ratio is zero: the adiabatic flow has no interior critical point at all, which is precisely why $\lambda$ is a limit there rather than a solution property, and why §8's Sgr A* calculation quotes $\lambda = 1/4$ without a sonic radius.

$R_B \equiv 2GM/c_\infty^2$, with the factor of 2. Some authors write $R_B = GM/c_\infty^2$ and every derived ratio then shifts by a factor of 2. The definition with the 2 is used here because it is the one Baganoff et al. (2003) use in the sentence §8 quotes, and mixing the two conventions across a comparison would be a silent factor-of-two error.
What Bondi proved, in his own words, because this is easy to overstate. His Summary reads: "The factor of proportionality is not determined by the steady-state equations, though it is confined within certain limits. Arguments are given suggesting that the case physically most likely to occur is that with the maximum rate of accretion." His Sect. 4 derives $\lambda \le \lambda_c$ and his eq. (19) says the rate "cannot exceed" $4\pi\lambda_c(GM)^2c_\infty^{-3}\rho_\infty$. So (5.3) is an upper bound that the steady equations enforce, together with a physical argument — his, not a theorem — that the bound is attained. §8 is written so that its conclusion does not depend on that argument.

7.1 What is new here, and what Module 2 already did

A reader who has come this far will notice that Module 2 §10 anchored on this same table, and is entitled to ask whether the book is checking one measurement twice and counting it as two results. It is not, and the distinction is worth setting out before any number is computed.

Module 2 used the density and velocity exponents to test continuity, which forces $\alpha-\beta = 2$ exactly for a steady spherical flow; the mean fits give $1.961$ and the median fits $2.035$, bracketing the prediction. It then used the density and temperature exponents to test the adiabatic closure, which predicts a temperature exponent of $-\alpha(\gamma-1) = -1.340$ against a measured $-0.792\pm0.028$, a $19.6\sigma$ refutation recorded in its §10.5.

This module uses the same four numbers to test three different things: the critical-point bound $\gamma \lt 3/2$ of Proposition 4, which is a statement about a singular point and not about a closure; the sign of the velocity exponent, which distinguishes the solution families of Proposition 3; and the momentum equation itself, fed the measured pressure gradient, which neither earlier test touched. §7.4(i) does re-use Module 2's refutation of a closure, and it cites that result rather than re-deriving it.

One further caveat, inherited from Module 2 and not repaired here. The fits describe the mean or median of a distribution of streams at each radius — slow dense wind and fast tenuous wind mixed — not any single stream. Module 2's §10 set out the condition under which power-law exponents carry across such a mixture, namely radial self-similarity of the mixing weights, and the same condition is assumed here. It is an assumption, and §7.6 names it again where it bites hardest.

7.2 Check 3 — the effective index lands inside the transonic window

For a polytropic flow, $P \propto \rho^\gamma$ with an ideal gas $P \propto \rho T$ gives $T \propto \rho^{\gamma-1}$, so with $\rho \propto r^{-\alpha}$ and $T \propto r^{e_T}$,

$$ \gamma_{\rm eff} \;=\; 1 + \frac{-e_T}{\alpha}. \tag{7.1}$$

The mean fits give $\alpha = 2.010\pm0.038$ and $e_T = -0.792\pm0.028$, hence $\gamma_{\rm eff} = 1.3940\pm0.0158$. The median fits give $\alpha = 2.093\pm0.046$ and $e_T = -0.913\pm0.039$, hence $1.4362\pm0.0210$.

Check 3 — CONFIRMED. Proposition 4 requires $\gamma \lt 3/2$ for an accelerating transonic wind to exist. The measured $\gamma_{\rm eff}$ is $1.3940\pm0.0158$, which is $\mathbf{6.7\sigma}$ below $3/2$ (median fits: $1.4362\pm0.0210$, $3.0\sigma$ below). The ratio $\gamma_{\rm eff}/(3/2)$ is $0.9294$ for the mean fits and $0.9575$ for the median. At those indices the critical slopes of (4.1) are $+0.2051$ and $-0.8634$ (mean), $+0.1246$ and $-0.8408$ (median): an accelerating branch exists in both cases.

The same number separates the wind from both idealised closures: $\gamma_{\rm eff}$ is $24.9\sigma$ above the isothermal $1$ and $17.3\sigma$ below the adiabatic $5/3$ for the mean fits ($20.8\sigma$ and $11.0\sigma$ for the median). The solar wind is neither, which is the physical content of Module 2 §10.5 restated as an index.

This is an inequality satisfied, not an equality met, and it is weaker than Module 2's anchor. Module 2 predicted $\alpha-\beta = 2$ exactly and the measurement landed on it. Here the prediction is a one-sided bound and the measurement falls inside it with room to spare, which a wide range of unrelated physics would also do. Two further limitations belong with the result. The derivation of (7.1) assumes a single polytropic index from the critical point to 1 au, and §7.5 shows that assumption is false. And a bound cannot be violated by a positive result, only by a negative one, so the check could have failed and did not — which is its entire force, and all of it.

7.3 Check 4 — the sign of the velocity exponent

Proposition 3 gives three qualitatively different objects and §3.1 gives their signatures: a transonic wind accelerates, so $\beta \gt 0$; a breeze decays as $r^{-2}$, so $\beta \lt 0$; a static corona has $v = 0$ and no exponent at all. The measurement is $\beta = +0.049\pm0.010$ (mean) and $+0.058\pm0.013$ (median).

Check 4 — CONFIRMED. $\beta \gt 0$ at $\mathbf{4.9\sigma}$ against the formal error and $4.1\sigma$ against the year-to-year scatter (median fits: $4.5\sigma$ and $4.8\sigma$). The solar wind accelerates through the Helios range. It is a transonic wind and not a breeze, and Proposition 1 has already excluded the static case on independent grounds.

The strength of this check is that it requires no model to interpret and no parameter to be chosen: one measures a sign. Its weakness is the same thing — a sign carries one bit. What it establishes is which of Proposition 3's families the wind belongs to, and nothing about whether the family's quantitative predictions hold. The next three subsections show that they do not.

7.5 Check 7 — the single-polytrope repair does not work either

The obvious repair is to abandon $\gamma = 1$ and use the measured $\gamma_{\rm eff} = 1.3940$ throughout. It fails, in three independent ways, and each one is computed rather than argued.

First, a polytropic wind with $\gamma \gt 1$ has a minimum launch temperature: below it the gas runs out of enthalpy before reaching the critical point. At $r_0 = 1.03\,R_\odot$ that minimum is $T_0 \gt 3.540$ MK for $\gamma = 1.3940$, and $3.804$ MK for the median $1.4362$. Both exceed the top of the measured 2–3 MK range, by factors of $1.18$ and $1.27$, and exceed its bottom by $1.77$ and $1.90$.

Second, force the solution to reproduce the observed 1-au speed. With $\gamma = 1.3940$ that requires $T_0 = 5.39$ MK, and the resulting solution has a critical radius of $r_c = 1.020\,R_\odot$.

That critical radius lies below its own base. The model was launched from $r_0 = 1.03\,R_\odot$ and places its critical point at $1.020\,R_\odot$, inside it. The flow would be supersonic from the base upward, contradicting the premise — a subsonic base — on which the solution was constructed. No observation is needed to reject it. And no observed sonic radius is quoted here, because none has been sourced: Verscharen et al. (2021) give $r_S = 0.309\pm0.004\,R_\odot$ and say plainly it is a lower limit. Nor should the Alfvén radius be substituted for it — their $12.080\pm0.236\,R_\odot$ (first fast-latitude scan) and $9.504\pm0.221\,R_\odot$ (third) describe a different surface.

Third, that same forced solution gets the wrong temperature and the wrong acceleration. It predicts $T = 5.817\times10^{4}$ K at 1 au against the measured $9.670\times10^{4}$ K, a ratio of $0.602$; and $\mathrm{d}\ln v/\mathrm{d}\ln r = +0.00802$ at 1 au against the measured $+0.049\pm0.010$, too small by a factor of $6.1$.

Check 7 — REFUTED, and the diagnosis is specific. What fails is the single index, not the polytropic idea. $\gamma$ is near $1$ low in the corona, where the heat is deposited and the gas is nearly isothermal, and near $1.4$ beyond $0.3$ au, where §7.2 measures it. No one index describes both regions, and the fitted $\gamma_{\rm eff}$ is an average over a range in which the true index varies.

7.6 Check 5 — the sharpest measurement in the module

Every comparison so far has tested a wind model. The momentum equation can be tested without one. Rearranging (3.5) for the logarithmic slope and keeping the pressure gradient general,

$$ \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln v}{\mathrm{d}\ln r} \;=\; \frac{1}{v^2}\left[-\frac{r}{\rho}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}r}-\frac{GM}{r}\right] \;=\; \frac{(\alpha-e_T)\,c_T^2 - GM/r}{v^2}, \tag{7.2}$$

where the second form uses $P \propto \rho T \propto r^{-(\alpha-e_T)}$, so that $-\mathrm{d}\ln P/\mathrm{d}\ln r = \alpha-e_T$ is read directly off the fitted exponents. No closure is assumed — not isothermal, not polytropic, not barotropic. The pressure gradient is measured; the gravity is known; the speed is measured. Everything on the right of (7.2) comes from the data, and the left side is also measured, independently, as $\beta$.

At 1 au with the mean fits, $v = 435.6\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, $T = 9.670\times10^{4}$ K and $n_p = 7.57\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$: $\alpha-e_T = 2.802$, $c_T^2 = 1.4301\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-2}}$ — that is $(37.8\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}})^2$ — giving a pressure term $+0.02112$ and a gravity term $-GM/(rv^2) = -0.00468$. Hence

$$ \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln v}{\mathrm{d}\ln r}\right)_{\rm predicted} = 0.01644 \pm 0.00061 \qquad\text{against}\qquad \beta_{\rm measured} = 0.049 \pm 0.010. \tag{7.3}$$
Check 5 — a SHORTFALL, and it is a measurement rather than a verdict. Ratio measured/predicted $= \mathbf{2.98}$: proton thermal pressure supplies about one third of the observed acceleration at 1 au. The gap is $3.2\sigma$ against the formal error and $2.7\sigma$ against the year-to-year scatter. The median fits give $0.01356\pm0.00074$ against $0.058\pm0.013$, a ratio of $4.28$ at $3.4\sigma$ and $3.7\sigma$.

The prediction's own error, $\pm0.00061$, is small enough that it barely moves the significance, which is why it is printed: the uncertainty in this comparison lives almost entirely in the measured $\beta$.

What this does not establish. It does not name the missing force. Two assumptions stand between (7.3) and any such conclusion. First, $c_T^2$ uses a single temperature for protons, electrons and helium, and that is false at 1 au; relaxing it raises the predicted pressure term, so the true shortfall in proton pressure alone is if anything larger, but the arithmetic of a multi-species pressure gradient is not done here. Second, (7.2) is nonlinear in $v$ and the quantities substituted into it are ensemble medians of a mixed flow, not properties of one stream. The honest statement is a measured shortfall. What supplies the rest — wave pressure, a turbulent cascade, or something else — is the question Module 2 §10.5 deferred to Module 10 on this same data set, and it is deferred again here.

And the composition does not rescue it. Venzmer & Bothmer treat the wind as a pure proton plasma — their Sect. 1, p. 2: "In the analyses, we treat the solar wind as a proton plasma — the average helium abundance is about 4.5 % and in slow wind at solar cycle minimum is even less than 2 %." Their density is therefore a proton density on a proton-plasma assumption, and the 5 per cent helium of (2.1) is this module's own choice and not theirs. Redoing (7.3) at $\mu = 1/2$ raises $c_T^2$ by a factor $1.1163$ to $1.5964\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-2}}$ and the prediction to $0.01890\pm0.00068$, moving the ratio from $2.98$ to $\mathbf{2.59}$ and the shortfall from $3.2\sigma$ to $3.0\sigma$. The composition is worth about 12 per cent in $c_T^2$ and less than half a unit in the ratio. It is not the missing force either.

A careful reader will object that the predictor and the predicted are not fully independent: the pressure exponent uses the fitted $\alpha = 2.010$, and steady continuity ties $\alpha$ to $\beta$ by $\alpha = 2+\beta$, which Module 2 §10 verified. Imposing that tie exactly, $\alpha = 2.049$, moves the prediction from $0.01644$ to $0.01674$ — 1.8 per cent, against a measured-to-predicted ratio of $2.98$. The conclusion is unmoved.

7.7 Check 8 — the mass flux, and why it is a weak test

Verscharen, Bale & Velli (2021) measured the radial mass flux per steradian, $F_m = r^2NMU_r$, from Ulysses: about $3.5\times10^{-16}$ au² g cm⁻² s⁻¹ sr⁻¹ over the poles during the first fast-latitude scan, $2.2\times10^{-16}$ during the third, and $1\times10^{-16}$ to $15\times10^{-16}$ in the equatorial region (their Sect. 3.1). Multiplying by $4\pi\,\mathrm{au}^2$ — a conversion computed here, not quoted from them, since they never state a total — gives $9.843\times10^{11}$, $6.187\times10^{11}$ and $2.812\times10^{11}$ to $4.218\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$ respectively. Helios flew in the ecliptic, so the equatorial range is the one to compare against; the polar rung is quoted because $4\pi\,\mathrm{au}^2$ times it is the familiar $10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$.

The Helios fits supply an independent cross-check: $n_p = 7.57\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $v = 435.6\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ at 1 au give $\dot M = 1.861\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$, that is $F_m = 6.62\times10^{-16}$, comfortably inside the equatorial range and derived without Ulysses.

The Parker prediction with the Baumbach–Allen base density gives $2.897\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$ at 1 MK — a ratio of $0.294$ to the polar rung — and $1.566\times10^{11}$ at the fitted $0.947$ MK, a ratio of $0.159$. At 2 MK it gives $5.385\times10^{13}$, a ratio of $54.7$.

Check 8 — a weak test, and it must not be reported as a confirmation. By (6.1), $\mathrm{d}\ln\dot M/\mathrm{d}\ln T_0 \approx 13.2$. A factor of 3 in $\dot M$ is therefore an 8.3 per cent test of $T_0$. Agreement to a factor of a few here is not evidence that the model is right; it is evidence that an exponential was evaluated at roughly the right temperature.

The inverse is the more informative direction, because it exposes the base density rather than hiding behind it. To deliver the observed mass flux, an isothermal model needs a base electron density of $1.074\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ at 1 MK, $3.036\times10^{7}$ at 1.5 MK, or $5.778\times10^{6}$ at 2 MK — against the $3.161\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ that Baumbach–Allen supplies. Three temperatures within the plausible range demand base densities spanning a factor of $186$. Given that the base density is itself an extrapolation below its source's fitted range (§2.1), this comparison constrains very little, and saying so is more useful than quoting the one temperature at which the two happen to agree.

8. The astrophysical check: Bondi accretion onto Sgr A*

Proposition 5 needs three numbers: a mass, a gas density and a sound speed at the accretion radius. For the black hole at the centre of the Galaxy all three have been measured, which is rare, and a fourth quantity — the actual accretion rate — has been bounded independently, which is rarer. The comparison is therefore possible, and it is the largest ratio this book has produced.

The mass is the GRAVITY Collaboration's, from the orbits of individual stars: $M = 4.30\times10^{6}\,M_\odot$ to about $\pm0.25$ per cent, that is $8.5502\times10^{39}$ g. The gas is Chandra's, from Baganoff et al. (2003), their Sect. 11.1.2: $n_{\rm e} \approx 130\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $kT_{\rm e} \approx 2$ keV $= 2.321\times10^{7}$ K, with the flow taken as adiabatic, $\gamma = 5/3$, and the gas fully ionised at twice solar abundance, $\mu \approx 0.70$.

Baganoff et al.'s density convention is $\rho = n_{\rm e}\mu m_{\rm H}$ — mass per particle multiplied by the electron density — and it is reproduced here deliberately, so that the comparison in §8.1 tests the same quantity they computed. Their $\mu \approx 0.70$ is quoted against their own $m_{\rm H}$ and is the one mean molecular weight in this module not converted to the $m_{\rm u}$ convention of (2.1), because converting a number inside a quoted formula would misrepresent the source. The consequences of the convention are computed and shown to be immaterial at the end of §8.1.

Those inputs give $\rho = 1.5221\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $c_\infty = \sqrt{\gamma kT/(\mu m_{\rm H})} = 675.4\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, against the $670\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ the paper quotes — a ratio of $1.0080$, the difference being rounding in their printed inputs. Then $R_B = 2GM/c_\infty^2 = 0.0811$ pc. By (5.2) at $\gamma = 5/3$ the critical radius is zero: the adiabatic flow has no interior critical point, which is exactly why $\lambda_c = 1/4$ enters as a limit. Proposition 5 then gives

$$ \dot M_{\rm Bondi} \;=\; 5.0549\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}} \;=\; 8.022\times10^{-6}\ M_\odot\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}. \tag{8.1}$$

8.1 First, a check that is not a test of nature

Baganoff et al. computed the Bondi rate from their own numbers and got $\dot M_B \approx 3\times10^{-6}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ with $\lambda = 1/4$. They used the black-hole mass standard in 2001, which their own paper states as $2.6\times10^{6}\,M_\odot$ and which this module also recovers, as $2.61\times10^{6}\,M_\odot$, by back-solving their quoted $R_B \approx 0.05$ pc and $c_\infty \approx 670\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Since $\dot M \propto M^2$, rescaling to the GRAVITY mass gives $8.15\times10^{-6}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$.

Check 9 — a pipeline check, not a test. Ratio $8.15/8.02 = 1.016$. Baganoff et al. computed their number from their own $n_{\rm e}$ and $kT$ with this same formula, so agreement here confirms only that the formula has been implemented identically to theirs. It says nothing about the Galactic Centre. The test is the next subsection.

The density convention can be varied to see whether it matters. Taking instead $\rho = n_{\rm e}m_{\rm p}(1+4y)/(1+2y)$ at $y = 0.1$ — mass per electron, for twice-solar helium — gives $2.537\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, a factor $1.67$ larger, and $\dot M = 1.34\times10^{-5}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$. Every conclusion below moves by less than a factor of two and none changes.

A second consistency check, also not a test: Baganoff et al.'s Sect. 11.3 gives a local diffuse plasma at $kT \approx 1.3$ keV and $n_{\rm e} \approx 26\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and quotes $\dot M_{B,\rm local} \sim 1\times10^{-6}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$; this module's formula at their mass gives $1.13\times10^{-6}$, a ratio of $1.13$. (Their $26\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ is a root-mean-square electron density at unity filling factor, from their Sect. 7.)

Carry the condition with the number. The upper limits assume a magnetic field near equipartition strength, ordered, and largely radial, and Marrone et al. say in their Sect. 4 that this "cannot be justified observationally": "Magnetic fields that are a fraction $\epsilon$ of the equipartition strength will raise the accretion rate limits by $\epsilon^{-2/3}$ (a factor of 10 for $\epsilon = 3\%$)." Applying their own scaling: $\epsilon = 1$ gives an upper limit of $2.0\times10^{-7}$ and a ratio of $40$; $\epsilon = 0.10$ gives $9.3\times10^{-7}$ and a ratio of $9$; $\epsilon = 0.03$ gives $2.1\times10^{-6}$ and a ratio of 4. They add that if the field is reversed with a small bias, "the accretion rate upper limits derived from our RM detection would no longer hold" at all. The headline factor of 40 is the equipartition number and must never be printed without that clause.

Two further comparisons do not depend on the field at all, and the refutation rests on them.

The lower limits. Marrone et al. state explicitly that these "are not subject to the above caveats since the uncertainties act to raise the minimum accretion rate". The Bondi rate exceeds them by factors of $535$ to $2674$. An upper limit that might be soft is one thing; a rate that exceeds even the minimum plasma content the rotation measure demands, by three orders of magnitude, is another.

The radiative efficiency. Baganoff et al.'s absorption-corrected 2–10 keV luminosity is $\approx 2\times10^{33}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$. If the Bondi rate were actually accreting, the available power would be $\dot M c^2 = 4.543\times10^{41}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$, so the implied efficiency is

$$ \eta \;=\; \frac{L_X}{\dot M_{\rm Bondi}c^2} \;\approx\; 4\times10^{-9}. \tag{8.2}$$ One significant figure and no more. Baganoff et al. give $L_X = (2.4^{+3.0}_{-0.6})\times10^{33}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$ and state that the 2–10 keV luminosity is "known only to within a factor of two"; they then round to $\approx 2\times10^{33}$ throughout their discussion, which is the figure used here. The interval on $L_X$ alone spans $\eta = 4\times10^{-9}$ to $1\times10^{-8}$. Quoting three digits for a quantity whose numerator is uncertain by a factor of two would be a false precision, and the conclusion needs none.
Check 11 — REFUTED, independently of the magnetic field. A geometrically thin accretion disc radiates with $\eta \approx 0.1$. The implied $\eta \approx 4\times10^{-9}$ is smaller by a factor of $\mathbf{2\times10^{7}}$. Either the gas is not accreting at the Bondi rate, or it is accreting and radiating almost nothing — and both readings refute the model as stated, because the model assumes radiation is dynamically unimportant and that the mass arrives.

8.3 What is refuted, stated exactly

Not the algebra. §5.1 verified (5.3) against a direct assembly from the critical point to eight figures and against Bondi's own table to every legible digit. And note that the algebra was never doing as much work as is often claimed: Bondi proves $\lambda \le \lambda_c$, so even a perfect implementation does not fix the coefficient. A refutation resting on "the algebra is right, so the physics must be wrong" would be resting on a theorem that does not exist.

What fails are the three physical premises of Proposition 5, each of which Sgr A* violates in a way that is separately documented:

  1. No angular momentum. The gas has some. It circularises near $100\,r_S$ — five decades inside $R_B$, marked on Fig. 4 — rather than falling radially in, and a circularised flow must transport angular momentum outward before any mass reaches the centre. That is Module 11's subject and nothing in this module can address it.
  2. No outflow. Bondi's solution has $\dot M$ independent of radius by construction. The observed density profile near Sgr A* is shallower than the $r^{-3/2}$ that free-fall requires, which means $\dot M$ falls with decreasing radius: most of the gas captured at $R_B$ never arrives.
  3. Adiabatic and radiatively unimportant. (8.2) shows the flow radiates $2\times10^{7}$ times less efficiently than a thin disc. That is the defining property of a radiatively inefficient accretion flow, and the literature Baganoff et al. review in their Sect. 11 exists because of it.

Each premise, on its own, would reduce the rate. All three act in the same direction, and together they span the factor of 40 — or 4, or 535, depending on which leg is being used — without difficulty. The value of the calculation is not that it predicts the rate. It is that it predicts a rate which is definitely wrong, by a definite amount, in a definite direction, and the size of the discrepancy is the measure of how much physics the spherical picture omits.

C1. Take an isothermal corona at $T_0 = 1$ MK. Find $c_T$, the critical radius $r_c$ in solar radii and in au, and $v(1\ \mathrm{au})$. Then show that the escape speed at $r_c$ is exactly $2c_T$, and explain why that identity is forced rather than coincidental.
Solution

$c_T = \sqrt{k_{\rm B}T/\mu m_{\rm u}} = 121.61\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ with $\mu = 0.56220$. Then $r_c = GM_\odot/(2c_T^2) = 4.4868\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{cm} = 6.449\,R_\odot = 0.02999$ au. Integrating (3.3) on the transonic branch to 1 au gives $v = 451.3\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}} = 3.71\,c_T$.

The escape speed at $r_c$ is $\sqrt{2GM/r_c} = 243.2\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, which is $2c_T$ to every digit. It must be: $r_c \equiv GM/2c_T^2$ rearranges to $2GM/r_c = 4c_T^2$, so $v_{\rm esc}(r_c) = 2c_T$ identically, for every temperature and every central mass. The critical radius is defined as the place where the escape speed is twice the sound speed, and the identity is a restatement of the definition rather than a result.

The physical reading is worth having. At $r_c$ the thermal energy per particle, $\tfrac12\mu m_{\rm u}c_T^2$, is one quarter of the binding energy $\tfrac12\mu m_{\rm u}v_{\rm esc}^2$. Inside that radius gravity dominates and the flow is subsonic; outside it, pressure does, and the flow can accelerate freely.

C2. At a mass-loss rate of $9.84\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$, how long would the Sun take to shed $10^{-4}$ of its mass? Compare with its age, and with Module 2's own figure for the same quantity.
Solution

$10^{-4}M_\odot = 1.98841\times10^{29}$ g, so $t = 2.0201\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{s} = 6.401\times10^{9}$ yr, a factor $1.40$ longer than the Sun's $4.57\times10^{9}$ yr. Running the same rate over the Sun's actual age removes $7.14\times10^{-5}\,M_\odot$, seven parts in $10^{5}$.

The reconciliation with Module 2, which a reader should demand. Module 2 §10 computed $1.72\times10^{-14}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ and $7.9\times10^{-5}\,M_\odot$ over 4.6 Gyr. The rate used here is $1.56\times10^{-14}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$, which is $10$ per cent smaller, and the two are not in conflict: they are different measurements. Module 2 took the Helios median fit at 1 au and assumed the ecliptic values hold over the whole sphere, which its own provenance note flags as false; this module takes Verscharen et al.'s polar rung, converted by $\dot M = 4\pi F_m\,\mathrm{au}^2$. A 10 per cent difference between an ecliptic extrapolation and a polar measurement of a flow known to be latitude-dependent is smaller than one would expect, not larger. Both give $\approx 8\times10^{-5}\,M_\odot$ over the Sun's life, and the conclusion — that the wind is irrelevant to the Sun's mass budget and dominant for the heliosphere — is the same in both.

C3. Compute the Schwarzschild radius of Sgr A* at the GRAVITY mass, express $R_B$ in those units, and find the free-fall time from $R_B$. Then explain why Baganoff et al.'s statement that $R_B = 0.06$ pc is "$2\times10^{5}\,r_S$" is not in conflict with your answer.
Solution

$r_S = 2GM/c^2 = 1.2699\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{cm} = 0.085$ au. With $R_B = 0.0811$ pc from §8, $R_B/r_S = 1.970\times10^{5}$. The free-fall time $\sqrt{R_B^3/(2GM)}$ is $184.4$ yr — short enough that the flow has had ample time to reach a steady state, which is one premise of Proposition 5 that Sgr A* does not obviously violate.

Baganoff et al. adopt $R_B = 0.06$ pc and call it $2\times10^{5}\,r_S$ at their black-hole mass of $2.6\times10^{6}\,M_\odot$. Both $R_B$ and $r_S$ scale linearly with $M$ at fixed $c_\infty$, so the ratio is nearly mass-independent and their figure and ours agree. What does change is the absolute radius: at the GRAVITY mass, $0.06$ pc is only $1.46\times10^{5}\,r_S$. The lesson is that a ratio quoted in gravitational radii and a length quoted in parsecs carry different mass dependences, and a comparison across two papers with different assumed masses has to convert one of them — which is exactly what §8.1 does with the $(M_{\rm new}/M_{\rm old})^2$ rescaling of the rate.

D1. The rate of accretion onto a mass moving at speed $v$ relative to the gas is usually written $\dot M \propto (c_\infty^2+v^2)^{-3/2}$. Tabulate $\dot M/\dot M(0)$ at $v/c_\infty = 0$, $0.5$, $1$, $2$ and $5$, and state precisely what kind of result that formula is.
Solution

The ratios are $1.0000$, $0.7155$, $0.3536$, $0.0894$ and $0.0075$, matching $(1+v^2/c_\infty^2)^{-3/2}$ exactly, since that is the formula. At $v = 5c_\infty$ the rate is down by a factor $133$: motion suppresses accretion far more effectively than temperature does, because $v$ enters where $c_\infty$ does and the exponent is $-3/2$ in the sum.

What kind of result it is. It is Bondi's own conjecture, not a derivation — his Sect. 8, in his own words: "it seems likely that it represents the order of magnitude of the accretion rate in the intermediate case, in which a star of mass $M$ moves with relative velocity $V$ in a uniform cloud of gas." He constructed it to interpolate between two cases he had solved: the velocity-limited Hoyle–Lyttleton rate, his eq. (21), and the temperature-limited rate at $\gamma = 3/2$, his eq. (22). Nothing in this module derives it, and the flow it describes is not spherically symmetric, so Propositions 2–6 do not apply to it.

One detail of the prefactor. Bondi writes $A = 2\pi(GM)^2(V^2+c^2)^{-3/2}\rho_\infty$, and his $2\pi$ is $4\pi\lambda_c(3/2)$, the coefficient for the single $\gamma$ his eq. (22) used. This module writes $4\pi\lambda_c(\gamma)$ so that $v \to 0$ reproduces (5.3) at any $\gamma$. That generalisation is a reasonable one and it is not his; it is recorded here so that no reader attributes it to him.

D3. Estimate the energy the solar wind carries, as a fraction of the solar luminosity, in three ways: bulk kinetic energy, the work done against gravity, and Verscharen et al.'s measured energy flux. Comment on what the three together establish.
Solution

With $\dot M = 9.843\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$ and $v = 435.6\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$: $\tfrac12\dot Mv^2 = 9.3384\times10^{26}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$, which is $2.440\times10^{-7}\,L_\odot$. The gravitational term $GM_\odot\dot M/R_\odot = 1.878\times10^{27}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}} = 4.91\times10^{-7}\,L_\odot$ — twice as large as the kinetic term, so most of the energy invested in the wind goes into lifting it out of the potential well rather than into its final speed. Verscharen et al.'s measured $F_E \approx 1$ au² g s⁻³ sr⁻¹ over the poles converts to $2.812\times10^{27}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}} = 7.35\times10^{-7}\,L_\odot$, close to the sum of the other two, as it should be.

All three are within a factor of three of one another and all are $\sim10^{-6}$ of $L_\odot$. The solar wind is energetically negligible for the Sun and structurally decisive for the heliosphere, which is the same conclusion C2 reaches about mass. It also tells us why the coronal heating problem is hard: the energy required is so small a fraction of the luminosity that almost any mechanism could supply it, and the smallness gives no leverage for choosing between mechanisms.

A mixture that must be declared. $\dot M$ here is Verscharen et al.'s polar rung and $v$ is Venzmer & Bothmer's ecliptic fit. They describe different wind streams at different latitudes. The mixture is defensible for an order-of-magnitude energy budget — the two differ by less than a factor of two in every relevant quantity — but the answer is an order of magnitude and not a measurement, and it should not be quoted to three digits elsewhere.

K1. The Sun moves at about $26\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ through the local interstellar cloud, taken as $n = 0.2\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ at $T = 7\times10^{3}$ K. Compute the accretion radius and rate, compare with the wind, and say whether the answer matters.
Solution

Taking the cloud as fully ionised with $\mu = 0.6$ against $m_{\rm H}$, $c_\infty = 9.81\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so $v/c_\infty = 2.65$ and motion dominates. Then $R_B = 2GM/(c_\infty^2+v^2) = 3.437\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{cm} = 2.3$ au, and the rate from D1's formula is $5.411\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}} = 8.59\times10^{-17}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$.

Against the outward wind at $9.84\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$, the ratio is 182: the Sun sheds mass $182$ times faster than it gathers it, so accretion is irrelevant to its mass budget by more than two orders of magnitude.

And the accretion does not happen at all, for a second and independent reason: $R_B = 2.3$ au lies deep inside the heliosphere, whose boundary is near $120$ au. Interstellar gas never reaches that radius; the solar wind sweeps it aside first. Two separate arguments kill the same effect, which is worth noticing — the ratio 182 would be the answer even if the heliosphere did not exist.

The ionisation assumption. $\mu = 0.6$ takes the cloud as fully ionised, which it is not; it is partly ionised, and a neutral cloud would give $\mu = 1.27$ and $c_\infty = 6.75\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Because $v^2 \gg c_\infty^2$ the denominator is dominated by the motion, so $\dot M$ moves by only 11 per cent and no conclusion changes. The assumption is stated rather than buried because a reader checking the sound speed would otherwise find it unexplained.

K2. A $10\,M_\odot$ black hole sits in a molecular cloud at $n = 10^{4}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $T = 20$ K. Find $R_B$, the Bondi rate, and the ratio to the Eddington rate. What does the answer imply?
Solution

With $\mu = 2.33$ against $m_{\rm H}$ for molecular gas, $c_\infty = 0.2662\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ — cold gas is very quiet. Taking $\gamma = 1$ so $\lambda_c = \mathrm{e}^{3/2}/4 = 1.1204$, $R_B = 2GM/c_\infty^2 = 3.746\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{cm} = 250\,413$ au $= 1.2140$ pc, and $\dot M = 5.124\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}} = 8.133\times10^{-4}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$.

The Eddington luminosity is $L_{\rm Edd} = 4\pi GMm_{\rm p}c/\sigma_{\rm T} = 1.257\times10^{39}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$, and at $\eta = 0.1$ the corresponding rate is $\dot M_{\rm Edd} = 1.399\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$. The Bondi rate exceeds it by a factor of $\mathbf{3664}$, and at that rate the hole would double its mass in $1.23\times10^{4}$ yr.

What it implies is that the calculation has refuted itself. A flow accreting three thousand times the Eddington rate would radiate far above $L_{\rm Edd}$, and the resulting radiation pressure would drive the gas away — violating the premise, used in Proposition 5, that radiation is dynamically unimportant. The honest reading is not "black holes in molecular clouds double in ten thousand years"; it is that spherical Bondi accretion is self-inconsistent in this regime, and a calculation that includes radiation is required. Module 13 supplies it.

$n = 10^{4}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ is taken as the total particle density here, not the H₂ density. Reading it as H₂ instead would raise $\rho$ by 20 per cent and $\dot M$ with it. $L_{\rm Edd}$ is quoted from Module 13 and not derived in this module; its $m_{\rm p}$ is a genuine proton mass and not a mean molecular weight.

K3. Express Sgr A*'s X-ray luminosity and the three accretion rates of §8 in Eddington units, and say what the pattern shows.
Solution

$L_{\rm Edd} = 5.405\times10^{44}\ \mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$ at $4.30\times10^{6}\,M_\odot$, so $L_X/L_{\rm Edd} = 3.70\times10^{-12}$. At $\eta = 0.1$, $\dot M_{\rm Edd} = 9.545\times10^{-2}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$, giving $\dot M_{\rm Bondi}/\dot M_{\rm Edd} = 8.405\times10^{-5}$ and, for Marrone's equipartition upper bound, $2.095\times10^{-6}$.

The pattern is the point. The Bondi rate is already $10^{-4}$ of Eddington, so Sgr A* is not accretion-starved in any sense that radiation pressure could explain — nothing is pushing the gas away. Yet the luminosity is $10^{-12}$ of Eddington, eight orders of magnitude below the rate. Comparing the two columns isolates the discrepancy: a factor $\sim20$ lives in the rate itself, between Bondi and Marrone, and the remaining seven orders of magnitude live in the radiative efficiency, which (8.2) puts at $4\times10^{-9}$ against a thin disc's $0.1$. Sgr A* is faint mainly because the gas that does arrive radiates almost nothing, not because little gas arrives.
