Ten modules of this book have written the same sentence in ten different ways: there is a magnetic field here, and it matters, and we are not carrying it. Module 1 ended with a plasma whose mean free path exceeds the system size and could say only that a field must be doing the confining. Module 2 left the intracluster medium with a Reynolds number of about $600$ and a thermal conductivity large enough to erase the structure that is observed. Module 5 could not say what holds a subcritical cloud up. Module 7 needed a statement accurate to one degree about a field its source never measured. Module 10 confirmed Kolmogorov's exponent most cleanly on a magnetic spectrum in a plasma its own argument does not describe. Twenty such promises are on the record, each with the file and line it was printed at, and this module exists to pay them.

What it costs is small, and that is the surprise. One term is added to Module 2's momentum equation — the Lorentz force — and one equation is added to close it, which is Ohm's law fed into Faraday's. Nothing else changes. Module 2 §4 proved its moment hierarchy under the hypothesis $\nabla_{\mathbf v}\cdot\mathbf a = 0$, and observed in the same breath that the Lorentz force $\mathbf a = (q/mc)\,\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ satisfies it, "so magnetohydrodynamics, in Module 12, therefore inherits (4.1) unchanged". That sentence is honoured here and not reproved. So is Module 2 §11's: "Proposition 3 was proved in a form that admits the Lorentz force, so nothing here needs redoing."

This module has no terrestrial measurement, and §4 says so where a reader will first miss it. Every other module in this book puts a laboratory number between the derivation and the sky — Module 4's speed of sound in air, Module 6's dry adiabat, Module 10's Kolmogorov constant. All four checks here are astronomical. What stands in the terrestrial slot is a reproduction of another chapter of this book, and it is labelled as one.

1. When is a fluid magnetised?

"Magnetised" is not one question. It is at least three, and they have different answers for the same gas.

Definition 1 (magnetic pressure, the plasma beta, and the Alfvén speed). For a field of strength $B$ in a gas of number density $n$ at temperature $T$ and mass density $\rho$, define $$ p_{\rm mag} \equiv \frac{B^2}{8\pi}, \qquad \beta \equiv \frac{p_{\rm gas}}{p_{\rm mag}} = \frac{8\pi nk_{\rm B}T}{B^2}, \qquad v_{\rm A} \equiv \frac{B}{\sqrt{4\pi\rho}}. \tag{1.1}$$ $\beta$ asks whether the field can push the gas. $v_{\rm A}$ is a combination of $B$ and $\rho$ with the dimensions of a speed; that it is the speed of anything is not assumed here and is proved in Proposition 6. Both $p_{\rm mag}$ and the tension that accompanies it are derived, not posited, in §3.
Definition 2 (the magnetisation). $\omega_{\rm c}\tau$, where $\omega_{\rm c} = ZeB/mc$ is the gyrofrequency and $\tau$ the collision time, is the gyro-phase in radians that a particle turns between collisions. The collision time meant throughout this module is Braginskii's — his equations (2.5e) for electrons and (2.5i) for ions — because §8's transport coefficients are defined on it. §8.1 prints the one other collision time this book uses, says exactly how the two differ, and accounts for the difference to six figures.
Proposition 1 (the two speeds are one ratio). For an ideal gas with ratio of specific heats $\gamma$, $$ \frac{v_{\rm A}}{c_s} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma\beta}} \tag{1.2}$$ identically, where $c_s = \sqrt{\gamma p_{\rm gas}/\rho}$. The mass per particle cancels.
$v_{\rm A}^2/c_s^2 = (B^2/4\pi\rho)\big/(\gamma p_{\rm gas}/\rho) = B^2/(4\pi\gamma p_{\rm gas})$. The density has cancelled, and $B^2/4\pi = 2p_{\rm mag}$, so the ratio is $2p_{\rm mag}/(\gamma p_{\rm gas}) = 2/(\gamma\beta)$. ∎

This is why $\beta$ is the census variable and $v_{\rm A}$ is not. The Alfvén speed needs a mean mass per particle and therefore a composition; $\beta$ needs neither. 2. The induction equation, and why flux freezing is a number

The field obeys Maxwell's equations; the gas obeys Module 2's. What couples them is Ohm's law, and what comes out is a single equation for $\mathbf B$ with exactly two terms.

Proposition 2 (the induction equation). For a fluid of uniform electrical conductivity $\sigma_{\rm e}$ moving non-relativistically, $$ \frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} \;=\; \nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B) \;+\; \eta_{\rm m}\nabla^2\mathbf B, \qquad \eta_{\rm m}\equiv\frac{c^2}{4\pi\sigma_{\rm e}}, \tag{2.1}$$ with $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ preserved for all time if it holds once.
Ohm's law in the frame of the moving fluid is $\mathbf j = \sigma_{\rm e}(\mathbf E + \mathbf u\times\mathbf B/c)$, so $\mathbf E = \mathbf j/\sigma_{\rm e} - \mathbf u\times\mathbf B/c$. Ampère's law without the displacement current gives $\mathbf j = (c/4\pi)\nabla\times\mathbf B$. Substituting both into Faraday's law $\partial\mathbf B/\partial t = -c\,\nabla\times\mathbf E$, $$ \frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} = \nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B) - \frac{c^2}{4\pi\sigma_{\rm e}}\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B), $$ and $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B) - \nabla^2\mathbf B = -\nabla^2\mathbf B$. Taking the divergence of (2.1) gives $\partial(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)/\partial t = \eta_{\rm m}\nabla^2(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)$, a diffusion equation for $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B$; if it starts at zero it stays there. ∎
Dropping the displacement current is an ordering, and here is the ordering. $|\partial_t\mathbf E/c|$ against $|\nabla\times\mathbf B|$ is of order $(U/c)^2$ once $E \sim UB/c$ is used, so the term is smaller than the one kept by $(U/c)^2$. For the fastest flow in this module's census, the solar wind at $435.6\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, that is $2.1\times10^{-6}$. It is an ordering and not an identity, and it fails for any relativistic flow — which is why this module's equations do not apply to a pulsar magnetosphere or a jet, and §11 says so.
Proposition 3 (the magnetic Reynolds number, and why size is the variable). The ratio of the two terms of (2.1) on a flow of speed $U$ over a scale $L$ is $$ \mathrm{R_m} \;\equiv\; \frac{UL}{\eta_{\rm m}}. \tag{2.2}$$ With Braginskii's $\sigma_\parallel = 1.96\,n_{\rm e}e^2\tau_{\rm e}/m_{\rm e}$ and his $\tau_{\rm e}\propto T^{3/2}/(n\ln\Lambda)$, the density cancels and $$ \eta_{\rm m} \;\propto\; \frac{\ln\Lambda}{T^{3/2}} \tag{2.3}$$ depends on the temperature and the Coulomb logarithm alone. Moreover $$ \frac{L^2/\eta_{\rm m}}{L/U} \;=\; \frac{UL}{\eta_{\rm m}} \;=\; \mathrm{R_m} \tag{2.4}$$ identically: the ohmic decay time measured in the system's own dynamical times is the magnetic Reynolds number.
The inductive term is of order $UB/L$ and the resistive term of order $\eta_{\rm m}B/L^2$; their ratio is $UL/\eta_{\rm m}$. For (2.3), $\sigma_\parallel \propto n\tau_{\rm e}$ and $\tau_{\rm e}\propto T^{3/2}/(n\ln\Lambda)$, so $\sigma_\parallel\propto T^{3/2}/\ln\Lambda$ and $\eta_{\rm m} = c^2/4\pi\sigma_\parallel \propto \ln\Lambda/T^{3/2}$. Equation (2.4) is arithmetic: $(L^2/\eta_{\rm m})(U/L) = UL/\eta_{\rm m}$. ∎

Equation (2.3) is the reason astrophysical plasmas are good conductors, and it is not the reason a reader expects. A large plasma is a good conductor through its size, not through its density. Two gases at the same temperature have the same resistivity whether one is a million times denser than the other; what differs is $L$, and $L$ enters $\mathrm{R_m}$ linearly. The table makes the point, and its last column is (2.4) used as a self-check on the column above it.

Proposition 4 (Alfvén's theorem). If $\eta_{\rm m} = 0$, the magnetic flux $$ \Phi(t) \;=\; \int_{S(t)}\mathbf B\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf S \tag{2.5}$$ through any surface $S(t)$ carried with the fluid is constant in time.
A surface moving with the fluid changes the flux through it in two ways: the field changes in place, and the boundary sweeps through the field. The transport theorem for a surface integral gives $$ \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t} = \int_{S}\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf S \;-\; \oint_{\partial S}(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol\ell, $$ where the second term is the sweeping, written with the sign that follows from $\mathrm{d}\mathbf S = \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf u\,\mathrm{d}t$ on the swept strip. Stokes's theorem turns the line integral into $\int_S\nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf S$, so $$ \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t} = \int_{S}\Big[\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} - \nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)\Big]\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf S = \int_{S}\eta_{\rm m}\nabla^2\mathbf B\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf S $$ by (2.1), which vanishes when $\eta_{\rm m} = 0$. The surface was arbitrary. ∎
Flux freezing is a measurement, not an idealisation. The smallest $\mathrm{R_m}$ in the table is the solar corona's, $1.024\times10^{12}$, which is $\mathbf{12.0}$ decades. By (2.4) that is the number of dynamical times a coronal loop would need in order to lose its field by ohmic decay. The word "decades" is printed beside the exponent that produced it, because a phrase like "many orders of magnitude" cannot be checked and would have been wrong: Module 10 §12 found three wrong order-of-magnitude claims in its own prose, each of them a number with no occurrence in any saved run.

The result of Proposition 4 is Alfvén's, in a one-page note of 1942. That paper was not fetched for this book and is cited for the result only, in the same way Module 1 cites Maxwell (1867); its volume, pages and date were confirmed at Crossref and nothing else about it is claimed here. The proof above is this module's own.

3. Magnetic pressure and magnetic tension

The field acts on the gas through the Lorentz force on the current it carries. What that force is, mechanically, is the content of this section, and it is what Module 2 §11, Module 3 §11, Module 4 §11, Module 5 §11 and Module 7 §5 have each been promised.

Proposition 5 (the Lorentz force splits into a pressure and a tension). The force per unit volume on a conducting fluid is $$ \mathbf f = \frac{1}{c}\,\mathbf j\times\mathbf B = \frac{1}{4\pi}(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf B = -\nabla\!\left(\frac{B^2}{8\pi}\right) + \frac{(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B}{4\pi}. \tag{3.1}$$ The first term is an isotropic pressure $p_{\rm mag} = B^2/8\pi$. The second is a tension $B^2/4\pi$ along the field; where a field line has radius of curvature $R_c$ it contributes a force per volume $B^2/(4\pi R_c)$ directed toward the centre of curvature.
Use the identity $\nabla(\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf B) = 2(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B + 2\mathbf B\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B)$, so that $$ (\nabla\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf B = -\mathbf B\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B) = (\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B - \tfrac12\nabla B^2. $$ Dividing by $4\pi$ gives (3.1). For the curvature, write $\mathbf B = B\hat{\mathbf b}$ with $\hat{\mathbf b}$ a unit vector. Then $$ \frac{(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B}{4\pi} = \frac{B}{4\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\ell}\big(B\hat{\mathbf b}\big) = \hat{\mathbf b}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\ell}\!\left(\frac{B^2}{8\pi}\right) + \frac{B^2}{4\pi}\frac{\partial\hat{\mathbf b}}{\partial\ell}, $$ where $\partial/\partial\ell$ is the derivative along the field line. The first piece is a pressure gradient along $\hat{\mathbf b}$, which exactly cancels the field-parallel part of $-\nabla(B^2/8\pi)$ — so the magnetic pressure acts only across the field, never along it. The second piece uses the Frenet relation $\partial\hat{\mathbf b}/\partial\ell = -\hat{\mathbf n}/R_c$ with $\hat{\mathbf n}$ pointing away from the centre of curvature, giving a force $B^2/(4\pi R_c)$ toward it. ∎
The cancellation in that proof is the whole physical content, and it is easy to read past. A magnetic field is not a gas with pressure $B^2/8\pi$. Along its own direction the pressure term is cancelled exactly, and a straight uniform field exerts no force at all. What a field resists is being squeezed (the pressure, acting across the lines) and being bent (the tension, acting along them). Every result in §4 to §9 is one of these two, or both.

4. Three wave speeds, and what one of them costs Module 7

Module 4 derived sound from a gas whose only restoring force was its own pressure, and its captures-and-misses table names magnetic pressure and tension as the restoring forces it left out. Adding them turns one wave into three.

Proposition 6 (the three MHD wave speeds). Linearising the ideal MHD equations about a uniform state with field $\mathbf B_0$, for a plane wave with wavevector at angle $\theta$ to $\mathbf B_0$, the phase speeds are $$ v_{\rm Alfv\acute en} = v_{\rm A}\cos\theta, \qquad v_{\pm}^2 = \tfrac12\left(v_{\rm A}^2 + c_s^2\right) \pm \tfrac12\sqrt{\left(v_{\rm A}^2+c_s^2\right)^2 - 4v_{\rm A}^2c_s^2\cos^2\theta}, \tag{4.1}$$ with $v_+$ the fast and $v_-$ the slow magnetosonic speed. At $\theta = 0$, $v_+ = \max(v_{\rm A},c_s)$ and $v_- = \min(v_{\rm A},c_s)$. At $\theta = 90^\circ$ the slow and Alfvén speeds both vanish and only the fast wave survives, at $\sqrt{v_{\rm A}^2+c_s^2}$.
Write $\rho = \rho_0+\rho_1$, $\mathbf u = \mathbf u_1$, $\mathbf B = \mathbf B_0 + \mathbf B_1$ and keep first order. Continuity and the adiabatic closure give $p_1 = c_s^2\rho_1$; the induction equation (2.1) at $\eta_{\rm m}=0$ gives $\partial_t\mathbf B_1 = \nabla\times(\mathbf u_1\times\mathbf B_0)$; and the momentum equation with (3.1) gives $$ \rho_0\,\partial_t\mathbf u_1 = -\nabla p_1 - \nabla\!\left(\frac{\mathbf B_0\cdot\mathbf B_1}{4\pi}\right) + \frac{(\mathbf B_0\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B_1}{4\pi}. $$ Substituting $\propto\exp[i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x-\omega t)]$ and eliminating $\rho_1$ and $\mathbf B_1$ leaves a $3\times3$ eigenvalue problem for $\mathbf u_1$. The component of $\mathbf u_1$ perpendicular to both $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf B_0$ decouples: it involves no compression, so no $p_1$, and only the tension term survives, giving $\omega^2 = (\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v_{\rm A})^2 = k^2v_{\rm A}^2\cos^2\theta$. That is the Alfvén wave, and the proof shows what it is: a transverse wave on a string under tension $B^2/4\pi$ with mass per unit length $\rho$, whose speed $\sqrt{\rm tension/\rho}$ is exactly $v_{\rm A}$ — which is the promised justification for calling $v_{\rm A}$ a speed. The remaining $2\times2$ block, in the plane of $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf B_0$, has determinant condition $$ v^4 - v^2\left(v_{\rm A}^2+c_s^2\right) + v_{\rm A}^2c_s^2\cos^2\theta = 0, \qquad v \equiv \omega/k, $$ a quadratic in $v^2$ whose two roots are (4.1). At $\cos\theta = 1$ it factorises as $(v^2-v_{\rm A}^2)(v^2-c_s^2)=0$; at $\cos\theta = 0$ the constant term vanishes, so one root is $0$ and the other $v_{\rm A}^2+c_s^2$. ∎

4.1 Module 7's one-degree bound, recomputed — a validation, not a check

Module 7 §9.4 states the debt in one line: "A purely hydrodynamic calculation that needs a one-degree statement about a field it never mentions is a calculation waiting for magnetohydrodynamics." That inequality is recomputed here from Proposition 6's dispersion relation rather than copied, so that the comparison below is able to fail.

A validation of the machinery, and not a check against nature. With $\rho_h = 5.8609\times10^{-16}$ and $\rho_l = 2.6211\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ read out of Module 7 §9.1's provenance note, and $\Delta U = 20\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ read from the sentence that calls it "the shear measured on the flank of a coronal mass ejection", (4.2) gives $\mathbf{0.06747}$ G against Module 7's printed $0.0675$, ratio $0.9995$, and $\mathbf{0.09541}$ G against its $0.0954$ on the one-sided hypothesis, ratio $1.0001$. This is labelled the way Module 5 labelled Kandori's contrast column: a check that two parts of this book agree, which is worth having and is not evidence about the Sun.
The angle is measured from perpendicular, and the first draft of this module's generator got it backwards. (4.2) bounds $B\cos\theta$, so at fixed $B$ it bounds $\cos\theta$, and the permitted departure from perpendicular is $\arcsin(\cos\theta)$ and not $\arccos(\cos\theta)$. Writing $\arccos$ printed $86.13^\circ$ where Module 7 prints $3.87^\circ$ — a ninety-degree error that no mechanical check in this project reads, and which was caught only because Module 7 had printed its table.

And here is the gap this module cannot fill. The book's shape is: derive the terrestrial result, change the regime, check against a measured astronomical number. Module 4 had the speed of sound in air, Module 6 the dry adiabat, Module 10 the Kolmogorov constant. Magnetohydrodynamics has terrestrial measurements — Alfvén waves were first seen in liquid mercury, and every tokamak is an MHD experiment — and none of them was read for this book. What occupies the terrestrial slot above is a reproduction of Module 7, which is a check against this book and not against a laboratory. The four checks of §§5–8 are all astronomical, and a reader should weigh them knowing that no bench-top number stands behind them.

5. The anchor: the Parker spiral

Module 9 built a wind that leaves the Sun radially and is still accelerating at 1 au. The Sun rotates. A field line is frozen to the gas by Proposition 4 and its foot is carried round by the rotation while its body streams outward — so the line is drawn into a spiral, and the shape of that spiral is fixed by two numbers this book already has: the solar rotation rate and the wind speed. Nothing is fitted.

Proposition 7 (the Parker spiral). For a steady, spherically symmetric, radial wind of speed $v(r)$ from a source surface at $r_0$, on a star rotating at angular rate $\Omega$, the frozen-in field has $$ B_r(r) = B_r(r_0)\left(\frac{r_0}{r}\right)^{2}, \qquad \frac{B_\phi}{B_r} = -\,\frac{\Omega\,(r-r_0)\sin\theta}{v}, \tag{5.1}$$ with $\theta$ the colatitude. The first follows from $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ alone and is exact; the second is the kinematics of a line whose foot rotates.
The radial part. $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ integrated over a sphere of radius $r$ gives $\oint B_r\,\mathrm{d}S = 4\pi r^2\langle B_r\rangle = $ constant, since no flux is created between two spheres. Hence $B_r r^2$ is constant. No dynamics entered, which is why this half is exact and is the half the control in Check 1 tests.

The azimuthal part. Work in the frame rotating with the star, in which the flow is steady. There the velocity is $\mathbf v' = v\hat{\mathbf r} - \Omega(r-r_0)\sin\theta\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$ — Parker's equation (24), with $r_0$ his $b$ — and in a steady state the field must be parallel to the flow, for otherwise the flow would sweep the field into a configuration that changes with time. Hence $\mathbf B \parallel \mathbf v'$, so $$ \frac{B_\phi}{B_r} = \frac{v'_\phi}{v'_r} = -\frac{\Omega(r-r_0)\sin\theta}{v}, $$ which is Parker's equation (26). ∎
Parker's $(r-r_0)$ is carried, and the module prints what dropping it would cost. His (26) reads $B_\phi = B(\theta,\phi_0)(\omega/v_m)(r-b)(b/r)^2\sin\theta$. The familiar $\Omega r\sin\theta/v$ is his large-$r$ limit, stated as a limit, and his (27) places the $45^\circ$ surface at $r\simeq(v_m/\omega)\sin\theta$ only after $b$ has been dropped. Printing $\Omega r\sin\theta/v$ while citing (26) is the one thing that is not defensible, so this module does neither. At 1 au with $r_0 = 2.5\,R_\odot$, carrying $r_0$ lowers $-B_\phi/B_r$ from $0.9840$ to $0.9726$, by $1.16$ per cent; dropping it raises the ratio by $1.18$ per cent. The two figures are one term seen from its two ends, and $r_0/1\,\mathrm{au} = 0.01163$ is why. The source surface is written $r_0$ throughout, because $b$ is Module 1's impact parameter and Module 10's forcing parameter.

The observable consequence is the total field's radial falloff. Writing $x \equiv \Omega(r-r_0)\sin\theta/v$, $$ |\mathbf B| = B_r\sqrt{1+x^2}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln|\mathbf B|}{\mathrm{d}\ln r} = -2 + \frac{x^2}{1+x^2}\left(\frac{r}{r-r_0} - \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln v}{\mathrm{d}\ln r}\right). \tag{5.2}$$ At small $r$ the field is nearly radial, $x\ll1$, and the index is $-2$. At large $r$ it is nearly azimuthal, $x\gg1$, and the index tends to $-1$. Between the two, over the range a spacecraft actually flew, the index is something in between — and that is a measurement.

6. The Alfvén surface, bracketed by two measurements

Close to the Sun the field is strong and the wind slow, so $v \lt v_{\rm A}$ and the field controls the flow: it can enforce corotation, and it carries away angular momentum. Far out the wind is fast and the field weak, so $v \gt v_{\rm A}$ and the flow drags the field, which is what §5 just measured. In between there is a surface where the two are equal, and it is the boundary between those two regimes.

Proposition 8 (the wind crosses its own radial Alfvén speed exactly once). For the wind of Proposition 7, mass conservation gives $\rho \propto r^{-2}v^{-1}$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ gives $B_r\propto r^{-2}$, so the radial Alfvén speed obeys $$ v_{\rm A,r} \;=\; \frac{B_r}{\sqrt{4\pi\rho}} \;\propto\; \frac{r^{-2}}{\sqrt{r^{-2}v^{-1}}} \;=\; r^{-1}v^{1/2}. \tag{6.1}$$ If $v$ is non-decreasing outward, $v/v_{\rm A,r}\propto r\,v^{1/2}$ is strictly increasing, so the equation $v = v_{\rm A,r}$ has exactly one root $r_{\rm A}$.
$\dot M = 4\pi r^2\rho v$ constant gives $\rho\propto r^{-2}v^{-1}$; substituting into $B_r/\sqrt{4\pi\rho}$ with $B_r\propto r^{-2}$ gives (6.1). Then $v/v_{\rm A,r}\propto v\cdot r\,v^{-1/2} = r\,v^{1/2}$, a product of two non-decreasing positive factors one of which is strictly increasing. A strictly increasing continuous function crosses the value $1$ at most once, and does so at least once given that it is $\ll1$ at the base and $\gg1$ at 1 au. ∎
The Alfvén speed in (6.1) takes $B_r$ and not $|\mathbf B|$, and the first draft of this module's generator used $|\mathbf B|$. Two reasons, either of which settles it. (i) §5 has just proved that $|\mathbf B|$ does not scale as $r^{-2}$; $B_r$ does, by $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$, and it is the $r^{-2}$ scaling the extrapolation inward uses. (ii) The measured quantity is the Weber–Davis Alfvén radius, which Verscharen, Bale & Velli define on the radial Alfvén speed, because it is the radial components that enter the flow deflection they fit. Using $|\mathbf B|$ inflated every radius below by $\sqrt{1+x_1^2}$, a factor near $1.40$, and changed the module's headline.

7. Magnetic support against collapse

Module 5 §11 is owed this: magnetic pressure and tension add support against collapse, and the statement that results is not an extra pressure term but a critical mass-to-flux ratio. Below it no field strength is enough; above it the cloud is held for as long as the flux stays with the gas.

Definition 3 (the mass-to-flux ratio). For a cloud of mass $M$ threaded by magnetic flux $\Phi$, $$ \lambda_\Phi \;\equiv\; \frac{M/\Phi}{(M/\Phi)_{\rm crit}}, \qquad \left(\frac{M}{\Phi}\right)_{\rm crit} = \frac{c_\Phi}{\sqrt G}. \tag{7.1}$$ $\lambda_\Phi \gt 1$ is supercritical: the field cannot hold the cloud. The symbol carries its subscript because bare $\lambda$ is Module 1's mean free path book-wide, and both appear in this module.
Proposition 9 (the critical ratio is a pure number). For a flux-frozen, self-gravitating cloud, the gravitational and magnetic energies both scale as $1/R$ at fixed $M$ and $\Phi$, so their ratio is independent of the radius and the criterion for collapse is a condition on $M/\Phi$ alone. The coefficient is $$ c_\Phi = \frac{c_1\sqrt5}{3\pi} = 0.125745 \quad\text{(uniform sphere)}, \qquad c_\Phi = \frac{1}{2\pi} = 0.159155 \quad\text{(flattened cloud)}. \tag{7.2}$$
The form is derived. For a uniform sphere of mass $M$ and radius $R$ the gravitational energy is $W = -3GM^2/5R$. Flux freezing fixes $\Phi = \pi R^2\langle B\rangle$, so $\langle B\rangle = \Phi/\pi R^2$ and the magnetic energy inside the cloud is $\mathcal M = (B^2/8\pi)(4\pi R^3/3) = \Phi^2/(6\pi^2R)$. Both go as $1/R$, so their sum is either positive or negative for all $R$ at once: compressing the cloud cannot change the sign. The virial condition $\mathcal M + W = 0$ therefore gives a critical ratio and not a critical radius, $$ \frac{\Phi^2}{6\pi^2R} = \frac{3GM^2}{5R} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{M}{\Phi} = \frac{1}{3\pi}\sqrt{\frac{5}{G}}, $$ which is (7.2) with $c_1 = 1$.

The coefficients are stated, not derived. Mouschovias & Spitzer (1976) show that the virial estimate is an approximation and fit a correction factor to exact equilibrium models: their equation (2) reads $M_{c\Phi} = (c_1/3\pi)(5/G)^{1/2}\Phi$ and their equation (4) gives $c_1 = 0.53$, whence $c_\Phi = 0.53\sqrt5/3\pi = 0.125745$. Nakano & Nakamura (1978) solve the flattened case and find the disc stable when $\sigma_0/B_0 \lt (4\pi^2G)^{-1/2}$ — a column density over a field — so $c_\Phi = 1/2\pi$ exactly. ∎
This module never prints $0.13$. The number that circulates for Mouschovias & Spitzer's coefficient is $0.13$; their two-page note prints $c_1 = 0.53$ and never prints $0.13$ at all. The correct value is $0.125745$, and $0.13$ is $1.0338$ times it. Two further traps from the same paper, because both are ways of being wrong by a lot: their $c_2 = 0.60$ applies to the maximum external pressure and not to the mass, and their footnote 1 warns that what Spitzer (1968) writes $(M_c/M)^{2/3}$ is here $(M_{c\Phi}/M)^2$, because $M_{c\Phi}$ is the critical mass at fixed flux and $M_c$ the critical mass at fixed initial density, with $M_{c\Phi}/M = (M_c/M)^{1/3}$. A module that quotes one symbol for the other is wrong by a cube.

8. Transport across a field, and the answer that is wrong by twenty-three decades

Module 2 §10 put two problems together and pointed at this module for both: the intracluster medium's Reynolds number of about $600$, which is not large enough for the turbulence that is observed, and a thermal conductivity so large that it would erase the temperature structure of cluster cores within a fraction of the cluster's age. It said the two "have the same suspected resolution — magnetic suppression of transport across field lines — and Module 12 treats both together." This section is that treatment, and it ends by refuting the classical form of the resolution.

Proposition 10 (perpendicular transport is suppressed by $(\omega_{\rm c}\tau)^{-2}$). In an unmagnetised gas a random walk of step $\lambda$ per collision time $\tau$ gives a diffusivity $\sim\lambda^2/\tau$. Across a field, a particle cannot travel $\lambda$ between collisions: it returns to within a gyroradius of where it started. The step is therefore $r_g$ rather than $\lambda$, and $$ \frac{D_\perp}{D_\parallel} \;\sim\; \frac{r_g^2}{\lambda^2} \;=\; \frac{1}{(\omega_{\rm c}\tau)^2}, \tag{8.1}$$ since $\lambda = v\tau$ and $r_g = v/\omega_{\rm c}$. The same factor governs the conductivity and the viscosity, with different numerical coefficients.
Between collisions the guiding centre is displaced along $\mathbf B$ by $\lambda$ and across it by at most $2r_g$; the perpendicular displacement per collision is therefore $O(r_g)$ and uncorrelated from one collision to the next. A random walk of $N$ steps of size $s$ in time $N\tau$ has diffusivity $s^2/\tau$, so $D_\perp/D_\parallel = r_g^2/\lambda^2$. With $\lambda = v\tau$ and $r_g = v/\omega_{\rm c}$ the speed cancels and the ratio is $(\omega_{\rm c}\tau)^{-2}$. ∎

Braginskii (1965) does the calculation properly and supplies the coefficients. For $Z = 1$ his equations (2.12) and (2.13) on p. 217 give the electron conduction, $$ \kappa_\parallel = 3.16\,\frac{n_{\rm e}T_{\rm e}\tau_{\rm e}}{m_{\rm e}}, \qquad \kappa_\perp = 4.66\,\frac{n_{\rm e}T_{\rm e}}{m_{\rm e}\omega_{\rm e}^2\tau_{\rm e}}, \qquad \frac{\kappa_\perp}{\kappa_\parallel} = \frac{4.66}{3.16}\frac{1}{(\omega_{\rm e}\tau_{\rm e})^2}, \tag{8.2}$$ and his equations (2.22) and (2.23) on p. 218 give the ion viscosity, which Module 10 §6.2 already uses, with $\eta_0^{\rm B} = 0.96\,n_iT_i\tau_i$ and $\eta_1^{\rm B} = (3/10)\,n_iT_i/(\omega_i^2\tau_i)$.

(2019) bound the measured suppression of the Coma cluster's effective viscosity at $\sim10$ to $\sim1000$. The classical factor is larger than the measured one by between $\mathbf{22.8}$ and $\mathbf{24.8}$ decades. Both decade figures are printed rather than rounded into a phrase, because a phrase cannot be checked and the phrase this one replaced read "twenty-odd".

The direction of the failure is the physical content. The measurement lies far nearer the unsuppressed Coulomb value than the classical perpendicular one. That is not a small correction to (8.1); it says the premise of (8.1) is wrong for a real cluster. The field in a cluster is tangled, so heat and momentum follow field lines that wander, and a particle that has travelled one mean free path along a line has also moved a long way across the mean field direction. The step is therefore neither $\lambda$ nor $r_g$, and it is set by the field-line random walk and by pressure-anisotropy microinstabilities.

What the effective transport is, this book does not compute. Module 10 printed Zhuravleva's bound and refused to state a value; this module does the same. Two caveats travel with that bound and both are the authors': the factor depends on the Prandtl number, which they leave free, and "it is not at present possible to prove that the observed fluctuations are associated with the turbulence in clusters as we do not have direct velocity measurements." §11 records that nobody in this book repays it, rather than pointing at a module that will not deliver.

9. The magnetorotational instability

Module 8 §10 names the magnetorotational instability in its list of what this module builds, so it is not optional. It is done locally: the shear is assumed, not derived from a disc model, because disc structure — the $\alpha$ prescription, the thin-disc solution — is Module 11.

The problem it solves is stated in one line. A Keplerian disc has angular velocity $\Omega\propto R^{-3/2}$, so its specific angular momentum $\ell = \Omega R^2 \propto R^{1/2}$ increases outward. By the Rayleigh criterion that is hydrodynamically stable: displace a ring outward and it has too little angular momentum to stay there, and it falls back. A purely hydrodynamic Keplerian disc does not transport angular momentum, and therefore does not accrete. Adding the weakest imaginable magnetic field destroys that stability.

Proposition 11 (the local MRI dispersion relation). Let $q \equiv -\mathrm{d}\ln\Omega/\mathrm{d}\ln R$, so $q = 3/2$ for a Keplerian disc, and let the epicyclic frequency be $\kappa_{\rm ep}^2 = 2(2-q)\Omega^2$. For an axisymmetric, incompressible perturbation with wavevector $\mathbf k\parallel\hat{\mathbf z}$ along a weak vertical field, writing $\sigma \equiv \gamma/\Omega$ and $x \equiv kv_{\rm A}/\Omega$, $$ \sigma^4 + \sigma^2\!\left(\frac{\kappa_{\rm ep}^2}{\Omega^2} + 2x^2\right) + x^2\!\left(x^2 - 4 + \frac{\kappa_{\rm ep}^2}{\Omega^2}\right) = 0. \tag{9.1}$$ There is a growing root whenever $x^2 \lt 2q$. At $q = 3/2$ this gives instability for $kv_{\rm A} \lt \sqrt3\,\Omega$, a maximum growth rate $$ \gamma_{\max} = \frac{q}{2}\,\Omega = 0.75\,\Omega \quad\text{at}\quad kv_{\rm A} = \frac{\sqrt{15}}{4}\,\Omega = 0.96825\,\Omega, \tag{9.2}$$ and marginal stability at $kv_{\rm A} = \sqrt3\,\Omega = 1.73205\,\Omega$.
Work in a local frame corotating at $R_0$ with coordinates $(x_1,x_2,x_3)$ along $(\hat{\mathbf R},\hat{\boldsymbol\phi},\hat{\mathbf z})$, and linearise the ideal MHD equations for displacements $\boldsymbol\xi\propto\exp(\gamma t + ikx_3)$. The tidal and Coriolis terms give the standard epicyclic pair, and the field contributes tension $-(kv_{\rm A})^2\boldsymbol\xi$ to each in-plane component — the field lines act as springs joining the fluid element to its unperturbed radius. The two in-plane equations are $$ \gamma^2\xi_1 - 2\Omega\gamma\xi_2 = -(k^2v_{\rm A}^2 - 2q\Omega^2)\,\xi_1, \qquad \gamma^2\xi_2 + \frac{\kappa_{\rm ep}^2}{2\Omega}\gamma\xi_1 = -k^2v_{\rm A}^2\,\xi_2, $$ whose determinant condition is (9.1) after dividing by $\Omega^4$.

The instability condition. Equation (9.1) is a quadratic in $\sigma^2$ with positive coefficient on $\sigma^2$, so it has a positive root for $\sigma^2$ if and only if its constant term is negative, that is if and only if $x^2 - 4 + \kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2 \lt 0$. Substituting $\kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2 = 2(2-q)$ gives $x^2 \lt 2q$, so $x \lt \sqrt3$ at $q = 3/2$.

The maximum. Differentiate (9.1) implicitly with respect to $x^2$ and set $\mathrm{d}\sigma^2/\mathrm{d}x^2 = 0$: the surviving condition is $2\sigma^2 + 2x^2 - 4 + \kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2 = 0$. With $q = 3/2$, $\kappa_{\rm ep}^2/\Omega^2 = 1$, this is $\sigma^2 = (3 - 2x^2)/2$. Substituting into (9.1) and solving gives $x^2 = 15/16$ and $\sigma^2 = 9/16$, hence $\sigma = 3/4$ and $x = \sqrt{15}/4$. ∎
Two things about this result that the algebra hides.
(i) There is no threshold field. The unstable band is $kv_{\rm A} \lt \sqrt3\,\Omega$, so a weaker field simply moves the band to larger $k$ — shorter wavelengths — without closing it. The instability disappears only when the longest unstable wavelength no longer fits inside the disc. That is why the condition that matters in practice is $\lambda_{\rm marg} \lt H$ and not a condition on $B$ alone.
(ii) The growth rate does not depend on the field at all. $\gamma_{\max} = q\Omega/2$ contains no $B$, no $n$ and no $T$. A weaker field changes which wavelength grows fastest, not how fast it grows. In e-folds per orbit that is $2\pi(q/2) = 3\pi/2 = 4.712$ for every Keplerian disc — an identity, not a property of any particular one.

10. Anisotropy, critical balance, and Module 10's debt

Module 10 §7 confirmed Kolmogorov's $-5/3$ most cleanly on the magnetic spectrum of the solar wind, and said plainly what was uncomfortable about that: the confirmed exponent belongs to a magnetised plasma, which is not the fluid its Proposition 2 describes, and the same exponent follows from a quite different argument about magnetised turbulence. Its own sentence is the one to keep: "agreement of an exponent does not select between two derivations that give the same exponent."

Proposition 12 (critical balance gives the same exponent, and an anisotropy K41 does not have). Suppose an Alfvénic cascade keeps the linear Alfvén crossing time and the nonlinear turnover time equal at every scale: $$ k_\parallel v_{\rm A} \;\sim\; k_\perp\,\delta v_\perp. \tag{10.1}$$ If the perpendicular cascade is Kolmogorov, $\delta v_\perp \sim (\varepsilon/k_\perp)^{1/3}$, then $$ k_\parallel \;\sim\; k_\perp^{2/3}L^{-1/3}, \qquad E(k_\perp) \;\propto\; k_\perp^{-5/3}. \tag{10.2}$$
Substituting $\delta v_\perp \sim \varepsilon^{1/3}k_\perp^{-1/3}$ into (10.1) gives $k_\parallel \sim k_\perp^{2/3}\varepsilon^{1/3}/v_{\rm A}$; writing $\varepsilon \sim v_{\rm A}^3/L$ at the outer scale, where the balance is imposed, gives $\varepsilon^{1/3}/v_{\rm A} = L^{-1/3}$ and hence the first of (10.2). For the second, the perpendicular energy spectrum follows from $\delta v_\perp^2 \sim k_\perp E(k_\perp)$ with $\delta v_\perp \propto k_\perp^{-1/3}$, so $E \propto k_\perp^{-5/3}$. ∎
The exponent is not the discriminant. The anisotropy is, and this book does not measure it. K41 and critical balance predict the same $-5/3$ from different premises, so a measured $-5/3$ selects neither. What distinguishes them is the first half of (10.2): K41 predicts isotropic eddies, and critical balance predicts eddies that grow more elongated along the field at every smaller scale, with $k_\parallel \propto k_\perp^{2/3}$. No anisotropy measurement was read for this book, and this module therefore declares the discriminating measurement outside it. Module 10's four indices — $1.66$, $1.72$, $1.66$, $1.58$, mean $1.6550$ against $5/3$, ratio $0.9930$ — are reprinted here so that the two modules quote one set, and no new check is built on them.
Goldreich & Sridhar's own restriction travels in the same sentence as their result, every time this module states it. The third and fourth sentences of their abstract read: "Here, as in Paper I, we restrict attention to the symmetric case where the oppositely directed waves carry equal energy fluxes. This precludes application to the solar wind in which the outward flux significantly exceeds the ingoing one." Proposition 12 above is therefore a statement about a symmetric Alfvénic cascade, and it may not be presented as a prediction for the solar-wind indices reprinted beside it — which are precisely the measurement its authors excluded.

This matters for a shipped page. Module 10 §7 and its captures-and-misses table both attribute the $5/3$ of the solar wind's magnetic spectrum to Goldreich & Sridhar as an alternative prediction. No number depends on it — Module 10 §13 already records that they are "not used numerically anywhere" — so it is a prose attribution and not a computed claim, and the correction belongs to Module 10's author and not to this module. Module 12 carries the caveat whether or not that edit is made.

Note also that the bare exponent $2/3$ is not dimensional. Their relation is $k_\parallel \approx k_\perp^{2/3}L^{-1/3}$, and the outer scale $L$ is what makes the two sides agree in units. A module that printed $k_\parallel \sim k_\perp^{2/3}$ alone would be printing a relation between quantities of different dimensions.

11.1 The point Module 1 asked this module to return to

Module 1 §9 observed that the magnetic rescue of the fluid description is partial, and partial in a measurable way. A field constrains motion across itself and does nothing to motion along itself, so a magnetised collisionless plasma is not an ordinary fluid with a scalar pressure: it has $p_\parallel$ along the field and $p_\perp$ across it, and the two relax toward each other only through collisions the plasma is not having. The resulting description is the Chew–Goldberger–Low or double-adiabatic theory. Pressure anisotropy is routinely measured in the solar wind, and it drives the firehose and mirror instabilities, which scatter particles and so act as the plasma's substitute for collisions.

That is the return, and it is all of it. Single-fluid MHD as built in §2 and §3 assumes a scalar pressure and therefore cannot contain any of it. No instability threshold is printed here, because no CGL source was read for this book, and a threshold quoted from memory is the failure this project keeps finding in its own drafts. What can be said without a source is the structural point: the same anisotropy that Module 1 named is the leading candidate for the microinstabilities that §8 needed and could not compute, so two of this book's unpaid debts are probably one debt.

12. Problems

Every modelling input a solution uses is stated in its own problem. Where an input is a census value with no source, or an assumption rather than a measurement, the statement says so — because a problem that hides its assumptions teaches the wrong habit. C1. An active-region corona has $n = 10^{9}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $T = 10^{6}$ K and $B = 10$ G — the field taken from Module 7 §9.5, which states that the number is illustrative and not a measurement. Take $\mu = 0.61$ and $\gamma = 5/3$. Find $p_{\rm gas}$, $p_{\rm mag}$, $\beta$, $v_{\rm A}$ and $c_s$, and then obtain $v_{\rm A}/c_s$ twice: once from the two speeds and once from (1.2). Why must the two agree exactly rather than approximately?
Solution

$\rho = n\mu m_{\rm u} = 10^{9}\times0.61\times1.66054\times10^{-24} = 1.0129\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.

$p_{\rm gas} = nk_{\rm B}T = 10^{9}\times1.380649\times10^{-16}\times10^{6} = 1.3806\times10^{-1}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$, and $p_{\rm mag} = B^2/8\pi = 100/(8\pi) = 3.9789\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$. Hence $\beta = 0.1381/3.979 = \mathbf{0.0347}$: the field carries $28.8$ times the gas pressure.

$v_{\rm A} = B/\sqrt{4\pi\rho} = 10/\sqrt{4\pi\times1.0129\times10^{-15}} = 8.864\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}} = \mathbf{886.4\ km\,s^{-1}}$. $c_s = \sqrt{\gamma k_{\rm B}T/\mu m_{\rm u}} = \sqrt{(5/3)(1.380649\times10^{-16})(10^{6})/(1.0129\times10^{-24})} = 1.507\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}} = \mathbf{150.7\ km\,s^{-1}}$.

The ratio of the two speeds is $886.4/150.7 = \mathbf{5.8807}$. From (1.2), $\sqrt{2/(\gamma\beta)} = \sqrt{2/((5/3)(0.034699))} = \mathbf{5.8807}$. The two agree to six figures.

They must agree exactly because (1.2) is an identity, not an approximation. The proof of Proposition 1 cancels $\rho$ between the two speeds, so the same $\mu$ enters both and leaves. If the two columns ever disagreed it would not mean the physics had changed; it would mean a $\mu$ had been altered in one place and not the other. That is why the table in §1 prints both columns and their ratio.

C2. A coronal loop $100$ Mm long ($L = 10^{10}$ cm) has $n = 10^{9}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $T = 10^{6}$ K and a flow along it of $U = 10\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ — a loop flow speed, not a wind speed. Using Braginskii's $\sigma_\parallel = 1.96\,n e^2\tau_{\rm e}/m_{\rm e}$ with his $\tau_{\rm e}$ of equation (2.5e) and $\ln\Lambda = 18.66$, find $\eta_{\rm m}$, $\mathrm{R_m}$, the ohmic decay time $L^2/\eta_{\rm m}$, and the dynamical time $L/U$. Then compute the ratio of the last two and say why you did not need to.
Solution

$\eta_{\rm m} = c^2/4\pi\sigma_\parallel = \mathbf{9.768\times10^{3}\ cm^2\,s^{-1}}$. For comparison, that is about sixty-five thousand times the kinematic viscosity of air; a coronal plasma is a poor conductor by laboratory standards and an extraordinary one by astrophysical ones, and the next line is why.

$\mathrm{R_m} = UL/\eta_{\rm m} = (10^{6})(10^{10})/9.768\times10^{3} = \mathbf{1.024\times10^{12}}$.

$L^2/\eta_{\rm m} = 10^{20}/9.768\times10^{3} = 1.024\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{s} = \mathbf{3.244\times10^{8}\ yr}$, and $L/U = 10^{10}/10^{6} = 10^{4}\ \mathrm{s} = \mathbf{3.169\times10^{-4}\ yr}$.

Their ratio is $1.024\times10^{12}$ — the magnetic Reynolds number again, to six figures. You did not need to compute it, because (2.4) says $(L^2/\eta_{\rm m})/(L/U) = UL/\eta_{\rm m}$ identically. The point of doing it anyway is that the identity turns the ohmic-time column into a check on the $\mathrm{R_m}$ column beside it: if one is ever edited alone, the ratio stops closing.

And the comparison is with the loop's own dynamical time, deliberately. Comparing $3.244\times10^{8}$ yr with the age of the universe would be meaningless — the loop does not live that long. Comparing it with the $10^{4}$ s the loop takes to do anything at all is the comparison that says the field is frozen in.

C3. Take $\Omega$ from the sidereal rotation period $25.38$ d — the NSSDC value, adopted at $16^\circ$ latitude — a source surface $r_0 = 2.5\,R_\odot$, $\theta = 90^\circ$, and a wind speed held constant at $v = 435.6\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, which the Helios fits do not support and which is assumed here only to keep the arithmetic in one line. Using (5.1), find $-B_\phi/B_r$ and the spiral angle at $1$ au and at $5$ au, and the local index (5.2) at each. Then say what dropping $r_0$ would do at $1$ au.
Solution

$\Omega = 2\pi/(25.38\times86400) = \mathbf{2.86533\times10^{-6}\ rad\,s^{-1}}$ and $r_0 = 2.5\times6.957\times10^{10} = 1.739\times10^{11}$ cm $= 0.011626$ au.

At 1 au: $-B_\phi/B_r = \Omega(r-r_0)/v = (2.86533\times10^{-6})(1.4960\times10^{13} - 1.739\times10^{11})/(4.356\times10^{7}) = \mathbf{0.9726}$. The spiral angle is $\arctan(0.9726) = \mathbf{44.20^\circ}$, and (5.2) with $\mathrm{d}\ln v/\mathrm{d}\ln r = 0$ gives $-2 + (0.9726^2/1.9459)(1.0118) = \mathbf{-1.5082}$.

At 5 au: $-B_\phi/B_r = \mathbf{4.9088}$, angle $\mathbf{78.49^\circ}$, index $\mathbf{-1.0376}$.

The spiral tightens outward, and the index climbs from $-1.51$ toward the asymptotic $-1$: by 5 au the field is almost entirely azimuthal, and the total field is falling off nearly as $1/r$ rather than as $1/r^2$.

Dropping $r_0$ replaces $(r-r_0)$ by $r$ and raises $-B_\phi/B_r$ at 1 au from $0.9726$ to $0.9840$, by $\mathbf{1.18}$ per cent; equivalently, carrying $r_0$ lowers it by $1.16$ per cent. The two percentages are the same term seen from its two ends, and $r_0/1\ \mathrm{au} = 0.011626$ is why they are nearly equal. At 5 au the effect is five times smaller still, which is exactly why Parker states $\Omega r\sin\theta/v$ as a large-$r$ limit.

D1. A spherical cloud $10$ pc across has $n_{\rm tot} = 100\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ per particle at $\mu = 2.33$, so that $n({\rm H_2}) = 83.3\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$; take $R = 5$ pc and $B = 10\ \mu$G, an order-of-magnitude census value for which this book has no source at this density. Find $M$, and then $M_\Phi$ and $\lambda_\Phi$ for both geometries of (7.2). Which of the two answers should be quoted, and why is that the wrong question?
Solution

$\rho = n\mu m_{\rm u} = 100\times2.33\times1.66054\times10^{-24} = 3.8691\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, and with $R = 5\ \mathrm{pc} = 1.5428\times10^{19}$ cm, $M = \tfrac43\pi R^3\rho = 5.952\times10^{36}\ \mathrm{g} = \mathbf{2.993\times10^{3}\,M_\odot}$.

The flux is $\Phi = \pi R^2B = \pi(1.5428\times10^{19})^2(10^{-5}) = 7.478\times10^{33}$ Mx, and $M_\Phi = (c_\Phi/\sqrt G)\Phi$.

Sphere, $c_\Phi = 0.53\sqrt5/3\pi = 0.125745$: $(M/\Phi)_{\rm crit} = \mathbf{4.8673\times10^{2}}$ g Mx⁻¹, $M_\Phi = \mathbf{1.831\times10^{3}\,M_\odot}$, $\lambda_\Phi = 2993/1831 = \mathbf{1.635}$.

Sheet, $c_\Phi = 1/2\pi = 0.159155$: $(M/\Phi)_{\rm crit} = \mathbf{6.1605\times10^{2}}$ g Mx⁻¹, $M_\Phi = \mathbf{2.317\times10^{3}\,M_\odot}$, $\lambda_\Phi = \mathbf{1.292}$.

Neither should be quoted alone, and that is the answer. Both are supercritical, so the conclusion — the field cannot hold this cloud — is the same either way and is robust. But the two numbers differ by the coefficient ratio $1.2657$, and Troland & Crutcher's entire measured range for real cores is $1.4$ to $2.6$. The geometric factor alone spans $\mathbf{0.381}$ of the log width of that range. A $\lambda_\Phi$ quoted without its assumed geometry is not a measurement, and quoting $1.635$ as though it were one would be claiming a precision the modelling does not have.

Note also what has been assumed about the flux: $\Phi = \pi R^2B$ takes a uniform field threading the cloud's mid-plane. A tangled field of the same $|B|$ threads less net flux and so makes the cloud more supercritical still.

D2. At $1$ au take $n = 7.57\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $|\mathbf B| = 6.05$ nT and $v = 435.6\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ — Venzmer & Bothmer's mean fits — with protons only, $-B_\phi/B_r = 0.9726$ from Problem C3, $v$ held constant, $B_r\propto r^{-2}$ and $\rho\propto r^{-2}v^{-1}$. Solution

$\sqrt{1+x_1^2} = \sqrt{1+0.9726^2} = \mathbf{1.3950}$, so $B_r = 6.05/1.3950 = \mathbf{4.3370}$ nT $= 4.337\times10^{-5}$ G.

$\rho = n m_{\rm p} = 7.57\times1.6726\times10^{-24} = 1.2662\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, so $v_{\rm A,r} = B_r/\sqrt{4\pi\rho} = \mathbf{34.38\ km\,s^{-1}}$ and $v/v_{\rm A,r} = 435.6/34.38 = \mathbf{12.669}$.

That answer is wrong twice over. First, the extrapolation inward uses $B\propto r^{-2}$, and §5 proved that $|\mathbf B|$ does not scale that way — $B_r$ does, by $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$. Either reason alone settles it.

D3. An intracluster medium has $n = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $T = 10^{8}$ K and $B = 1\ \mu$G. Using Braginskii's $\tau_{\rm e}$ with its $4\sqrt{2\pi}$, the derived electron-branch $\ln\Lambda = 37.08$ (Module 1's census value is $37.8$), and his $\kappa_\parallel = 3.16$, $\kappa_\perp = 4.66$ for $Z = 1$, find $\omega_{\rm e}\tau_{\rm e}$ and $\kappa_\perp/\kappa_\parallel$. Before quoting the suppression as the answer, check it against Zhuravleva et al.'s measured $10$–$1000$.
Solution

$\tau_{\rm e} = \mathbf{7.4227\times10^{12}}$ s and $\omega_{\rm e} = eB/m_{\rm e}c = \mathbf{17.588\ rad\,s^{-1}}$, so $\omega_{\rm e}\tau_{\rm e} = \mathbf{1.3055\times10^{14}}$.

By (8.2), $\kappa_\perp/\kappa_\parallel = (4.66/3.16)(\omega_{\rm e}\tau_{\rm e})^{-2} = 1.4747/(1.7043\times10^{28}) = \mathbf{8.6523\times10^{-29}}$.

That answer must be checked before it is quoted, and it fails the check. A perpendicular conductivity $8.65\times10^{-29}$ of the parallel value would leave a cluster core with essentially no conduction across the field at all — and that is not what is observed either. The classical coefficient does not describe a real cluster.

And the comparison must be made on the right quantity. Zhuravleva et al. measure a viscosity, so the number to compare is $\nu_\perp/\nu_\parallel$. The ion magnetisation is $\omega_i\tau_i = 4.3087\times10^{12}$, giving $\nu_\perp/\nu_\parallel = (0.3/0.96)(\omega_i\tau_i)^{-2} = \mathbf{1.6833\times10^{-26}}$, a classical suppression factor of $5.941\times10^{25}$. Against their measured $10$ to $1000$, the classical value is too small by between $\mathbf{22.8}$ and $\mathbf{24.8}$ decades. Comparing $\kappa_\perp/\kappa_\parallel$ with a viscosity bound would be two different quantities in one sentence, and this problem exists to stop that.

Why the two $\ln\Lambda$ values. $37.08$ is the electron-branch logarithm derived from this $n$ and $T$; $37.8$ is Module 1's census value, reused by Module 10. The $1.9$ per cent between them is one of the two factors in (8.3), and it is not a correction to either module.

K1. A protoplanetary disc at $R = 1$ au around $1\,M_\odot$ has $B = 1$ G, $n = 10^{14}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $T = 300$ K and $\mu = 2.33$. Take $q = -\mathrm{d}\ln\Omega/\mathrm{d}\ln R = 3/2$, assumed and not derived. Find $\Omega_{\rm K}$, $\gamma_{\max}$, the number of e-folds per orbit, $v_{\rm A}$, $c_s$, $H = c_s/\Omega$, and the marginal wavelength $\lambda_{\rm marg} = 2\pi v_{\rm A}/(\sqrt3\,\Omega)$. Is the disc unstable? Then explain why your e-folds-per-orbit answer contains no $B$, no $n$ and no $T$.
Solution

$\Omega_{\rm K} = \sqrt{GM/R^3} = \mathbf{1.9910\times10^{-7}\ rad\,s^{-1}}$, a period of $\mathbf{1.00}$ yr, as it must be at 1 au around a solar mass.

$\gamma_{\max} = (q/2)\Omega = 0.75\times1.9910\times10^{-7} = \mathbf{1.4932\times10^{-7}\ s^{-1}}$, so the e-folding time is $6.70\times10^{6}$ s $= 77.5$ days.

E-folds per orbit $= 2\pi\gamma_{\max}/\Omega = 2\pi\times0.75 = \mathbf{4.712}$.

$\rho = 10^{14}\times2.33\times1.66054\times10^{-24} = 3.869\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, so $v_{\rm A} = \mathbf{0.1434\ km\,s^{-1}}$ and $c_s = \mathbf{1.3357\ km\,s^{-1}}$; $\beta = 104.1$. Then $H = c_s/\Omega = 6.709\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{cm} = \mathbf{0.04485}$ au, and $\lambda_{\rm marg} = 2\pi v_{\rm A}/(\sqrt3\Omega) = 2.613\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{cm} = \mathbf{0.01747}$ au $= \mathbf{0.3895\,H}$.

Yes, unstable. $\lambda_{\rm marg} \lt H$: the longest unstable wavelength fits inside the disc with room to spare, so the MRI operates.

Why $4.712$ contains none of the inputs. (9.2) gives $\gamma_{\max} = q\Omega/2$ with no dependence on $v_{\rm A}$ whatever, so e-folds per orbit is $2\pi(q/2) = 3\pi/2$ for every Keplerian disc. It is an identity about the shear, not a property of this disc. What the field does control is which wavelength grows fastest, and therefore whether any unstable wavelength fits — which is the calculation of $\lambda_{\rm marg}/H$ above, and the only place $B$, $n$ and $T$ enter the verdict.

What this does not establish. The MRI needs the gas coupled to the field, and a $300$ K disc at $n = 10^{14}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ is very nearly neutral. The verdict above is a statement about an ideal MHD disc; whether this disc is ideal enough is a question about its ionisation fraction that this module does not compute.

K2. Module 7 §9.1 gives $\rho_h = 5.8609\times10^{-16}$ and $\rho_l = 2.6211\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ for the two sides of a coronal-mass-ejection flank, and calls $\Delta U = 20\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ the measured shear — an upper limit. Using (4.2), find the bound on $B\cos\theta$ for equal fields and for a one-sided field, and then the permitted angle at $B = 1$ G and $B = 10$ G. The angle is measured from perpendicular to $\mathbf B$; which inverse trigonometric function do you need, and what does the other one give?
Solution

The prefactor is $\sqrt{2\pi\rho_h\rho_l/(\rho_h+\rho_l)} = \sqrt{2\pi(5.8609)(2.6211)\times10^{-32}/(8.4820\times10^{-16})} = 3.3734\times10^{-8}$ in CGS. With $\Delta U = 2\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$,

$B\cos\theta \lt \mathbf{0.06747}$ G for equal fields, and $\sqrt2$ times that, $\mathbf{0.09541}$ G, for a one-sided field. Module 7 prints $0.0675$ and $0.0954$; the ratios are $0.9995$ and $1.0001$.

At $B = 1$ G, $\cos\theta \lt 0.06747$, so $\theta$ must lie within $\arcsin(0.06747) = \mathbf{3.87^\circ}$ of perpendicular. At $B = 10$ G, $\cos\theta \lt 0.006747$ and the angle is $\mathbf{0.39^\circ}$. Both match Module 7 §9.5's table exactly.

You need $\arcsin$. The bound is on $\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is measured from the field; the departure from perpendicular is $90^\circ - \theta$, whose sine is $\cos\theta$. Using $\arccos$ instead gives $\arccos(0.06747) = \mathbf{86.13^\circ}$ — the complement, and a ninety-degree error. It is worth noticing that $86.13^\circ$ looks perfectly plausible on its own: it is a legal angle, it varies sensibly with $B$, and nothing about it announces that it is wrong. The only thing that catches it is a second source printing the same table, which is why Module 7 printing its angle column was worth more than it looked.

K3. For the intracluster medium of Problem D3, compute the magnetisation two ways: as $\lambda/r_g$, with $\lambda$ Module 1's Coulomb mean free path at $\ln\Lambda = 37.8$ and $r_g = v_\perp/\omega_{\rm c}$ with $v_\perp = \sqrt{2k_{\rm B}T/m_{\rm p}}$; and as $\omega_i\tau_i$ with Braginskii's $\tau_i$ at the derived $\ln\Lambda = 37.081$. Account for the ratio exactly, and say which of the two belongs in $(\omega_{\rm c}\tau)^{-2}$.
Solution

$\lambda = \mathbf{22.5}$ kpc $= 6.944\times10^{22}$ cm. $v_\perp = \sqrt{2k_{\rm B}T/m_{\rm p}} = \mathbf{1285\ km\,s^{-1}}$ and $\omega_{\rm c} = eB/m_{\rm p}c = 9.579\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, so $r_g = v_\perp/\omega_{\rm c} = 1.3414\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm} = \mathbf{1.3414\times10^{5}}$ km. Hence $\lambda/r_g = \mathbf{5.1767\times10^{12}}$.

Braginskii's $\tau_i$ at $\ln\Lambda = 37.081$ is $4.4981\times10^{14}$ s, so $\omega_i\tau_i = \mathbf{4.3087\times10^{12}}$.

The ratio is $5.1767/4.3087 = \mathbf{1.2015}$, and it is two factors and nothing else:

$\sqrt{3/2} = \mathbf{1.224745}$, times $\ln\Lambda_{\rm e}/\ln\Lambda = 37.081/37.8 = \mathbf{0.980985}$, giving $1.224745\times0.980985 = \mathbf{1.2015}$.

The $\sqrt{3/2}$ is a component count and is exact. A Spitzer mean free path is $v_{\rm rms}\tau$ with the three-dimensional rms speed $\sqrt{3k_{\rm B}T/m}$; the first calculation divided it by $\sqrt{2k_{\rm B}T/m}$, the rms of the two components perpendicular to $\mathbf B$, because that is the speed a gyroradius takes. Dividing by a speed $\sqrt{2/3}$ times too small makes the answer $\sqrt{3/2}$ times too large. The second factor is not a convention at all but two different evaluations of the Coulomb logarithm, differing by $1.9$ per cent.

Braginskii's belongs in $(\omega_{\rm c}\tau)^{-2}$, because his transport coefficients $1.96$, $3.16$, $4.66$, $3.9$ and $2$ are defined on his $\tau$. Using the other one there would suppress the transport by a further factor $1.2015^2 = 1.44$ for no physical reason. The first number is still worth having, but as a count: a proton turns $10^{12.71}$ gyro-orbits between collisions.
